His thinking in five minutes

Parag Parikh started as a sub-broker on Dalal Street in 1979; later his wife and mother sold some of their jewellery to help him grow. He became India's pioneer of behavioural finance: the study of why investors make the same mistakes again and again. In 2013 he launched a mutual fund with just one scheme, put his own money in it, and later closed his broking business to avoid conflicts. He died in 2015 in Omaha, after attending Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting.

  • 1979: began as a sub-broker; his wife and mother later sold some of their jewellery to help fund him
  • 1 scheme: all his mutual fund offered at launch in 2013; he called fund houses' many schemes a fad
  • 2 books: Stocks to Riches (2005) and Value Investing and Behavioural Finance (2009)
  • 1999: kept telling clients to sell technology shares at the height of the boom, and lost business for it

His thinking, one line each

  • Study the mind. Investor behaviour matters as much as the balance sheet.
  • Leave the herd. Greed and fear move prices; profit from them, don't join them.
  • The law of the farm. No shortcuts: plant, wait for years, then harvest.
  • A good business. Honest management, a simple business, a moat and pricing power.
  • Skin in the game. Invest your own money alongside your clients.
  • One fund, not a grocery shop. One clear way of investing, not a new scheme for every fashion.

The big ideas

  1. From toothpaste tubes to shares. As a young man he wanted to make toothpaste tubes.
  2. Study the mind, not just the accounts. He believed investor behaviour moves markets as much as company results.
  3. Leave the herd. Greed and fear make Indian markets swing.
  4. Mistakes with Indian faces. His books name the traps: loss aversion, sunk costs, the endowment effect, mental accounting, decision paralysis.
  5. The law of the farm. Value investing is boring.
  6. What a good business looks like. He looked for four things: honest management that treats minority shareholders well, a simple business, a strong moat and pricing power.
  7. Skin in the game. He insisted that those who manage money should invest alongside their clients, and he removed every conflict he could, even at a cost.
  8. One fund, not a grocery shop. While fund houses launched scheme after scheme, he launched one.
  9. Standing up to the system. He was outspoken about the failings of India's finance industry, and argued with the regulator when he thought a rule would hurt competition.
  10. A firm built to outlast him. He died suddenly in 2015, in Omaha, after Warren Buffett's annual meeting.

Spot the bias

Which bias is this? Tap your answer

  1. A WhatsApp group says a small company will double. Everyone you know is buying, so you buy too. Herd behaviour. Buying because others are buying. His books warn that the crowd is usually late.
  2. You bought at ₹500. It is ₹300 and the business has got worse, but you won't sell until it's back to ₹500. Loss aversion. Refusing to take a loss. The market doesn't know or care what you paid.
  3. Your Diwali bonus feels like free money, so you gamble it on options instead of investing it. Mental accounting. A rupee is a rupee, wherever it came from. Treat a bonus like any other savings.
  4. You keep shares your father left you, though you would never buy them at today's price. Endowment effect. Valuing something more because you own it. Ask: would I buy this today?
  5. You paid ₹50,000 for a trading course, so you keep trading to make it worthwhile, though you keep losing. Sunk cost fallacy. Money already spent is gone. Only future costs and benefits should decide.
  6. There are so many mutual fund schemes that you can't choose, so your savings sit idle for years. Decision paralysis. Too many choices freeze you. One reason he launched only one fund.

His four marks of a good business

  1. Credible management that treats minority shareholders fairly.
  2. A simple business you can understand.
  3. A strong moat that keeps competitors out.
  4. Pricing power, so profits can keep up with costs.

Words to know

Behavioural finance
The study of how emotions and mental shortcuts shape investors' decisions.
Sub-broker
An agent who dealt through a registered stockbroker, often a first step into broking.
AMC
Asset management company: the firm that runs a mutual fund.
PMS
Portfolio management service: a manager running a separate portfolio for each wealthy client.
Herd behaviour
Doing what everyone else does because they are doing it.
Loss aversion
Feeling a loss about twice as strongly as an equal gain.
Mental accounting
Treating money differently depending on where it came from, such as a bonus.
Endowment effect
Valuing something more just because you own it.
SEBI
The Securities and Exchange Board of India, the market regulator.
