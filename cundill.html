His thinking in five minutes

Peter Cundill was a Canadian accountant who read Ben Graham's Security Analysis on a flight home in December 1973 and changed his life. He took over a small fund that had lost two-thirds of its value and turned it into the Cundill Value Fund. He hunted the world for shares selling below their liquidation value, from Japan to Europe, and compounded about 22% a year for his first fifteen years without a losing year. They called him the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.

  • 22%: a year: the Cundill Value Fund's first fifteen years, with no losing year
  • 30%+: a year: the fund's compound return in its first decade, to 1985
  • 37%: of the fund in Japan in 2004, with a third in cash, when bargains were scarce elsewhere
  • $500: his first investment, in a mining tip, lost within 48 hours

His thinking, one line each

  • Below liquidation value. Buy a dollar of assets for 40 or 50 cents.
  • Homework. Read the accounts; find what the balance sheet hides.
  • Go anywhere. Look hardest at the year's worst-performing market.
  • Keep cash. Hold cash when nothing qualifies; wait for crises.
  • Patience. Patience, patience and more patience.
  • Humility. Careful analysis still fails sometimes.

The big ideas

  1. A $500 lesson. His first investment, as a summer office boy, was a mining tip.
  2. The eureka moment. On a flight home in December 1973 he read Graham's Security Analysis.
  3. A dollar for forty cents. He bought shares priced below the company's liquidation value: what it would be worth if it closed and sold everything.
  4. His six tests. He wrote down his criteria: cheap against value and book, profitable, paying dividends, well off its high, and on a low P/E.
  5. Hidden assets. He read accounts for what they hid: buildings at old costs, valuable leases, power plants.
  6. Buy more as it falls. His best investment, Cleveland-Cliffs, fell by more than half after he bought.
  7. Indiana Jones. He searched the world for bargains, often starting with the country whose market had done worst that year.
  8. Cash is a position. When few shares met his tests he simply held cash, sometimes up to 40%, and waited.
  9. The one that went wrong. His worst investment, Cable and Wireless, met every Graham test and still failed.
  10. Patience, history and giving back. He ended advising patience, picking good managers, and studying history, and left a large prize for history writing.

The Cundill screen

Would it pass the Cundill screen?

  1. The price is below your estimate of intrinsic value
  2. The price is below book value, ideally below working capital less long-term debt
  3. The company is profitable, ideally with five years of growing earnings
  4. It pays a dividend, ideally a rising one
  5. The price is under half its former high, ideally near its low
  6. The P/E is under ten

His written criteria as reported by Morningstar Canada, in our words. A study aid, not a recommendation.

Three trades, three lessons

  1. J. Walter Thompson: the accounts left out buildings and a lease; homework found them.
  2. Cleveland-Cliffs: he kept buying as it halved, and earned over 30% a year.
  3. Cable and Wireless: every test passed, and it still failed.

Words to know

Liquidation value
What a company would fetch if it stopped trading and sold everything, after paying its debts.
Net-net
A share priced below current assets minus all liabilities: Graham's deepest bargain.
Working capital
Current assets, such as cash, stock and money owed by customers, minus current liabilities.
Book value
Assets minus liabilities, as shown in the accounts.
P/E
Price to earnings: the share price divided by yearly earnings per share.
Net cash
Cash minus all debt.
Margin of safety
The gap between price and value that protects you if you are wrong.

Related investors

Also from the wider world

  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • Francisco García Paramés. Taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch and Buffett's letters, then beat Spain's market for 21 years, survived his worst years, and kept going.
  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Luiz Barsi Filho. Shined shoes in a São Paulo tenement, then built his own pension one dividend share at a time, and became Brazil's biggest individual investor.
  • Allan Gray. Rose 10% at Fidelity while the 1962 market crashed, founded South Africa's biggest private fund firm and Orbis, then gave his control to charity.

From other countries

  • Benjamin Graham. Taught Buffett, wrote the two founding books of value investing, and turned a near-ruinous 1929 into rules for never being ruined again.
  • Anthony Bolton. Ran Fidelity Special Situations for 28 years, about 19.5% a year, by buying good companies the crowd had given up on.
  • Chandrakant Sampat. India's original value investor: left the family business in the 1950s, bought the Indian arms of global companies cheaply, and taught a generation.
