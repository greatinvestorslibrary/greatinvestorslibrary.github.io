His thinking in five minutes

John Templeton grew up in a small town in Tennessee, won a Rhodes scholarship, and in 1939 borrowed $10,000 to buy every cheap share he could as war broke out. His Templeton Growth Fund, started in 1954, looked for bargains across the world long before that was usual. His creed: buy at the point of maximum pessimism, search everywhere, stay humble, and never think you know it all.

  • 14.5% a year: Templeton Growth Fund, 1954 to 1992, as reported by AP and the New York Times
  • $10,000 → $2m: what $10,000 put into the fund in 1954 had grown to by 1992
  • 104 stocks: bought in 1939 at $1 or less, 34 of them in bankruptcy (some say 37); only 4 became worthless
  • $90m: profit on bets against overpriced technology shares in 2000, according to his great-niece

His thinking, one line each

  • Maximum pessimism. The best bargains appear when almost everyone is selling.
  • Search the world. Look in every country and every industry, not just your own.
  • Bargains among quality. Cheap is not enough: buy good businesses at low prices.
  • Value, not forecasts. Judge each company on its own merits, not the economic outlook.
  • Diversify and watch. Spread by industry, risk and country, and keep checking.
  • Humility. An investor who has all the answers hasn't understood the questions.

The big ideas

  1. Buy at maximum pessimism. Shares only become bargains when people sell them, and people sell when they are gloomy.
  2. The 1939 basket. As war broke out in Europe, he borrowed $10,000 and bought 100 shares in each of 104 companies trading at $1 or less.
  3. Search the whole world. He looked for bargains in every country, not just at home.
  4. Bargains among quality. Cheap is not enough.
  5. Value, not forecasts. Buy individual companies on their own merits, not on what you think the economy or the market will do.
  6. Diversify, and keep watching. There is safety in numbers.
  7. Don't panic. When markets crash, don't sell in fear.
  8. Selling into euphoria. The mirror of buying at maximum pessimism is selling at maximum optimism.
  9. Humility. Invest, don't speculate.
  10. Distance, thrift and giving. He did his best work far from Wall Street, lived frugally, and gave much of his fortune to the study of the spiritual side of life.

Where is the mood?

The four moods, and what he did

  1. Pessimism. Prices have fallen hard, the news is grim and people are selling to escape. This is the point he looked for: buy, carefully, among quality companies.
  2. Scepticism. Prices are recovering, but most people doubt it will last. Keep holding; bargains are getting rarer but still exist.
  3. Optimism. Rising prices feel normal and good news is everywhere. Be selective. Fewer bargains; check each holding is still cheap.
  4. Euphoria. Everyone is talking about shares, and 'this time is different' is in the air. Time to sell what is overpriced. In 2000 he even bet on falls.

A study aid built on the four moods widely credited to him; real markets rarely announce which stage they are in.

His 16 rules, in brief

  1. Aim for real returns, after tax and inflation.
  2. Invest; don't trade or speculate.
  3. Stay flexible about types of investment.
  4. Buy low.
  5. Look for bargains among quality companies.
  6. Buy value, not trends or forecasts.
  7. Do your homework, or hire wise experts.
  8. Diversify by industry, risk and country.
  9. Keep watching what you own.
  10. Don't panic.
  11. Learn from your mistakes.
  12. Begin with a prayer.
  13. Accept that beating the market is hard.
  14. Stay humble: nobody has all the answers.
  15. There is no free lunch.
  16. Don't be fearful or negative too often.

Words to know

Contrarian
Deliberately going against the crowd.
Bargain hunting
His own name for his style: buying shares far below what they are worth.
Real return
Your return after tax and inflation.
Short selling
Borrowing shares to sell them, hoping to buy them back later at a lower price.
Lock-up
A period after a share's first listing when insiders may not sell.
Initial public offering (IPO)
A company's first sale of shares to the public.
Mutual fund
A pooled fund that invests many people's money together.
Diversification
Spreading money across many holdings to reduce the harm of any one mistake.

Related investors

Also from the United States

  • Warren Buffett. Ran Berkshire Hathaway for sixty years and explained every decision in a yearly letter to its owners.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
  • Peter Lynch. Ran the Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, beat the market by a mile, and showed ordinary people how to find good stocks.
  • Benjamin Graham. Taught Buffett, wrote the two founding books of value investing, and turned a near-ruinous 1929 into rules for never being ruined again.
  • Philip Fisher. Ran his own firm for 68 years, found great companies by talking to everyone around them, and held Motorola for nearly half a century.
  • The Timeline: Buffett, Munger and Lynch side by side

From other countries

  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Anthony Bolton. Ran Fidelity Special Situations for 28 years, about 19.5% a year, by buying good companies the crowd had given up on.
  • Chandrakant Sampat. India's original value investor: left the family business in the 1950s, bought the Indian arms of global companies cheaply, and taught a generation.
