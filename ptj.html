His thinking in five minutes

Paul Tudor Jones learned to trade in the cotton pits of the 1970s, nearly wiped himself out in 1979, and decided never again to risk everything on one trade. He founded Tudor Investment in 1980, expected the 1987 crash, and his main fund is reported to have made 125.9% after fees that year, the year the market fell 22.6% in a single day. His rules are about defence first: cut losses, never add to losers, and look for trades that could pay five times what they risk.

  • 125.9%: his flagship fund's reported gain after fees in 1987, the crash year
  • 14 for 14: years up in the employee fund he managed alone: part of 1990 (from July), then every year 1991 to 2003
  • 5:1: the reward-to-risk ratio he looks for before taking a trade
  • 200 days: the moving average he uses as his line between offence and defence

His thinking, one line each

  • Defence first. Protect what you have; profits follow.
  • Losers average losers. Never add to a losing position.
  • Five to one. Risk one to make five, so you can be wrong most of the time.
  • The 200-day line. Below it, get out and play defence.
  • Everything is connected. Read currencies, bonds, stocks and commodities as one machine.
  • Give back. A good year should help people who had a bad one.

The 1987 crash

  • 25 August 1987 (record high): Dow 2,722.42

  • 19 October 1987 (Black Monday): Dow 1,738.74
  • 20 October 1987: Dow 1,841.01
  • 21 October 1987: Dow 2,027.85

The Dow closed at a record 2,722 on 25 August 1987. On Monday 19 October it lost 22.6% in one day, from 2,246.73 to 1,738.74, then bounced back above 2,000 within two days. It did not pass the old high until 24 August 1989. Market Wizards reported that his Tudor Futures Fund gained 62% in October 1987 alone, and his main fund is reported to have made 125.9% after fees that year.

The big ideas

  1. From Memphis to the cotton pit. After university a relative in the cotton business sent him to the great cotton trader Eli Tullis, who hired him and taught him on the floor of the New York Cotton Exchange.
  2. Mr. Stupid: the 1979 cotton loss. In 1979 a cotton trade went badly wrong and he lost 60 to 70% of his clients' money.
  3. Tudor Investment, 1980. In 1980 he started his own firm.
  4. 1987: the crash he expected. In 1987 his deputy Peter Borish mapped that year's market against the run-up to the 1929 crash, and the two expected a collapse.
  5. Defence first. His most repeated lesson: protect what you have before trying to make more.
  6. Five to one. He looks for trades that could make five times what they risk.
  7. The 200-day line. He watches the 200-day moving average of closing prices.
  8. Everything is connected. He is a global macro trader: he reads currencies, bonds, stocks and commodities as one system and bets on big shifts.
  9. Two eras. His flagship fund averaged about 26% a year from 1987 to 2007, then about 4.7% a year from 2008 to 2016.
  10. Robin Hood, and a reputation. After the 1987 crash he co-founded the Robin Hood Foundation to fight poverty.

His trademark: five to one

What does five to one really mean?

Put in how often your trades win and how much they make compared with what they risk. The calculator shows the average result per trade and the win rate you need just to break even.

The arithmetic: average result per trade = (win rate × reward) − (loss rate × 1), in units of what you risk. At 5:1, if 20% of trades win, the wins bring 0.2 × 5 = 1.0 and the losses cost 0.8 × 1 = 0.8, so on average each trade adds 0.2 of what you risk, before costs. The break-even win rate is 1 ÷ (1 + reward): about 16.7% at 5:1. On the full site you can try your own numbers.

Simple arithmetic for illustration: it ignores costs, taxes, slippage and the fact that real wins and losses vary. It is not a trading system.

His rules, in short

  1. Defence first: protect what you have.
  2. Never average losers: do not add to a losing trade.
  3. Trade smaller when it is going badly, bigger when it is going well.
  4. Look for five to one: risk one to make five.
  5. Respect the 200-day line: below it, get out.
  6. Never risk everything on one trade.

Our summary of his interviews in Market Wizards, with Tony Robbins and as quoted by Benzinga.

Defence or offence? Tap your answer

  1. A trade is down 20%. You buy more so your average price is lower. Offence gone wrong. Adding to a loser is exactly what he calls averaging losers.
  2. Before you enter, you know where you will get out if you are wrong, and the target is five times that distance. Defence. Knowing your exit and your reward-to-risk first is his 5:1 discipline.
  3. Your account is down three weeks running, so you double your position size to win it back. Offence gone wrong. He does the opposite: trade smaller when trading badly.
  4. An index you hold falls below its 200-day average. You sell and wait. Defence. That is his 200-day rule: below the line, play defence.

An honest note

In 1990 he pleaded guilty in a federal case after 86 acres of protected wetlands on his Maryland hunting estate were filled without a permit; he paid a $1 million fine and $1 million to the National Fish and Wildlife Foundation. In September 1996 Tudor Investment Corporation settled an SEC proceeding about alleged breaches of the 'uptick rule' in short sales over two days in March 1994, paying $800,000 without admitting or denying the findings. In April 2002 a private lawsuit claiming 'short-swing' profits under Section 16(b) was settled, with the Tudor defendants paying $1.45 million. In 2013 he apologised for saying that motherhood keeps women from being great traders, and in 2017, after a supportive email he had sent Harvey Weinstein was reported, he told staff he had believed and defended Weinstein too long. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Words to know

Futures
Contracts to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date; traded with borrowed money (leverage).
Leverage
Using borrowed money so gains and losses are larger than your own money alone.
Short selling
Selling something you do not own, hoping to buy it back cheaper.
Global macro
Trading on big economic shifts across currencies, bonds, stocks and commodities.
Moving average
The average closing price over a set number of days, redrawn each day.
Reward-to-risk
How much a trade could make compared with how much it could lose.
Drawdown
How far an account has fallen from its last high point.
Uptick rule
A US rule, since changed, limiting short sales to prices above the last trade.
