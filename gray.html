His thinking in five minutes

Allan Gray grew up in South Africa, studied at Harvard, and made his name at Fidelity in Boston in 1962, when the market fell 21.7% in a month and his portfolio rose. In 1973 he went home to Cape Town and started a firm that became South Africa's largest private investment manager, then founded Orbis to invest worldwide. He shunned publicity, asked more questions than he answered, and gave his controlling stake in both firms to a charitable foundation.

  • +10%: his Fidelity portfolio in 1962, while the market fell 21.7% in January
  • 1973: the year he started his own firm in Cape Town
  • 19.2%: a year: the firm's flagship equity fund since 1998, against 14.4% for its benchmark
  • 100%: of the family's controlling stake given to a charitable foundation in 2015

His thinking, one line each

  • Be contrarian. Look where others will not: cheap, neglected, unloved.
  • Think in cycles. Good times create bad times, and bad times create bargains.
  • Value, then dream. Stand on business value, and see the upside others miss.
  • Accept being wrong. Win more on the right calls than you lose on the wrong ones.
  • Serve the client. Fees and incentives that line up with clients' results.
  • Give it back. Wealth from free enterprise should help others.

The big ideas

  1. Butterworth to Harvard. He came from a pioneering family in the Eastern Cape, overcame dyslexia, and studied at Rhodes, the LSE and Harvard.
  2. 1962. At Fidelity in Boston, the market fell 21.7% in January 1962 and stayed weak until October.
  3. Chosen, not sold. In 1973 he started his own firm in Cape Town, believing clients should choose its services rather than be sold them.
  4. Contrarian by nature. He looked for cheap, neglected companies others overlooked, and trusted his own judgment over the crowd's mood.
  5. Cycles. He believed in cycles: good times breed bad times, and strong businesses bought near a cyclical low offer a discount and improving results.
  6. Value, then dream a little. He stood firmly on what a business was worth, then looked for the upside the market was not pricing.
  7. Wrong, and changing your mind. He accepted being wrong sometimes, because good calls would more than pay for bad ones, and he changed his mind when the facts changed.
  8. The price of being different. He warned that a long-term contrarian approach pays only for those who can bear long, uncomfortable spells of underperformance.
  9. Fees that serve clients. His firms tied their fees to results, so they earned more only when clients did, an idea Orbis took further with refundable fees.
  10. Giving it all back. He funded students' education for years, then gave his family's controlling stakes in both firms to a charitable foundation.

Crowd or contrarian?

Crowd or contrarian?

  • Buy what everyone is talking about or buy what everyone has forgotten
  • Sell when a good company has a bad year or buy more if the business is still sound
  • Judge a manager by the last year or judge by whole cycles
  • Stick to your view whatever happens or change your mind when the facts change

His side is on the right, from colleagues' accounts in 'Allan's legacy', in our words.

Five lessons he left

  1. Look where others won't: neglected, cheap, unloved.
  2. Think in cycles: good times make bad times.
  3. Stand on value, then dream a little.
  4. Expect to be wrong sometimes, and size for it.
  5. Bear the lean years: they are the price of being different.

Words to know

Contrarian
Going against the crowd: buying what others avoid, selling what they chase.
Cycle
The repeating rise and fall of an industry or market.
Compounder
A business that keeps growing its value at a high rate for many years.
Benchmark
The index or average a fund is compared with.
Performance fee
A fee that rises when a fund beats its benchmark and falls when it lags.
Refundable fee
A performance fee held back and returned if later results fall short.
Underperformance
Doing worse than the benchmark.

Related investors

Also from the wider world

  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • Francisco García Paramés. Taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch and Buffett's letters, then beat Spain's market for 21 years, survived his worst years, and kept going.
  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Peter Cundill. Read Graham on a plane in 1973, then hunted the world for shares below their liquidation value: the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.
  • Luiz Barsi Filho. Shined shoes in a São Paulo tenement, then built his own pension one dividend share at a time, and became Brazil's biggest individual investor.

From other countries

  • Zhang Lei. Learned investing at Yale under David Swensen, founded Hillhouse with $20 million, and preaches long-term bets on great entrepreneurs.
  • Walter Schloss. Never went to college, never visited a company, owned a hundred cheap shares at a time, and beat the market for 47 years.
  • Paul Tudor Jones. Lost most of his clients' money on one cotton trade in 1979, built his rules around defence, and is reported to have tripled his money in the 1987 crash.
