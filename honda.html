His thinking in five minutes

Seiroku Honda was born poor in 1866, became Japan's first doctor of forestry, and designed the forest of Meiji Shrine and parks across Japan. From the age of 25 he saved a quarter of every pay cheque before spending a yen, invested the savings in shares and forest land, and by 40 earned more from interest and dividends than from his salary. When he retired he gave almost all of it away. His 1950 book, My Confession of Wealth, is still read by Japanese savers today.

  • ¼: of every pay cheque saved first, from age 25, plus every bonus and windfall
  • Age 40: when interest and dividends passed his professor's salary
  • 30+: industries he owned shares in: railways, gas, power, paper, beer, spinning, cement and more
  • 2,600 ha: of Chichibu forest he gave to Saitama, whose income still funds student loans

His thinking, one line each

  • Save first. Set aside a quarter the day the money arrives, before spending anything.
  • Choose hardship early. Go through lean years while young, by choice, not by force.
  • Let it snowball. Small savings make a core; the core grows on its own.
  • Own good businesses. Spread savings across sound companies in many industries.
  • Bank part of the gain. Take a modest profit; when a holding doubles, sell half.
  • Give it back. Wealth from society should return to society.

The big ideas

  1. A poor farmer's son. He was born poor in 1866, studied his way up, and became Japan's first doctor of forestry and the designer of famous parks and forests.
  2. The quarter rule. Every time pay arrived, he set aside a quarter before spending anything, and saved every bonus and windfall in full.
  3. Hard years by choice. The rule hurt.
  4. Vanity is the enemy. He saw the wish to look well-off as the biggest obstacle to saving, and believed early hardship makes for a better life later.
  5. The snowball. Savings, once they form a core, grow by themselves.
  6. Own good businesses, widely. He put his savings into the sound companies of his day, starting with railways and spreading across more than thirty industries.
  7. Invest in what you know: his forest. As a forester, he understood land and trees.
  8. Take a modest profit, protect the principal. He followed a simple selling rule: take a 20% gain when it comes, and if a holding doubles, sell half so the original money is safe.
  9. Patience, and no shame about money. He waited patiently for good chances, invested boldly when times were bad, and rejected the old samurai pose of looking down on money.
  10. Give it back. At 60 he retired and gave almost everything away, mostly anonymously.

His three steps

  1. Save a quarter of income first, and every windfall in full.
  2. Invest once a little has built up.
  3. Wait patiently for good chances, without forcing anything.

As summarised by MUFG Bank from his book.

His selling rule

  1. A 20% gain: take it, bank it, and don't chase more.
  2. A double: sell half at once, so the original money is safe.

As described by Japan Exchange Group's education site, in our words.

In his own words

“金と言うものは雪達磨のようなもので”“Money is like a snowball.” From My Confession of Wealth, 1950, as quoted by President (our translation)
“貯金の問題は、方法の如何ではなく、実行の如何である”“Saving is a question not of method, but of doing it.” From My Confession of Wealth, as quoted by Daiwa (our translation)
“人生即努力、努力即幸福”“Life is effort; effort is happiness.” His motto, cited by his publisher (our translation)

Words to know

Tenbiki (天引き)
Deducting money at source, before you can spend it.
Meiji era
1868 to 1912: Japan's rapid modernisation under Emperor Meiji.
Sen (銭)
A hundredth of a yen, used in his day.
Nippon Railway
A private railway company of the Meiji era, nationalised in 1906.
Hectare
10,000 square metres, about 2.5 acres.
Bushi kuwanedo takayōji
'A samurai picks his teeth even when he hasn't eaten': keeping up appearances while pretending to scorn money.
Dividend
The share of profit a company pays to its shareholders.
