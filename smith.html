His thinking in five minutes

Terry Smith exposed creative accounting in 1992 and was sacked for refusing to withdraw his book. In 2010 he founded Fundsmith on three rules: buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing. Since launch it has returned 13.8% a year against 12.1% for world shares, though it trailed the market every year from 2021 to 2025.

  • 13.8% a year: Fundsmith Equity, Nov 2010 to Dec 2025
  • 12.1% a year: world shares (MSCI World) over the same years
  • +613%: total gain since launch, against +468% for world shares
  • +0.8%: in 2025: his weakest year, and the fifth in a row behind the index

His thinking, one line each

  • Buy good companies. Companies that earn high returns on their capital, in cash, year after year, without much debt.
  • Don't overpay. A great company bought at a silly price is a poor investment.
  • Do nothing. Trade rarely. In 2025 the fund replaced only about an eighth of its holdings.
  • Read the accounts. Reported profits can be dressed up. His first fame came from showing how.
  • You are the biggest risk. Investors hurt themselves most by chasing fashion, timing the market and trading too often.
  • Keep costs low. Every fee and dealing cost comes straight out of your return.

The big ideas

  1. Buy good companies. A good company earns a high return on the money tied up in it, in cash, and keeps doing so for decades.
  2. The numbers that show quality. Every year he publishes the same few measures for his companies beside the market's.
  3. Don't overpay. Even the best company can be a bad investment at the wrong price.
  4. Do nothing. Once you own good companies at sensible prices, the hardest and best thing is to leave them alone.
  5. Read the accounts. His first fame came from showing how companies dressed up their profits.
  6. You are the biggest risk. Most damage to investors' returns is self-inflicted: chasing fashion, trading too much, buying what they don't understand.
  7. Costs come out of your pocket. Fees, charges and dealing costs are certain; returns are not.
  8. Don't over-diversify. Twenty or so well-chosen companies give most of the safety of spreading out.
  9. Avoid poor businesses and star bosses. Stay away from companies that earn poor returns, and from companies that only work with a genius in charge.
  10. When it doesn't work. From 2021 to 2025 his fund trailed the market five years running.

The quality test (2025 figures)

  • Return on capital: his fund 31%, S&P 500 17%, FTSE 100 17%
  • Gross margin: his fund 62%, S&P 500 45%, FTSE 100 43%
  • Operating margin: his fund 28%, S&P 500 18%, FTSE 100 17%
  • Cash conversion: his fund 94%, S&P 500 89%, FTSE 100 99%
  • Interest cover (median): his fund 29x, S&P 500 9x, FTSE 100 8x

His ten rules, in our words

  1. Only invest in what you fully understand
  2. Don't try to guess the market's turns
  3. Keep fees as low as you can
  4. Trade as rarely as possible
  5. About twenty shares is enough: don't over-diversify
  6. Never invest just to save tax
  7. Never own poor-quality companies
  8. Prefer a business that doesn't need a genius to run it
  9. Don't buy hoping a greater fool will pay more later
  10. If the price upsets you, switch off the screen

The accounting tricks he exposed

  1. Acquisition accounting: writing down a bought company's assets before the deal, so later profits look bigger
  2. Deferred payments for acquisitions, which push the true cost into later years
  3. Extraordinary and exceptional items, used to keep bad news out of headline profit
  4. Off-balance-sheet finance: debts kept out of sight
  5. Contingent liabilities: promises that may cost money later
  6. Putting a value on brands as assets
  7. Changing depreciation rules to lift profit
  8. Capitalising costs: treating everyday spending as an investment
  9. Currency mismatching: borrowing where interest is low and depositing where it is high

Words to know

Return on capital employed (ROCE)
Operating profit as a percentage of the money tied up in a business. His main test of quality.
Gross margin
Sales minus the direct cost of making them, as a percentage of sales.
Operating margin
Profit after running costs, before interest and tax, as a percentage of sales.
Cash conversion
How much of the reported profit actually arrives as cash.
Interest cover
How many times profits cover the interest bill.
Free cash flow yield
The cash a company produces after investment, as a percentage of its value.
Turnover
The share of a portfolio bought and sold in a year.
Total cost of investment
All the costs of owning a fund, including dealing costs, not just the headline charge.
Index fund
A fund that simply buys a whole market index rather than choosing companies.
Creative accounting
Legal but misleading ways of making profits look bigger or smoother than they are.
