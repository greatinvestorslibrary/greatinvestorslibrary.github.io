His thinking in five minutes

Francisco García Paramés taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch's book and a letter he requested from Warren Buffett. From 1993 to 2014 his Spanish fund at Bestinver returned about 15.7% a year, more than five times its benchmark in total. He left over a disagreement with the owners, started Cobas in 2017, lived through years of painful losses, and stuck to his method. Spain calls him its Warren Buffett.

  • 15.7%: a year: his Spanish fund at Bestinver, 1993 to 2014
  • 2,279%: the fund's total gain over that time, against 410% for its benchmark
  • −14.1%: his first year running a global fund, 1998, while markets rose 16.5%
  • 20%: a year: Cobas's international fund over the five years to mid-2026

His thinking, one line each

  • Self-taught. Read the masters, then think for yourself.
  • Austrian lens. Markets work, are never in balance, and growth comes from saving.
  • Quality and price. High returns on capital, growth, little debt, bought cheap.
  • Risk is losing money. Not price swings, but the chance of a permanent loss.
  • No forecasts. Study companies, not the economy's next move.
  • Wait. Patience, and the clients to allow it, are the real edge.

The big ideas

  1. An uninspired start. He was an average student and a reluctant shop trainee.
  2. Self-taught at Bestinver. He joined a small fund firm owned by an industrial group as a young analyst, and within two years was running its Spanish fund.
  3. Twenty-one years of results. From 1993 to 2014 his Spanish fund returned about 15.7% a year: 2,279% in total, against 410% for its benchmark.
  4. Austrian economics. Ten years into his career he discovered the Austrian school of economics.
  5. Risk is losing money. He defines risk not as how much a price swings, but as the chance of losing money.
  6. Quality at a low price. He looks for strong businesses with high returns on capital, growth and little debt, bought when they are cheap.
  7. Leaving, and starting again. In 2014 he left Bestinver over differences with its owners.
  8. The hard years. Cobas had a brutal start: losses in 2018, then the worst period for value investing on record, and the Covid crash.
  9. The rebound. By mid-2026 his international fund had returned about 20% a year over five years, and Madrid had become Europe's value-investing capital.
  10. We are qualified to wait. His last lesson is patience: the best returns come to those who can wait, and to managers whose clients let them.

His checklist

Would it pass his checklist?

  1. It earns a high return on the capital it uses
  2. It has a strong competitive position that rivals find hard to copy
  3. It is still growing, not shrinking
  4. It carries little debt, or none
  5. It is run well, with good governance
  6. Its price is well below what you think it is worth
  7. You can hold it for years without needing the money

Built from his book, his Cobas letters and the CFA Institute's account of Bestinver, in our words. A study aid, not a recommendation.

Two ways to see risk

  • The price fell 30% this year or the business is losing its customers for good
  • Everyone says the economy will slow or the company has debts it cannot pay
  • The share is unpopular or you will need the money next month

On his view, the left side is noise and the right side is real risk. Our own examples.

Words to know

Value investing
Buying shares for less than the business is worth, then waiting.
Austrian school
A school of economics, from Mises and Hayek, that stresses markets, saving and the limits of forecasting.
ROCE
Return on capital employed: profit as a share of the money tied up in a business.
Benchmark
The index a fund is compared with.
Non-compete
A contract term that bars someone from working for a rival for a period.
Intrinsic value
What a business is really worth, based on the cash it will produce.
Volatility
How much a price swings up and down.

Related investors

Also from the wider world

  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Peter Cundill. Read Graham on a plane in 1973, then hunted the world for shares below their liquidation value: the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.
  • Luiz Barsi Filho. Shined shoes in a São Paulo tenement, then built his own pension one dividend share at a time, and became Brazil's biggest individual investor.
  • Allan Gray. Rose 10% at Fidelity while the 1962 market crashed, founded South Africa's biggest private fund firm and Orbis, then gave his control to charity.

From other countries

  • Seiroku Honda. A poor farmer's son who saved a quarter of every pay cheque from 25, invested it, and gave almost all of it away when he retired.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
  • Seth Klarman. Wrote a book that sold 5,000 copies and became a legend, and built a firm on one rule above all: avoid losing money.
