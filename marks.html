His thinking in five minutes

Howard Marks co-founded Oaktree Capital in 1995 and is best known for his memos to clients, which he has written since 1990. Buffett says they are the first thing he opens. Marks's message: you cannot predict the future, but you can know where you stand in the cycle, avoid big losses, and think differently from the crowd.

  • 1990: his first memo to clients; he has written them ever since, and they are free online
  • 4th percentile: where a pension fund ranked over 14 years though never in the top quarter in any year: the lesson of his first memo
  • ≈ $6 billion: invested by his partner's team from mid-September to the end of 2008, about $400 million a week, as panic peaked
  • 1995: co-founded Oaktree Capital in Los Angeles

His thinking, one line each

  • Second-level thinking. Ask not just what is good, but what everyone else already thinks.
  • Risk means losing money. Not price swings: the chance of a permanent loss.
  • It's what you pay. A good asset at too high a price is a bad investment.
  • The pendulum. Investor moods swing between greed and fear, rarely resting in the middle.
  • Prepare, don't predict. Know where you are in the cycle, even if you can't know what comes next.
  • Avoid the losers. Steady, above-average results with few disasters beat heroics.

The big ideas

  1. Second-level thinking. To do better than others you must think differently, and better.
  2. Risk means losing money. For Marks, risk is not the jiggling of prices.
  3. It's what you pay. There is no such thing as a good or bad asset in itself, only a good or bad price.
  4. The pendulum. Investor psychology swings like a pendulum between greed and fear, optimism and despair.
  5. You can't predict. You can prepare.. Nobody can forecast the economy or the market reliably.
  6. Take the temperature. Watch how people around you behave.
  7. bubble.com. In January 2000, near the peak of the technology bubble, he warned that internet and telecom shares had become a bubble.
  8. Buying in the panic. Being prepared paid off in late 2008.
  9. Avoid the losers. Superior results over time come less from big wins than from avoiding big losses.
  10. Writing to think, and saying 'I don't know'. His memos are how he thinks.

Take the temperature

Pick one side of each pair for today's market

  • People are fearful or People are confident
  • Lenders are cautious or Lenders are eager
  • New share sales are scarce or New share sales are everywhere
  • Prices are below average or Prices are well above average
  • Bad news is in the headlines or Good news is in the headlines
  • Investors avoid risk or Investors chase risk
  • Few talk about shares or Everyone talks about shares
  • Bargains are easy to find or Bargains are hard to find

Our own pairs, built on his idea of taking the market's temperature. A study aid, not a market forecast.

Start with his first memo, The Route to Performance (1990), three pages long, then the book The Most Important Thing (2011).

  • The Route to Performance. His first memo: the pension fund that reached the top 4% over 14 years by never having a bad year.
  • You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.. Why he ignores economic forecasts but takes cycles seriously, and why knowing where you are matters more than knowing where you are going.
  • Taking the Temperature. Five market calls from 2000 to 2020, from bubble.com to the 2008 crisis and Covid, and how he reads the market's mood.
  • The Most Important Thing. His memos distilled: second-level thinking, price and value, understanding and controlling risk, cycles and the pendulum.
  • Mastering the Market Cycle. The cycles in the economy, profits, credit and investor psychology, and how to position yourself as they turn.

  • The Best of. Marks picks his favourite memos from 35 years and says why each matters.
  • Getting the odds on your side. His cautious memos, why he never says 'get out', and being honest about what he does not know.

Words to know

Second-level thinking
Thinking about what others think, and what is already reflected in the price.
Distressed debt
Bonds and loans of companies in, or close to, financial trouble.
High-yield bond
A bond from a riskier company that pays more interest; also called a junk bond.
Cycle
The repeated rise and fall of economies, profits, credit and moods.
Percentile
A ranking out of 100: the 4th percentile means better than 96% of the group.
Intrinsic value
What an asset is really worth, based on what it will earn.
Volatility
How much and how fast a price moves up and down.

Related investors

Also from the United States

  • Warren Buffett. Ran Berkshire Hathaway for sixty years and explained every decision in a yearly letter to its owners.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
  • Peter Lynch. Ran the Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, beat the market by a mile, and showed ordinary people how to find good stocks.
  • Benjamin Graham. Taught Buffett, wrote the two founding books of value investing, and turned a near-ruinous 1929 into rules for never being ruined again.
  • Philip Fisher. Ran his own firm for 68 years, found great companies by talking to everyone around them, and held Motorola for nearly half a century.

From other countries

  • Mark Minervini. Lost all his capital in the 1980s, then studied history's biggest winning stocks and won the U.S. Investing Championship twice, in 1997 and 2021.
  • Zhang Lei. Learned investing at Yale under David Swensen, founded Hillhouse with $20 million, and preaches long-term bets on great entrepreneurs.
