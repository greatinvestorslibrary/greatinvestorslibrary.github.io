John Maynard Keynes: Animal spirits

Much of what drives business and markets is not calculation but a spontaneous urge to act, which can switch off suddenly.

  • Not just arithmetic. Many decisions to invest rest on spontaneous optimism, which he called animal spirits, rather than on careful calculation. (General Theory, 1936)
  • When spirits fade. If optimism dims, investment can collapse even with no new facts. (General Theory, 1936)
  • Speculation and enterprise. He warned that when a nation's investment is driven by casino-like speculation, the job is likely to be done badly. (General Theory, 1936)
  • Use the mood, don't share it. His own best buys came when prices were greatly depressed: when animal spirits were at their lowest. (Memo, 1938)

Ask yourself

  • Where do you see animal spirits running high right now?
  • How would you tell a casino from an enterprise in today's market?
