Charlie Munger: Business models, moats and scale

He studied why some businesses compound for a century while others never earn their keep, and taught it through plain examples.

  • Scale isn't enough. Sears's advantages of scale did not stop it losing heavily to Walmart. Bureaucracy did Sears terrible damage. (USC 1994)
  • Who keeps the gain? In a commodity business, the advantages of great improvements flow through to customers, not to owners. (USC 1994)
  • Razor and blades. Gillette began selling blades in multiple packages, with the razor handle a one-time purchase. Repeat purchases built the business. (USC 1994)
  • A gem under the mess. GEICO had a magnificent business submerged in a mess, but still working. Clearing the mess released its value. (USC 1994)
  • Coca-Cola from scratch. It is 1884 in Atlanta. You have $2 million and must build a business worth $2 trillion. How would you do it? (Practical 1996)
  • Start from the arithmetic. He set the target first: 2.92 trillion eight-ounce servings in 2034. Netting 4¢ a serving would earn $117 billion. The numbers show what the business must become. (Practical 1996)
  • Many factors at once. A strong trademark, Pavlovian conditioning, social proof and being always available, all working together. (Practical 1996)
  • Owners can wreck a brand. With New Coke, the company's own executives came to the brink of destroying the most valuable trademark in the world. (Practical 1996)
  • Inflation sorts businesses. Some businesses produce little cash even when volume holds steady. Borrowing short and lending long, the savings and loan model, can stop working and go into reverse. (Blue Chip 1982)
  • No jewel. He told owners plainly that his savings and loan, as it then had to be run, was no jewel. Careful operators in that business earned very modest returns. (Wesco 1985)
  • Owners and managers. In that industry, managers were mostly not owners, and their rewards were tied to size and recent numbers. (Wesco 1985)
  • A weaker vehicle, said plainly. Each dollar of Wesco's book value, he wrote, gave much less intrinsic value than a dollar at Berkshire. (Wesco 2006)
  • Respect rivals you dislike. People underrate both the competence and the morals of competitors they dislike. (PHM 2005)

Ask yourself

  • For each holding: when the industry adopts the next cost-saving technology, does the owner keep the saving or does the customer?
  • Which of your businesses is a commodity described in flattering words?
