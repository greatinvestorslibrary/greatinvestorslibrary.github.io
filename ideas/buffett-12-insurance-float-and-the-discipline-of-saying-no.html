Warren Buffett: Insurance: float and the discipline of saying no

Every letter returns to insurance. The ideas carry over to any business that takes money today against a promise to pay later, such as lenders.

  • Float. Premiums arrive now and claims are paid later. Meanwhile the insurer invests money it holds and does not own. If premiums cover claims and expenses, the float is free, or better than free. (1995 to 2024)
  • Three questions about float. How much is there, what does it cost, and what is the long-run outlook for both? Size alone tells you nothing. (2002)
  • Four disciplines. Understand every exposure. Assess the likelihood and cost of loss conservatively. Set a price that leaves a profit. Walk away if that price is not available. (2011 · 2014)
  • The fourth is where most fail. National Indemnity let its volume shrink for years when prices were poor, and promised no layoffs so that staff would not write bad business to keep their jobs. Most insurers cannot bear to watch competitors take the business. (2004 · 2012)
  • Easy in, hard out. A badly priced contract takes a moment to sign and can take decades to pay for. (1995)
  • Surprises go one way. In long-dated promises, bad surprises far outnumber good ones. Reserve and price with that in mind. (2005)
  • Profit that is not profit yet. Under-priced catastrophe cover can look like easy money for years before one event takes it all back. Judge such a business over a long span. (1996 · 1997)
  • A weak reinsurer is no protection. Cheap cover from a party that may not pay is expensive. The same holds for any guarantee. (2002)
  • A revolving fund. Accounts show float as a debt due. In practice it is replaced as it is paid out, so its economic burden is far lower than the stated figure, as long as underwriting holds. (2012 to 2016)
  • The long tail. The true cost of some policies is not known for decades. Optimism has no place in pricing them. (2024)
“A bad reinsurance contract is like hell”Warren Buffett, 1995 letter

Ask yourself

  • For any lender or insurer I own: has it ever chosen to shrink when pricing was poor?
  • How fast is it growing against the industry, and what would have to be true for that growth to be safely priced?
