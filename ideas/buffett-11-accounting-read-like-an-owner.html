Warren Buffett: Accounting: read like an owner

Reported numbers are where analysis starts. His letters are a thirty-year course in where they mislead.

  • Depreciation is real. It is a worse cost than most, because the cash goes out first and the benefit arrives later. He is scathing about measures that leave it out. (2002)
  • Adjusted earnings. Share-based pay is a cost. Restructuring that recurs every few years is a cost. Figures that strip them out flatter management. (2015 · 2016 · 2021)
  • Standing still costs more than the books say. For capital-heavy businesses, real maintenance spending runs above the depreciation charge. Owner earnings are lower than reported profit. (2016 · 2018 · 2023)
  • The big bath. Large one-time charges and reserves set up at acquisitions can be fed back into later years to smooth profit. He named the practice and declined to use it. (1998)
  • Not all amortisation is a cost. Writing down software is a true expense. Writing down purchased customer lists or goodwill often is not, because the asset may be growing in value. Separate the two before judging earnings. (1999 · 2012)
  • The chosen base year. A growth table that starts in a terrible year or ends in a wonderful one can make ordinary performance look brilliant. Check the endpoints. (2003)
  • Bottom-line noise. Since unrealised gains on shares began flowing through profit, reported net income swings wildly and says little. He directs readers to operating earnings. (2017 · 2018 · 2019)
  • Book value retired. After three decades as his yardstick, he dropped book value. It understates operating businesses, and buybacks above book reduce it while raising value per share. (2018)
  • Three warning signs. Weak accounting choices, footnotes you cannot follow, and management that broadcasts profit forecasts. If you cannot understand a note, he assumes that was intended. (2002)
  • Self-graded exams. An insurer's profit depends on its own estimate of future claims. Such estimates are usually too low. The same caution applies to any business where today's profit rests on a guess about tomorrow's losses. (1999 · 2001 · 2017)
  • Four questions for the auditor. He drafted them for audit committees. The first: had the auditor alone prepared these accounts, what would have been done differently? (2002)

Ask yourself

  • What is this company's profit after real maintenance spending and after counting share-based pay?
  • Which number in the accounts depends most on management's own estimate, and what happens if it is 20% wrong?
