Warren Buffett: Managers, pay and boards

He looks for people who run a company as if it were their family's only asset, and for pay and boards that keep them that way.

  • Three tests, in one sentence. Good returns on the tangible capital the business needs, managers who are both able and honest, and a sensible price. (2019)
  • The hundred-year instruction. His managers are told to run the business as if they owned all of it, it were the only asset their family would ever have, and it could not be sold for a century. (1998)
  • Choose carefully, then leave them alone. He hires with great care and manages very little. Capital allocation and the choice of chief executive stay at the centre. Everything else is delegated. (2010)
  • Pay for what the manager controls. Bonuses tied to the results of the manager's own unit. A share price moves for reasons outside any one person's control and makes a poor yardstick. (1996 · 1997 · 2006)
  • Options are a cost; bought shares are commitment. Accounting long treated options as free. He counts them as pay and deducts them when valuing a company. He prefers managers who buy shares with their own money and carry the downside too. (1998 · 2001 · 2002)
  • Beware the chief executive who forecasts growth. Very few large companies sustain 15% a year. A leader who promises it will eventually be tempted to manufacture it. (2000)
  • The company that always makes its number. Business is too uneven for that. Consistently meeting expectations is evidence of management of the figures. (2002 · 2016 · 2022)
  • What a director is for. Business sense, an owner's outlook, and shares bought with personal money. When the fee is a large part of a director's income, independence is hard to keep. The core job is choosing and overseeing the chief executive. (2002 · 2003 · 2019)
  • The ratchet. Pay committees compare with peers, nobody wants to be below average, and so the average climbs every year. (2005)
  • Candour. Spin travels inward: tell the public a flattering story for long enough and you start to believe it. He writes each letter as he would want to be written to if a relative ran the business and he were the absent owner. (1995 · 2023)
  • No thumb-sucking. Munger's term for putting off the correction of a mistake. He calls delay the cardinal sin, worse than the original error. He adds that he never looks at where a candidate went to school. (2024)

Ask yourself

  • Does this promoter own a large stake bought or held with personal money, and how has minority capital been treated in past deals?
  • Does the annual report speak plainly about what went wrong last year?
