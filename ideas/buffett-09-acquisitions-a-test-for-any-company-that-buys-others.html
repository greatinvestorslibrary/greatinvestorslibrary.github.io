Warren Buffett: Acquisitions: a test for any company that buys others

Most deals help the seller and the adviser. Use his filters to judge every serial acquirer you own.

  • The seller knows more. The seller picks the moment and writes the forecast. He treats sellers' projections as entertainment. Most acquisitions damage the buyer's shareholders. (1995)
  • Compare with the stock market. Every proposed purchase has to beat the alternative of simply buying shares in the best listed companies. Many managements never run that comparison. (1995)
  • His six filters. Large enough to matter. Consistent earning power already shown. Good returns on equity with little debt. Management in place. A simple business. A stated price. No turnarounds, no auctions, no hostile bids. (1999 · 2003 · 2008)
  • Be the buyer of choice. Owners who care what happens to their company after the sale prefer a buyer with no exit plan and no debt-loading. That reputation took decades to build and wins deals money alone could not. (2000 · 2002 · 2008)
  • The spreadsheet never disappoints. Once a chief executive wants a deal, the forecasts and the promised synergies will be found to support it. In 2017 he sat out because prices for decent businesses had reached records, pushed by cheap debt. (2017)
  • Who is advising? An adviser paid only if the deal closes will favour closing. He proposed hiring a second adviser paid only if the deal fails. (2009 · 2019)
  • Like a marriage. Surprises arrive after the ceremony. Most of his have worked out, and the good ones grow to become most of the value while the poor ones shrink in importance. (2019)
  • Overpaying, in his own words. The $11 billion after-tax write-down on Precision Castparts was his error alone. He was too hopeful about its normal level of profit and so paid too much. Nobody misled him. (2020)
  • The handshake. Some of his best purchases closed after one meeting with no formal checks. That works only when you know exactly who you are dealing with. (1997 · 2003)
  • Tuck-ins under proven managers. Small additions to businesses run by tested people carry the least risk. They extend talent already proven. (2006)
“Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut.”Warren Buffett, 2019 letter

Ask yourself

  • Does this company's acquisition record pass his six filters? What did it pay with, cash or shares?
  • After its last three deals, did return on capital rise or fall?
