Warren Buffett: Moats: what makes a business worth owning

A great business earns a high return on the money inside it, and can keep doing so because something stops competitors from taking it away.

  • Three kinds of savings account. Great: pays a very high rate and needs few new deposits to grow. Good: pays a fair rate, but only on large new deposits. Gruesome: grows fast, demands ever more money and pays little on it. He names See's Candies, FlightSafety and airlines as the three. (2007)
  • See's in numbers. Bought in 1972 for $25 million, when it earned under $5 million before tax on $8 million of capital. By 2007 it earned $82 million on $40 million. Along the way it sent $1.35 billion to the parent after keeping only $32 million for itself. That cash bought other businesses. (2007)
  • The word that matters is enduring. A moat that must be rebuilt every few years is not a moat, which rules out industries prone to rapid change. And if results depend on one superstar, the star is great, not the business. (2007)
  • The Inevitables. A handful of companies will almost certainly dominate their fields for decades. A larger group will probably do so. Leaders in fast-moving industries belong to neither group. Even with an Inevitable you can overpay, and the usual way they get hurt is management drifting away from the core. (1996)
  • Is the moat wider this year? He asks managers to strengthen the competitive position every year, even at the cost of current profit. Earnings bought by squeezing customers or skimping on the brand are a withdrawal from the moat. (2005 · 2008)
  • Lowest cost wins slowly. Where customers buy mainly on price, the operator with the lowest costs keeps taking share. GEICO is his thirty-year example. (1995 · 2000 · 2013)
  • Some trades demand brilliance daily. A retailer has to be clever every single day, because rivals copy each good idea at once. Other businesses run for decades on one right decision made long ago. He prefers the second kind. (1995)
  • Moats can drain. Newspapers were close to a local monopoly. Once readers and advertisers had alternatives, capable managers could only slow the decline. When industry economics and management pull in opposite directions, economics wins. (2006 · 2012)
  • Growth is a number, not a virtue. Growth that needs capital earning a poor return destroys value. Seeing that an industry will grow is easy. Working out who will keep the profit is the hard part, and when he cannot do that he passes. (2007 · 2009)
  • Later, he bought capital-heavy. With too much cash for small gems, he bought a railroad and utilities: huge capital needs, steady returns on very large sums. In 2023 he said the railroad must spend more than its depreciation just to stand still, and that utility regulation had turned out worse than he assumed. (2009 · 2010 · 2023)

Ask yourself

  • What exactly stops a well-funded competitor from taking this company's customers? Has that barrier grown or shrunk in three years?
  • Of each extra unit of profit, how much must go back in just to stay in place?
