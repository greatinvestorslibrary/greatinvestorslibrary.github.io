Warren Buffett: Price, value and the margin of safety

Value is the cash a business will hand its owners over time. Price is what you pay for that. The gap between the two is your return and your protection.

  • Aesop wrote the formula. A bird in the hand against birds in the bush. To value anything you need three answers: how sure are you the birds are there, when will they appear and how many, and what does a risk-free bond pay meanwhile? Growth is one input among several, and it can subtract value. (2000)
  • A range, never a point. Intrinsic value is an estimate. If the honest range is too wide to act on, move to something else. A rough figure that is about right beats an exact one that is wrong. (2000 · 2005 · 2010)
  • The two numbers are different things. What you pay and what you get are separate questions. In the crisis he restated the aim: a good business at a fair price, ahead of a poor one at a low price. (2008)
  • Wonderful at fair beats fair at wonderful. He began with statistically cheap, mediocre companies. Munger persuaded him that such bargains do not scale and seldom last. This is the largest single change in his method, and he credits it entirely to his partner. (2012 · 2014 · 2023)
  • Great companies at full prices earn ordinary returns. Buy first-class businesses at rich valuations and you earn roughly their profit growth, nothing extra. A high price removes the margin of safety even when the business is superb. (2005 · 1997)
  • A stated hurdle. He stayed out of shares unless he saw a high probability of at least 10% a year before tax. Idle cash was the accepted alternative. (2002)
  • Check expectations against arithmetic. Shareholders together cannot earn more than businesses earn. Any return forecast has to fit economic growth plus inflation plus dividends, less costs. He ran this sum against the optimism of 1999 and against pension-fund assumptions in 2007. (1999 · 2007)
  • Value it as if it had no debt. Cheap borrowing can make a poor purchase look fine per share. He prices every business on an all-equity basis, then decides. (2000 · 2017)
  • Size is an anchor. As capital grows, the list of ideas that can move the total shrinks. A small investor has a real edge here, and it erodes with every doubling. (1997 · 1998)
“Price is what you pay; value is what you get.”Warren Buffett, 2008 letter
“nothing recedes like success”Warren Buffett, 1997 letter

Ask yourself

  • What return does today's price imply if the business simply does what it has done before? What has to go right for more?
  • Is my estimate a range? How wide is it, and would I still buy at the bottom of it?
