Warren Buffett: Capital allocation: what happens to retained profit

Over a decade, what management does with the profit it keeps counts for more than the profit itself.

  • An order of preference. First, reinvest in existing businesses where that widens the moat. Second, buy other businesses. Third, repurchase shares when they sell well below value. Dividends come when none of these can earn a good return. (2012)
  • Dividend or sell a little? He worked an example: owners who keep everything reinvested and sell a small slice each year can end with more cash and more capital than under a payout, provided retained money earns well. Each owner also sets his own level of income. (2012)
  • Buybacks need two conditions. The company has more cash than the business needs, and the shares trade below a conservative estimate of value. With both, buying back is the surest use of money. Without the second, it destroys value for those who stay. (1999 · 2011 · 2016)
  • The IBM paradox. If you own a company that keeps buying its own shares, and you are not selling, you should hope the price stays low for years. Each repurchase then lifts your share of the business by more. (2011)
  • Ownership that grows by itself. Apple's repurchases raised Berkshire's percentage stake year after year without Berkshire buying a share. (2020 · 2021)
  • Issuing shares is selling the family silver. Paying for an acquisition with stock means handing over a slice of everything you already own. He paid for Dexter Shoe in shares; by 2007 those shares were worth $3.5 billion, for a purchase of about $400 million. (1997 · 2005 · 2009)
  • The 1924 insight. Edgar Lawrence Smith set out to show bonds beat shares and found the opposite, because companies keep part of their profit and compound it. Retained earnings are the engine. Everything else is commentary. (2019)
  • Deferred tax is an interest-free loan. Tax owed on unrealised gains stays invested and working until the day you sell. Each sale ends that loan. (2018)
  • Options that reward hoarding. A fixed-price option gains value when a manager simply retains earnings, with no skill involved. Watch how pay is set where profits pile up. (2005)
“What is smart at one price is stupid at another.”Warren Buffett, 2016 letter

Ask yourself

  • Over five years, how much profit did this company retain, and how much extra profit did that retained money produce?
  • When did management last buy back or issue shares, and at what price against value?
