André Kostolany

His thinking in five minutes

André Kostolany was born in Budapest in 1906, learned the stock market in Paris, fled to America in 1940, and spent his last decades explaining markets to Germans in books, columns and on television. He was not a value investor but a speculator, and he said so. He grew rich twice, lost nearly everything in between, and claimed to be right only 51% of the time. His thirteen books sold about three million copies, yet the English-speaking world barely knows him.

  • 1906–1999: seventy-plus years in markets, from Paris in the 1920s to Munich in the 1990s
  • 2×: rich twice: betting on the 1929 crash, then on German bonds after the war
  • 51%: the share of his trades he said were right; 49% were wrong
  • 3 million: copies of his 13 books sold, plus over 400 magazine columns

His thinking, one line each

  • Money + psychology. Prices follow the supply of money and the mood of the crowd.
  • The egg. Rises and falls each have three phases; buy in panic, sell in euphoria.
  • The dog and its owner. Prices run ahead and behind, but return to the economy.
  • Four Gs. Money, thoughts, patience and luck: lack one and you shake.
  • Hard-boiled hands. Buy when the shaky sell; hold when they panic.
  • 2 + 2 = 5 − 1. You get there in the end, but be ready for the minus one.

The big ideas

  1. From Budapest to the Paris bourse. A wealthy boy who wanted to be an art critic, he was sent by his father to learn broking in Paris in the 1920s.
  2. Rich twice, broke in between. He made a fortune betting on falling prices around 1929, lost it, and came close to bankruptcy more than once.
  3. The German bond coup. After the war he bought old German bonds that almost no one wanted, betting that Germany would want its good name back.
  4. Money plus psychology. He said market trends come from two things: how much money is available, and the mood of the crowd.
  5. The egg. He drew the market cycle as an egg.
  6. The dog and its owner. Prices relate to the economy like a dog to its owner on a walk: running ahead, falling behind, but always coming back.
  7. The four Gs. He said a successful investor needs four Gs: Geld, Gedanken, Geduld and Glück.
  8. Shaky hands and hard-boiled ones. He split investors into the shaky, who buy in euphoria and sell in panic, and the hard-boiled, who do the opposite.
  9. Sleeping pills and trams. His most famous advice: buy good shares, take sleeping pills, and stop looking.
  10. Teacher of a nation. In later life he explained markets to millions of Germans in columns, seminars and television, and co-founded an early asset manager.

Shaky hands or hard-boiled?

Shaky hands or hard-boiled? Tap your answer

  1. Prices have doubled, everyone at dinner talks about shares, and you buy for the first time. Shaky hands. Buying in euphoria, when the crowd is already in, is his picture of shaky hands.
  2. The market has fallen 35% on panic headlines. You buy more with spare money. Hard-boiled. Buying in the exaggeration of a fall, with your own money, is what the hard-boiled do.
  3. You borrowed to buy, prices drop, and the lender forces you to sell. Shaky hands. Without your own money, the first G, you cannot hold: you become shaky whether you like it or not.
  4. You bought a year ago, nothing has happened, and you are still waiting calmly. Hard-boiled. Patience in the quiet middle phases is the third G.
  5. A share you follow jumps 20% in a day; you chase it before it 'gets away'. Shaky hands. Never run after a tram or a share: another one always comes.

The four Gs

  1. Geld (money): your own, never borrowed.
  2. Gedanken (thoughts): a plan you have thought through.
  3. Geduld (patience): the most important.
  4. Glück (luck): it comes more often to those with the first three.

An honest note

Kostolany was a speculator, not a value investor, and he did not hide it. In his early years he made and lost fortunes with large, sometimes borrowed bets, and he came close to bankruptcy more than once. He published no audited record, so his wealth and many trades are known only from his own books and later accounts. We include him for his lessons on crowd psychology and patience, which have lasted, and we recommend none of his speculative methods.

Words to know

Speculator
Someone who buys or sells expecting to profit from price moves, often over shorter periods.
Hausse and baisse
French and German words for a rising market and a falling one.
Shaky hands (zittrige Hände)
Investors who buy in euphoria and sell in panic.
Hard-boiled (hartgesottene)
Investors who act against the crowd and hold through falls.
Bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest and is repaid at the end.
London Debt Agreement
The 1953 treaty that settled West Germany's old foreign debts.
Margin
Borrowed money used to buy securities.

Related investors

Also from the wider world

  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • Francisco García Paramés. Taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch and Buffett's letters, then beat Spain's market for 21 years, survived his worst years, and kept going.
  • Peter Cundill. Read Graham on a plane in 1973, then hunted the world for shares below their liquidation value: the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.
  • Luiz Barsi Filho. Shined shoes in a São Paulo tenement, then built his own pension one dividend share at a time, and became Brazil's biggest individual investor.
  • Allan Gray. Rose 10% at Fidelity while the 1962 market crashed, founded South Africa's biggest private fund firm and Orbis, then gave his control to charity.

From other countries

  • Paul Tudor Jones. Lost most of his clients' money on one cotton trade in 1979, built his rules around defence, and made a reported 125.9% after fees in 1987, the year of the crash.
  • Philip Fisher. Ran his own firm for 68 years, found great companies by talking to everyone around them, and held Motorola for nearly half a century.
  • Terry Smith. Exposed creative accounting and lost his job for it, then built Fundsmith on three rules: buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing.
