His thinking in five minutes

Duan Yongping built two electronics companies, Subor and BBK, whose offshoots grew into the phone makers OPPO and vivo. He stepped back in 2001, moved to California and turned to investing: a large bet on NetEase when it was in trouble, then Apple and Kweichow Moutai for many years. His rules: buying a stock is buying the company, buy only what you understand, never borrow, and keep a list of things you will not do.

  • about 100×: his gain on NetEase, bought in its post-2000 crisis, by his own account (not audited)
  • $620,100: his winning bid for lunch with Buffett in 2006, a record then; the money went to charity
  • 1 → 3: BBK split into three companies in 2001; they grew into OPPO, vivo and imoo
  • 0: interest-bearing loans: a fixed rule at BBK, OPPO and vivo

His thinking, one line each

  • Buying a stock is buying the company. A company is worth the cash it will produce in future, discounted. Full stop.
  • Only what you understand. He says he truly understands only a handful of companies.
  • Right business, right people, right price. Business model and culture first; price matters least.
  • Keep a stop-doing list. Most damage comes from doing things you already know are wrong.
  • Never borrow. Debt speeds up both gains and losses; one bad soaking can be permanent.
  • Benfen and a calm mind. Do the right thing, do it right, and stay calm when others don't.

The big ideas

  1. Buying a stock is buying the company. One sentence from Buffett changed how he invested.
  2. Only what you understand. Be honest about what you understand.
  3. The stop-doing list. Doing the right thing mostly means refusing to do wrong things.
  4. Never borrow. His companies take no interest-bearing loans, and he never borrows to invest.
  5. Right business, right people, right price. Look at the business model and the culture first.
  6. Benfen and a calm mind. Benfen means doing what you should, even when nobody asked.
  7. Hold great companies. Good companies rarely need selling.
  8. NetEase, Apple and Moutai. Three bets made him famous as an investor.
  9. The Buffett lunch and giving back. In 2006 he paid a record sum to lunch with Buffett, to charity.
  10. The mistakes he admits. He is frank about his errors.

A stop-doing list

Your own stop-doing list

  1. I never borrow money to buy shares
  2. I never bet against a company by short selling
  3. I never buy a business I can't explain
  4. I never buy on a tip or a story alone
  5. I never trade to win other traders' money
  6. I never keep a mistake going just because stopping hurts
  7. I never buy a company whose culture I dislike
  8. I never pay for something that produces no cash

These are our study questions, drawn from his talks and answers. They are not his words and not a recommendation.

BBK's stop-doing list, as reported

  1. No selling on credit.
  2. No late payments to suppliers.
  3. No late wages.
  4. No haggling: one price for every customer.
  5. No making products for other brands.
  6. No interest-bearing loans.
  7. No attacking competitors.

Gathered from his talks and press interviews. BBK has not published an official list, and the list grew over the years.

His decision test, in his own description: first ask whether something is the right thing to do. Only then ask whether it makes money.

Words to know

Benfen (本分)
Doing what you should, honestly and without being asked; not taking advantage of others.
A calm, ordinary mind (平常心)
Staying rational and returning to fundamentals whatever is happening around you.
Stop-doing list (不为清单)
A written list of things a person or company will not do, whatever the short-term gain.
Discounted cash flow
Valuing a company by adding up the cash it will produce in future, each year's cash shrunk back to today's money.
Short squeeze
When a share that many have bet against rises fast, forcing them to buy back at high prices.
Call option
A contract giving someone the right to buy a share at a set price. Selling one brings in cash now, but caps your gain.
Contract manufacturing (OEM)
Making products for another company to sell under its brand.
Opportunity cost
What you give up by choosing one use of money over the next best one.
