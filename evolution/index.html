Questions people ask

When did life on Earth begin?

At least 3.5 billion years ago, the age of the oldest widely accepted fossils of microbes. Some evidence hints at life even earlier, but that is still debated.

Did humans evolve from monkeys?

No. Humans and today's apes share common ancestors. Our family line split from the line leading to chimpanzees roughly 6 to 7 million years ago, so chimps are our cousins, not our ancestors.

When did humans start walking upright?

Signs of walking on two legs go back about 6 to 7 million years. By about 3.7 million years ago our relatives were clearly walking upright: their footprints are preserved in volcanic ash in Tanzania.

When did modern humans (Homo sapiens) appear?

About 300,000 years ago in Africa, based on the oldest fossils found so far, from Morocco.

Were Neanderthals our ancestors?

They were close relatives, a sister group rather than direct ancestors. Our species met them and had children together, so many people today whose ancestors came from outside Africa carry a small share of Neanderthal DNA, around 1 to 2 percent.

What are mitochondria?

Tiny power stations inside our cells. They are descended from bacteria that were swallowed by another cell over a billion years ago and, instead of being digested, stayed and worked together with it.

1. The spark of life

  1. The first ancestor (About 4.2 billion years ago?)
    Every living thing, from bacteria to banyan trees to you, comes from one shared ancestor, nicknamed LUCA. It was a single cell, possibly living near hot vents on the sea floor. A 2024 gene study suggests it lived about 4.2 billion years ago.
  2. Microbe cities (3.5 billion years ago)
    Mats of microbes built layered mounds called stromatolites in shallow seas. For billions of years, all life was tiny and single-celled.
  3. Oxygen fills the air (2.4 billion years ago)
    Blue-green microbes learned to use sunlight and gave off oxygen as waste. It built up in the air and poisoned many older microbes. But it also became the most powerful fuel life has ever had.
  4. The great partnership: mitochondria (About 2 billion years ago)
    A big cell swallowed a small oxygen-breathing bacterium but did not digest it. The two became partners. The host gave it food and shelter; the guest burned that food with oxygen and paid rent in energy. Its descendants, mitochondria, are the power stations in almost every cell of your body.
  5. Life invents sex (About 1 billion years ago)
    Some cells began making special sex cells and fusing them, mixing genes from two parents. Every child became a new shuffle of its parents. That shuffling helps families stay one step ahead of germs.

2. Bodies and chemicals

  1. The first feeling chemicals (More than 600 million years ago)
    Simple animals began using chemical messengers to coordinate their bodies. The ancestor of oxytocin and vasopressin, chemicals that today help mothers bond with babies and partners bond with each other, already existed more than 600 million years ago.
  2. Estrogen's ancient lock (More than 600 million years ago)
    Hormones work like keys, and cells have locks called receptors. Scientists rebuilt the ancestor of all steroid-hormone locks in the lab. It worked like an estrogen receptor. The locks for testosterone, progesterone and cortisol all evolved later from it.
  3. Nerves and the first brains (About 560 million years ago)
    Worm-like animals with a front and a back evolved nerve cells bunched at the head end: the first brains. They used dopamine and serotonin, the same chemicals that shape your mood and motivation today.
  4. Eyes, shells and legs (539 million years ago)
    In the Cambrian explosion, nearly every major animal body plan appeared. Animals got eyes, shells, legs and jaws, and the first hunters chased the first prey.
  5. A backbone (About 520 million years ago)
    Small fish-like animals with a stiff rod down their backs appeared: the start of the line that leads to sharks, frogs, birds and us.
  6. The stress hormone, cortisol (About 450 to 420 million years ago)
    Early jawed fish gained a system for handling danger: a burst of stress hormones that frees up energy to fight or flee. The receptor that responds to cortisol evolved its sharp focus on cortisol between about 450 and 420 million years ago. Testosterone's receptor also first appears in these jawed fish.

3. From fins to fur

  1. Fins become legs (375 million years ago)
    Fish with sturdy, bony fins, like Tiktaalik, began pushing themselves through shallow water and onto mud. Their descendants became the first four-legged animals.
  2. Our side of the family splits off (About 310 million years ago)
    Animals evolved eggs with a waterproof shell and could finally leave the water for good. Soon the family split: one side led to reptiles and birds, the other, the synapsids, led to mammals and us.
  3. Our ancestors survive the Great Dying (252 million years ago)
    Giant volcanic eruptions killed more than 90% of sea species and most land animals. Among the survivors were small, burrowing relatives of mammals. Without them, there would be no us.
  4. Fur, milk and warm blood (Over 200 million years ago)
    The first mammals were small, furry and probably active at night, hiding from dinosaurs. They kept warm from the inside and fed their babies milk, made by glands that evolved from sweat-like skin glands.
  5. Babies grow inside (About 160 million years ago)
    The ancestors of most mammals began growing babies inside the mother, fed by a placenta. Amazingly, a key placenta gene came from an ancient virus that got stuck in our ancestors' DNA.
  6. The asteroid gives mammals their chance (66 million years ago)
    A rock about 10 km wide struck what is now Mexico. About three-quarters of all species died, including all dinosaurs except birds. Small mammals that could hide and eat almost anything came through, and soon took over.

4. The primates

  1. The first primates (About 56 million years ago)
    Small tree-climbing mammals evolved grasping hands and feet, flat nails instead of claws, and eyes facing forward to judge jumps between branches.
  2. Seeing in full colour (About 40 to 30 million years ago)
    Some monkeys gained an extra colour sensor in their eyes. Now they could spot red fruit and young leaves among green ones. Apes, including us, inherited this colour vision. Most other mammals see fewer colours.
  3. Apes lose their tails (About 25 million years ago)
    The first apes appeared and their tails disappeared. In 2024 scientists found a likely cause: a 'jumping gene' that landed inside a tail-building gene called TBXT. When they copied the change into mice, some mice were born without tails.
  4. We part ways with chimpanzees (More than 7 million years ago)
    Our family split from the line that led to chimpanzees and bonobos. They are not our ancestors but our closest living cousins.

5. Standing up

  1. Sahelanthropus: the first step? (About 7 million years ago)
    In Chad, scientists found a 7-million-year-old skull with a chimp-sized brain. In January 2026, a new study of its thigh bone found a ligament mark seen only in upright walkers. It probably still climbed trees, but it could walk on two legs.
  2. Ardi: half in the trees (4.4 million years ago)
    Ardipithecus, nicknamed Ardi, walked upright on the ground but still had a grasping big toe for climbing, like a thumb on the foot.
  3. Why stand up at all? (Between 7 and 4 million years ago)
    Nobody knows for sure. Leading ideas: walking on two legs uses less energy than knuckle-walking; it frees the hands to carry food and babies; it lets you see over tall grass; and it keeps you cooler in the sun. Several of these may be true at once.
  4. Footprints in the ash (3.66 million years ago)
    At Laetoli in Tanzania, two or three upright walkers left a trail of footprints in wet volcanic ash, which then hardened like cement. The prints look strikingly like ours.
  5. The first stone tools (3.3 million years ago)
    Someone, probably an australopith, struck stones together to make sharp-edged flakes for cutting. This is older than any known member of our own genus, Homo.
  6. Lucy (3.2 million years ago)
    Lucy, an Australopithecus afarensis, stood about 1.1 metres tall, with a brain little bigger than a chimp's. Her hips and knees show she walked upright, though her long arms were still good for climbing.

6. The first humans

  1. The first of our genus, Homo (2.8 million years ago)
    A jawbone from Ledi-Geraru in Ethiopia is the oldest known member of Homo. In 2025, teeth from the same area showed that early Homo lived at the same time and place as a new, unnamed kind of Australopithecus.
  2. The nutcracker cousins (2.7 to 1.2 million years ago)
    Paranthropus had huge jaws and giant flat back teeth for grinding tough plants. They lived for over a million years beside early humans, then died out. Not every branch of our family survived.
  3. Homo habilis, the handy one (2.4 to 1.4 million years ago)
    Homo habilis had a brain about 600 cubic centimetres, half as big again as Lucy's. Its group made simple stone tools to cut meat and smash bones for marrow. Richer food may have helped fuel bigger brains.
  4. Homo erectus: a body like ours (From about 1.9 million years ago)
    Tall, long-legged and narrow-hipped, Homo erectus had a body built for walking and running long distances. It survived for well over a million years, longer than our species has existed so far.
  5. The first humans leave Africa (1.8 million years ago)
    Early humans spread out of Africa. At Dmanisi in Georgia, five skulls about 1.8 million years old show they reached Asia long before our species existed.
  6. The hand axe (1.76 million years ago)
    Homo erectus invented the hand axe: a teardrop-shaped stone, carefully shaped on both sides. It was the Swiss Army knife of the Stone Age and stayed in use for over a million years.
  7. Losing fur, gaining sweat and dark skin (Roughly 2 to 1.2 million years ago)
    To stay cool while active in the hot sun, our ancestors lost most of their body fur and gained millions of sweat glands. Bare skin needed protection from strong sunlight, so dark skin evolved.
  8. How our brains grew (Over 3 million years)
    Brain size roughly tripled, mostly gradually: about 400 cubic centimetres in early hominins, 600 in Homo habilis, about 900 in Homo erectus, around 1,400 in Neanderthals and about 1,350 in people today. The brain is 2% of your weight but uses about 20% of your energy.
  9. The family tree branches (About 1 million years ago)
    In 2025, scientists rebuilt a crushed million-year-old skull from Yunxian, China. They argued it belongs to an Asian branch related to Denisovans, which would mean our lineage split from our cousins over a million years ago, earlier than thought.
  10. Neanderthals (About 430,000 to 40,000 years ago)
    In Europe and western Asia lived the Neanderthals: strong, stocky people built for cold, with brains as big as ours or bigger. They made tools, hunted big animals, cared for the injured and buried some of their dead.
  11. Making fire (400,000 years ago)
    People used wildfire much earlier, but the oldest proof of making fire comes from Barnham, England: burnt earth, cracked flint and lumps of pyrite that make sparks when struck. Cooking made food easier to chew and digest.
  12. Homo naledi, deep in a cave (335,000 to 236,000 years ago)
    In a deep, dark cave system in South Africa, scientists found over 1,500 bones of Homo naledi: a small human with a brain only a third the size of ours, living at the same time as early Homo sapiens.

7. Us

  1. Homo sapiens: us (300,000 years ago)
    Our species appeared in Africa. The oldest known fossils, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, have faces like ours but longer brain cases. Our rounder skulls and our chins came later.
  2. Why men are taller, on average (Over hundreds of thousands of years)
    Men are about 7% taller than women on average, roughly 12 to 13 cm, but the two height ranges overlap a lot. Part of the cause is chemical: estrogen closes the growth zones in bones earlier, and a height gene on the Y chromosome adds about 3 cm. Whether competition between males also drove it is debated.
  3. Curves and hips (Shaped over millions of years)
    Women have wider hips on average because babies must pass through the pelvis. At puberty, estrogen also stores fat on hips and thighs. One idea is that this fat holds special fats, like DHA, that later help build a baby's brain through breast milk.
  4. The mystery of permanent breasts (Timing unknown)
    Other mammals' breasts swell when feeding young and shrink afterwards. Human breasts grow at puberty and stay, because they are mostly fat, not milk glands. Ideas include: a side effect of our fatty bodies and estrogen; a sign of good health and fat reserves; and help in keeping babies warm. Nobody knows which is right.
  5. Beards and body hair (Timing unknown)
    At puberty, testosterone and a stronger form of it, DHT, switch many hair roots on the face and chest from fine hairs to thick ones. Women's roots get far less of these hormones. Why men evolved beards is unclear; studies suggest beards make men look older and more dominant to other men more than they attract women.
  6. Love is chemistry too (Built over millions of years)
    Falling in love lights up dopamine, the reward chemical. Oxytocin and vasopressin help build trust and long bonds between partners and between parents and babies. Humans raise children for an unusually long time, and strong bonds helped our ancestors share that work.
  7. 'Mitochondrial Eve' (Roughly 200,000 to 150,000 years ago)
    Mitochondria pass only from mother to child. Trace them back in every living person and they meet in one woman who lived in Africa roughly 150,000 to 200,000 years ago. She was not the only woman alive, just the only one whose unbroken line of daughters reaches everyone today.
  8. The Denisovans get a face (At least 146,000 years ago)
    Denisovans were first found in 2010 from DNA in a finger bone. In 2025, proteins and DNA from dental plaque showed the nearly complete 'Dragon Man' skull from Harbin, China, was a Denisovan: a big-skulled, heavy-browed human.
  9. The island 'hobbits' (About 100,000 to 50,000 years ago)
    On Flores in Indonesia lived Homo floresiensis, about 1.1 metres tall, alongside dwarf elephants and Komodo dragons. On Luzon in the Philippines lived another small human, Homo luzonensis. Both vanished around 50,000 years ago.
  10. The first symbols (About 100,000 to 75,000 years ago)
    In Blombos Cave, South Africa, people engraved crisscross patterns on pieces of red ochre and made beads from shells. Symbols mean minds that think in ideas, and probably language.
  11. The oldest cave art (67,800 years ago)
    On Muna island in Indonesia, someone pressed a hand to a cave wall and sprayed red pigment around it. At least 67,800 years old, it is the oldest known rock art, probably made by our own species.
  12. Out of Africa, for good (About 60,000 to 50,000 years ago)
    Groups of modern humans spread from Africa across Asia, reaching Australia by boat at least 50,000 years ago, and later Europe and the Americas. Everyone outside Africa descends mostly from this movement.
  13. We met our cousins (About 50,500 to 43,500 years ago)
    Modern humans and Neanderthals had children together, mainly in a period around 47,000 years ago. Most people outside Africa carry about 1 to 2% Neanderthal DNA. People in Asia and Oceania also carry some Denisovan DNA.
  14. The last of the others (About 40,000 years ago)
    Neanderthals disappeared around 40,000 years ago, after the island humans and probably before the last Denisovans. Why is still debated: climate swings, small populations, competition, and mixing with us may all have played a part. Since then, we have been the only humans on Earth.
  15. Wolves become dogs (Roughly 30,000 to 15,000 years ago)
    Some wolves began living near human camps. Over generations they became dogs, our first tamed animal and hunting partner.
  16. Farming changes our bodies (About 11,700 years ago)
    As the Ice Age ended, people began farming. Food became more reliable, populations grew, and villages became cities. Our jaws got smaller with softer food, which is one reason wisdom teeth often do not fit.
  17. Grown-ups drinking milk (Mostly in the last 5,000 years)
    Most mammals lose the ability to digest milk sugar after childhood. In some farming peoples in Europe, Africa, the Middle East and South Asia, gene changes spread that keep it working for life. Famine and disease may have made it vital.
  18. You are here (Today)
    Over 8.2 billion people, all one species, sharing 99.9% of our DNA. We carry fish fins in our arms, a virus in our placenta, bacteria-turned-batteries in our cells, and a little Neanderthal in many of us. And we are still evolving.
