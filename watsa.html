His thinking in five minutes

Prem Watsa grew up in Hyderabad, studied chemical engineering at IIT Madras, and reached Canada in the early 1970s with almost no money. In 1985 he took control of a small, struggling trucking insurer and renamed it Fairfax Financial. He ran it on Ben Graham's value principles and wrote to its owners every year. Since 1985 Fairfax's book value per share has grown about 18.7% a year, and people call him Canada's Warren Buffett.

  • 18.7%: a year: growth in Fairfax's book value per share, with dividends, 1985 to 2025
  • 11.5%: a year: the S&P 500 with dividends over the same forty years
  • $2.1bn: of gains on credit default swaps by September 2008, bought from 2003 as protection
  • 15%: the long-term yearly target in Fairfax's guiding principles

His thinking, one line each

  • Value first. Buy below what a business is worth, as Graham and Buffett taught.
  • Protect the downside. Insure the company against disasters most people ignore.
  • Be early, and survive it. Being right too soon looks like being wrong; stay soundly financed.
  • Own your mistakes. Take the blame in the letter, and share the credit.
  • Decentralise. Hire good people, leave them alone, judge them over years.
  • Long-term only. Judge by book value per share over decades, not quarterly profit.

The big ideas

  1. Eight dollars and a degree. He grew up in Hyderabad, son of a teacher, studied engineering at IIT Madras, and arrived in Canada with almost nothing.
  2. A rescue becomes Fairfax. In 1985 he and his partners took over a small trucking insurer close to failure, renamed it Fairfax, and built it into a global insurance group.
  3. Graham's disciple. From his first letter he said Fairfax would follow the value approach of Benjamin Graham and his famous student, Warren Buffett.
  4. Book value per share, not quarters. He judges Fairfax by one long-term number: the growth of book value per share.
  5. Insure against the storm. He treats the investment portfolio like an insurer treats risk: buy protection against disasters others think impossible.
  6. Early looks like wrong. Being right too soon is painful.
  7. When protection costs too much. From 2010 he hedged all of Fairfax's shares against a deflationary slump that never came.
  8. Own your mistakes. His letters take the blame for what goes wrong and share the credit for what goes right, including public apologies.
  9. Integrity under scrutiny. In 2006 a Fairfax subsidiary restated its accounts and the SEC investigated.
  10. Patient, quiet and decentralised. He avoids publicity, runs Fairfax through independent managers, and thinks in decades.

Protection or a bet?

Fairfax's guiding principles, in short

  1. 15% a year: compound book value per share over the long term.
  2. Long term: focus on book value, not quarterly earnings.
  3. Soundly financed: always.
  4. Friendly growth: grow from within, and through friendly acquisitions.

From Fairfax's guiding principles as printed in its annual report, in our words.

Protection or a bet? Tap your answer

  1. You buy cheap insurance that pays if credit markets freeze, because a freeze would badly hurt your business. Protection. Insuring against the one thing that could sink you is how he described the swaps from 2003.
  2. You short the market with borrowed money because you think it will fall next month. A bet. A short-term call on direction, with borrowed money, is a bet, not insurance.
  3. You keep paying for a hedge for six years, though the danger you feared keeps not coming. A bet. When protection costs more than it could save, it becomes a costly view on the market: the 2010–2016 lesson.
  4. You keep enough cash and little debt so a crash can never force you to sell. Protection. Being soundly financed is the cheapest protection of all.

Our own examples of the distinction. A study aid, not advice.

An honest note

In February 2006 Fairfax's subsidiary Odyssey Re restated its results for errors in accounting for finite reinsurance contracts. Fairfax and Prem Watsa received SEC subpoenas in March 2006 relating to an answer he gave on an investor call about the company's review of those contracts. In June 2009 Fairfax said the SEC had completed its investigation and did not intend to recommend any enforcement action. Separately, the 2010–2016 equity hedges and some investments, such as BlackBerry, lost Fairfax a great deal of money, which he has acknowledged. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Words to know

Book value per share
What a company's assets minus its debts are worth on paper, divided by the number of shares.
Float
Money an insurer holds from premiums before it pays claims, which it can invest meanwhile.
Credit default swap (CDS)
A contract that pays out if a borrower fails to repay its debts; a kind of insurance.
Hedge
A position that gains when your main holdings fall, to cushion losses.
Deflation
Prices falling across an economy, which makes debts heavier and profits harder.
Finite reinsurance
A kind of reinsurance that transfers little real risk; its accounting drew scrutiny in the mid-2000s.
Decentralised
Run by the managers of each business, not from head office.
