His thinking in five minutes

Cheah Cheng Hye grew up poor in Penang, sold pineapples by the road after his father died, and never went to university. He became a financial journalist in Hong Kong, taught himself accounting, and in 1993 co-founded Value Partners with less than US$5 million. Its Classic Fund rose about 3,588% over the next 32 years, against 722% for the Hang Seng Index. Asia calls him its Warren Buffett.

  • < US$5m: Value Partners' money under management when it opened in 1993
  • +3,588%: Classic Fund, April 1993 to January 2025, against +722% for the Hang Seng Index
  • 2,500: company visits a year by his firm's analysts
  • 7 of 25: years in which the Classic Fund lost money, to 2018

His thinking, one line each

  • Do the legwork. Visit companies; spend little time in the office.
  • Graham, adapted. Classic value, with extra weight on cash flow and governance.
  • Buy when others sell. And sell when they buy.
  • Dull is fine. Prefer unexciting shares when the crowd chases thrills.
  • Kill the ego. Take yourself out of the decision.
  • Judge people. Most mistakes come from misjudging management.

The big ideas

  1. Pineapples in Penang. He grew up poor in Penang.
  2. Seventeen years of journalism. At 17 he joined a Malaysian newspaper, moved to Hong Kong in 1974, and spent 17 years reporting on Asian business.
  3. Self-taught. With no university degree, he wrote himself a study plan in accounting, law and statistics, and read annual reports cover to cover.
  4. A hobby fund becomes a giant. In 1993 he and a partner started Value Partners with under US$5 million.
  5. Graham, adapted for Asia. He calls himself an old-fashioned Graham-and-Dodd value investor, but puts more weight on cash flow and governance, which matter more in emerging markets.
  6. Two thousand five hundred visits. His firm's analysts make about 2,500 company visits a year and are told to spend little time in the office.
  7. Buy when others sell. He buys when people sell and sells when they buy, prefers dull shares, and stayed out of the dot-com mania.
  8. Stupid-clever. He says ego is an investor's worst enemy, and that about a third of his decisions are mistakes, mostly about people.
  9. Losing years, and the regulator. His fund lost money in seven of its first 25 years.
  10. The lucky unlucky boy. He credits luck and an era of fast growth as much as skill, and stepped back from the firm in 2025.

Buy or wait?

Would he buy, or wait? Tap your answer

  1. A sound company's shares have halved on bad news you think is overdone. You have visited and checked. Buy. Buying a sound business after an unfair markdown, having done the legwork, is the BYD pattern.
  2. Everyone wants a hot new sector, and prices have tripled in a year. Wait. He stayed out of the dot-com mania: the crowd's favourite is rarely cheap.
  3. A dull, cash-rich company trades cheaply, and nobody covers it. Buy. He prefers dull stocks when others chase exciting ones.
  4. The numbers look cheap, but you have doubts about whether the managers can be trusted. Wait. He says most of his mistakes came from misjudging management.

An honest note

On 5 October 2000 Hong Kong's Securities and Futures Commission publicly reprimanded Value Partners Limited and Cheah Cheng Hye, the person responsible for its investment decisions. It found that on 28, 29 and 31 December 1998 the firm had placed buy orders near the close of trading in five stocks, which left their closing prices higher than they might otherwise have been. The SFC found this unintentional but said he should have known it could affect prices, and cited inadequate controls. In January 2017 the SFC reprimanded two Value Partners companies and fined them HK$4 million in total because two funds had issued more shares than their constitutions allowed, and the firm reported this six months late; the SFC found no apparent investor losses, and he was not named. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Words to know

Value investing
Buying shares for less than the business is worth.
Graham and Dodd
Authors of Security Analysis (1934), the founding text of value investing.
Cash flow
Cash a business actually brings in, as opposed to accounting profit.
Corporate governance
How a company is controlled and whether minority shareholders are treated fairly.
Hang Seng Index
Hong Kong's main share index.
Public reprimand
A formal, published rebuke from a regulator.
Closing price
The last traded price of the day, used to value funds.

Related investors

Also from the wider world

  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • Francisco García Paramés. Taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch and Buffett's letters, then beat Spain's market for 21 years, survived his worst years, and kept going.
  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Peter Cundill. Read Graham on a plane in 1973, then hunted the world for shares below their liquidation value: the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.
  • Luiz Barsi Filho. Shined shoes in a São Paulo tenement, then built his own pension one dividend share at a time, and became Brazil's biggest individual investor.

From other countries

  • John Maynard Keynes. The century's most famous economist failed at forecasting markets, changed his method, and beat the UK market for a quarter of a century.
  • Radhakishan Damani. Dalal Street's famous bear turned patient investor, then built DMart from one Mumbai store on low costs, owned shops and fast payments.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
