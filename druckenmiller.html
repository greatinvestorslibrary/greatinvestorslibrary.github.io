His thinking in five minutes

Stanley Druckenmiller left a PhD to become a bank analyst in Pittsburgh, started his own fund at 28, and ran George Soros's Quantum Fund for 12 years. The bet against the British pound in 1992, reported to have made about $1 billion, was his idea. His own fund averaged about 30% a year for decades without a losing year, as reported. His trademark: watch the central banks, look 18 months ahead, and when you are sure, bet big.

  • About 30%: a year at his own fund from 1986, with no losing year, as reported
  • About $1bn: the reported profit on the 1992 bet against the pound, which was his idea
  • 18 months: how far ahead he tries to look, because the present is already in the price
  • 12 years: running George Soros's Quantum Fund, 1988 to 2000

His thinking, one line each

  • Look ahead. Picture the world 18 months from now; the present is in the price.
  • Follow the money. Central banks and liquidity move the whole market more than earnings.
  • How much, not how often. What counts is how much you make when right and lose when wrong.
  • Go for the jugular. When the case is strong, bet big; when it is not, bet little or nothing.
  • Change your mind. Cut losses fast when the facts change.
  • Know your streaks. Bet bigger when you are seeing the ball, smaller when you are not.

He never published an audited year-by-year record, and his funds were private. What we have are figures his firm and its investors gave the press, mainly when he closed his fund in 2010. We chart those, and say where the sources disagree.

The big ideas

  1. From a PhD to a bank in Pittsburgh. He studied English and economics at Bowdoin, started a PhD in economics, then left it in 1977 for a trainee job at Pittsburgh National Bank.
  2. Never invest in the present. His first mentor at the bank taught him to picture the world 18 months ahead.
  3. Liquidity moves markets. His mentor's second lesson: earnings do not move the overall market; the central bank does.
  4. Duquesne, 1981, and the 1987 crash. He founded his own firm, Duquesne Capital, at 28.
  5. Soros: how much, not how often. In 1988 he joined George Soros to run the Quantum Fund.
  6. Black Wednesday, 1992. In 1992 he concluded the British pound was overvalued inside Europe's exchange-rate system.
  7. Concentrate, and know when you are hot. He says diversification is overrated: when you really see something, put a lot behind it and watch it closely.
  8. 2000: buying the top. His most famous mistake: after selling technology shares in January 2000, he could not bear watching them rise, and bought back in at the very top in March.
  9. Thirty years without a losing year. When he closed Duquesne to outside money in 2010, it managed $12 billion and had averaged about 30% a year since 1986 without a losing year, as reported.
  10. Giving, forecasts and being wrong. He gives on a large scale, chairs the Harlem Children's Zone, and still speaks often about markets.

His trademark: the pound trade

Replay Black Wednesday

Step through 15 and 16 September 1992 as the Bank of England fought to hold the pound. Watch the interest rate it set, and why a bet against the pound had little to lose and much to gain.

  1. Tue 15 Sep, evening (Bank rate 10%): The head of Germany's central bank says Europe needs a wider realignment of currencies. Markets hear: the pound may be cut.
  2. Wed 16 Sep, morning (Bank rate 10%): Selling floods in. The Bank of England buys pounds, by mid-morning about £2 billion an hour.
  3. 11am (Bank rate 12%): Interest rates go from 10% to 12% to make holding pounds more attractive. The selling does not stop.
  4. Afternoon (announced, never applied: 15%): A second rise, to 15%, is announced. Still no rescue.
  5. 7.40pm (Bank rate 12%): Chancellor Norman Lamont says Britain is suspending its membership of the ERM. The rise to 15% is cancelled.
  6. Thu 17 Sep (Bank rate 10%): Rates fall back to 10%. Within a couple of days the pound is down about 15%.

Why the bet was one-sided: while Britain defended the pound's floor, it could not rise much, but if the floor broke it could fall a long way. Losing meant a small move; winning meant a big one. That shape, plus a strong case, is what Soros meant by going for the jugular.

Our timeline from Econlib and MoneyWeek; the 'one-sided' explanation is ours. Times are as reported; some accounts say 10.30am for the first rise and 7pm for the exit.

How much when right, how much when wrong?

His lesson from Soros: being right often matters less than the size of your wins and losses. Put in how often trades win and how much a win makes compared with a loss, and see the average result.

The arithmetic: average result per trade = (win rate × reward) − (loss rate × 1), in units of what you risk. At 5:1, if 20% of trades win, the wins bring 0.2 × 5 = 1.0 and the losses cost 0.8 × 1 = 0.8, so on average each trade adds 0.2 of what you risk, before costs. The break-even win rate is 1 ÷ (1 + reward): about 16.7% at 5:1. On the full site you can try your own numbers.

Simple arithmetic for illustration: it ignores costs, taxes and the fact that real wins and losses vary. It is not a trading system.

Fed or earnings? Tap your answer

  1. A company's profits beat forecasts, but the central bank is draining money from the system. For the market as a whole, which does he weigh more? The central bank. Earnings don't move the overall market, he says; the central bank and liquidity do.
  2. The economy is dull and slow, and the central bank is cutting rates to get it moving. Often good for shares. He has said some of the best times for shares come in a slow economy the central bank is trying to revive.
  3. Everyone agrees a company is wonderful; its profits are at a record today. Already in the price. Never invest in the present: picture where things will be in about 18 months.
  4. You bought something in a rush because it kept rising without you. His mistake. That is how he describes buying technology shares at the top in March 2000.

An honest note

His record is impressive but unaudited: the 30% average and the 'no losing year' come from his firm and its investors, as told to the press, and sources disagree on some years (about 11% or about 3% for 2008). The famous 1992 trade was run inside George Soros's fund, and credit is shared; Soros made the call to bet so big. His biggest public mistake, buying technology shares at the top in March 2000, helped push the Quantum Fund down 22% that year before he left. Some forecasts since then have also missed, such as the recession he expected in 2023. His style relied on borrowed money and very large positions, which can lose as fast as they win. We found no regulatory action against him in our searches. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Nothing on this page is a recommendation to buy, sell or trade anything, and none of it is a trading system. Performance figures are as reported by the press and his firm, and are not audited by us.

Words to know

Global macro
Trading on big economic shifts across currencies, bonds, shares and commodities.
Liquidity
How much money is flowing through the financial system, much of it set by central banks.
Central bank
The bank that sets a country's interest rates and money supply, such as the Federal Reserve or the Bank of England.
Short selling
Selling something you do not own, hoping to buy it back cheaper.
Leverage
Using borrowed money so gains and losses are larger than your own money alone.
ERM
Europe's Exchange Rate Mechanism, which kept member currencies within set bands against each other.
Devaluation
A cut in a currency's official value against others.
Family office
A firm that manages one family's own money, not outside investors'.
