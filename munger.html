His thinking in five minutes

Charlie Munger was Buffett's partner and Berkshire's vice chairman from 1978 until his death in November 2023, at 99. He trained as a lawyer, and his talks are as much about how to think and live as about money.

  • Before you decide. Run the checklist of models. Invert: how would this fail? Which tendencies are acting on you right now?
  • When you invest. Few, great, mispriced. Bet heavily when the odds are clear. Then sit, and keep costs and taxes low.
  • With other people. Look at incentives first. Be reliable. Deserve what you want. Choose people you admire.
  • Inside your head. Refuse envy, resentment, revenge and self-pity. Hunt for evidence against your own views.
  • Every day. Learn something. Go to bed a little wiser. Keep the habit for life.

The big ideas

  1. A latticework of models. Isolated facts do not stick and do not help.
  2. Invert, always invert. Many problems that resist a direct attack give way when turned round.
  3. Incentives. To predict what people will do, look first at what they are rewarded for.
  4. The psychology of misjudgment. He built a list of 25 mental tendencies that lead sensible people into error, and used it as a checklist.
  5. The inner life: envy, resentment, self-pity. Much of his life advice is about thoughts to refuse.
  6. Reliability, trust and deserving. He thought most of what people want in life is earned by being someone others can rely on.
  7. Learning for life. He saw lifelong learning as a duty, and as the main reason ordinary people overtake cleverer ones over a working life.
  8. Objectivity and the edge of what you know. Knowing the limits of your competence is worth more than its size.
  9. Investing: few, great, and then sit. The big money comes from a handful of great businesses, bought when the odds are clearly favourable, then held for a long time.
  10. Business models, moats and scale. He studied why some businesses compound for a century while others never earn their keep, and taught it through plain examples.
  11. Costs, consultants and febezzlement. Fees and frictional costs look small each year and become enormous over time.
  12. Accounting, honesty and candour. He wrote as a lawyer who distrusted flattering numbers.
  13. Adversity, patience and hard work. Setbacks are certain, and many are unfair.
  14. The Berkshire system, in his words. In his 2014 essay, written directly to Berkshire shareholders for the first time, he explained why the company had worked.

Twenty-five ways the mind misleads us

  1. Reward- and punishment-superresponse. Incentives move people more than they expect. Guard: Get incentives right; distrust paid advice.
  2. Liking/loving. We favour those we like and overlook their faults. Guard: Admire the right people; see them clearly.
  3. Disliking/hating. We ignore the merits of those we dislike. Guard: Judge disliked rivals fairly.
  4. Doubt-avoidance. We rush to any answer to end uncertainty. Guard: Slow down; hear both sides.
  5. Inconsistency-avoidance. We cling to past conclusions and habits. Guard: Seek evidence against yourself; stop bad habits early.
  6. Curiosity. The urge to know, mostly a help. Guard: Feed it for life.
  7. Kantian fairness. We expect fair treatment and resent its absence. Guard: Design arrangements that feel fair.
  8. Envy/jealousy. Others' gains provoke conflict. Guard: Name it in yourself; allow for it in others.
  9. Reciprocation. We return favours and hostility automatically. Guard: Delay angry replies; refuse gifts that buy judgment.
  10. Influence-from-mere-association. We credit or blame whatever sits near an outcome. Guard: Check past wins for luck; welcome bad news.
  11. Simple, pain-avoiding psychological denial. We bend facts that hurt. Guard: Face reality; avoid habits that feed denial.
  12. Excessive self-regard. We overrate ourselves and what is ours. Guard: Trust records over impressions.
  13. Overoptimism. We expect better than the odds. Guard: Use simple probability.
  14. Deprival-superreaction. Losses and near misses hurt far more than gains please. Guard: Watch for irrational fights over small losses.
  15. Social-proof. We copy others, most of all when confused. Guard: Be wary when everyone agrees and you are rushed.
  16. Contrast-misreaction. We judge by nearby comparisons. Guard: Measure against absolute worth.
  17. Stress-influence. Heavy stress distorts thinking. Guard: Decide big things when calm.
  18. Availability-misweighing. Vivid, recent facts get too much weight. Guard: Use a checklist; seek the dull, large facts.
  19. Use-it-or-lose-it. Unpractised skills fade. Guard: Practise to fluency.
  20. Drug-misinfluence. Chemicals distort judgment. Guard: Avoid them.
  21. Senescence-misinfluence. Age makes new complex skills harder. Guard: Keep learning; learn hard things early.
  22. Authority-misinfluence. We obey authority even when it is wrong. Guard: Choose leaders carefully; question orders.
  23. Twaddle. Empty talk crowds out real work. Guard: Keep it away from serious tasks.
  24. Reason-respecting. We comply when given reasons, even weak ones. Guard: Give real reasons; test the reasons you are given.
  25. Lollapalooza. Several tendencies acting together cause extreme results. Guard: Look for confluences, the source of most disasters.

In his own words

“To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail.”USC 1994
“Broadscale problems, by definition, cross many academic disciplines.”HLS 1998
“Invert, always invert.”HS 1986, quoting Jacobi
“Incentives are superpowers.”PHM 2005
“Generally speaking, envy, resentment, revenge, and self-pity are disastrous modes of thought.”USC Law 2007
“Don't sell anything you wouldn't buy yourself.”Stanford 1996
“The answer is I have a black belt in chutzpah.”UCSB 2003, explaining why, with no economics training, he would lecture economists

Read the originals

The talks are free to read in the publisher's online edition of Poor Charlie's Almanack. The letters link to published copies.

  • Letter to Blue Chip Stamps stockholders for 1982, signed by Munger as chairman, dated 17 February 1983. Writing to owners candidly, and how inflation sorts businesses.
  • Letter to Wesco Financial shareholders for 1985, dated 13 February 1986. A frank verdict on his own savings and loan business.
  • Harvard School commencement, Los Angeles, 13 June 1986. Prescriptions for a miserable life, and the habit of inverting problems.
  • A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom. The latticework of models, great businesses, and few big bets.
  • Worldly Wisdom, Revisited, with questions from students. Knowing what you know, three rules for a career, bearing hard times.
  • Practical Thought about Practical Thought?. The Coca-Cola thought experiment and his five helpful notions.
  • Letter to Wesco Financial shareholders for 1996. Super-cat reinsurance, lumpy results and conservative accounting.
  • Fiftieth reunion of the Harvard Law School class of 1948, 24 April 1998. Why professionals need the big ideas of many fields, taught as pilots are.
  • Investment Practices of Leading Charitable Foundations. Layers of fees, indexing and concentration.
  • Breakfast meeting of the Philanthropy Roundtable, 10 November 2000. Febezzlement, crowd folly and wealth effects.
  • The Great Financial Scandal of 2003. How a sound company is ruined by accounting games.
  • Academic Economics. Physics envy, counting what can be counted, second-order effects.
  • The Psychology of Human Misjudgment. The 25 tendencies that lead sensible people into error.
  • Letter to Wesco Financial shareholders for 2006, dated 27 February 2007. Plain talk about a weaker vehicle, and ignoring windfalls.
  • USC Gould School of Law commencement, 13 May 2007. His fullest statement on how to live.
  • Vice Chairman's Thoughts, Past and Future. What made Berkshire work, in his own account.
  • Poor Charlie's Almanack: the editor's chapters. How he decided, in the book's own account.

Test yourself

  1. Explain the latticework idea to a teenager, with one example.
  2. Use inversion on a real problem: list how to fail, then the plan.
  3. Name five of the 25 tendencies and give a recent example of each.
  4. What is lollapalooza, and why is it the most dangerous tendency?
  5. Which four modes of thought did he call disastrous, and why?
  6. State his three rules for a career.
  7. Why do learning machines overtake cleverer people?
  8. What is the twenty-slot ticket, and what does it change?
  9. Why do improvements in a commodity business often fail to help its owners?
  10. What is febezzlement? Give an example from your own costs.
  11. What made Berkshire's system work, in his four causes?
  12. Write one page, in his blunt style, of advice to your younger self.

Words to know

Worldly wisdom
Practical judgment built from the big ideas of many fields, used together on real problems.
Latticework of models
The connected set of those big ideas you think with. Facts are hung on it so they can be used.
Man with a hammer
The error of forcing every problem into the one model you know.
Inversion
Solving a problem backwards: ask what would cause failure, then avoid it.
Circle of competence
The area where you really know enough to judge. Knowing its edge matters more than its size.
Incentive-caused bias
Drifting towards conclusions that serve your own pay, often without noticing.
Lollapalooza
An extreme result produced when several psychological forces push the same way at once.
Too tough to understand
His third basket for ideas, beside yes and no. You never have to decide on those.
Opportunity cost
Judging any use of money against the next-best use available.
Febezzle
His word for wealth quietly lost to wasteful investment fees, modelled on Galbraith's 'bezzle'.
