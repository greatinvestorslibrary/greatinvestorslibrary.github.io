His thinking in five minutes

Li Lu reached the United States in 1989 with little money and less English, heard Warren Buffett speak at Columbia in 1993, and founded Himalaya Capital in 1997. Charlie Munger later called him the only outside manager he had ever invested with. His method: treat a share as part of a business, know exactly where your knowledge ends, buy with a margin of safety, and wait for the rare great bet.

  • 29%+ a year: his fund, January 1998 to 2009, after all expenses, in his own words (not independently audited)
  • 36% a year: the same fund from late 2004, when Munger joined, to 2009
  • −19%: his first year, on paper; a large investor left the following year
  • 10% of BYD: bought by Berkshire for about $230m in 2008, after Li Lu brought the company to Munger

His thinking, one line each

  • A share is part of a business. Returns come from the business growing in value, not from guessing prices.
  • The market serves you. Use its prices when they suit you; never let it tell you what something is worth.
  • Margin of safety. The future is uncertain, so buy well below what you think a business is worth.
  • Know where your circle ends. A small area you truly understand, with a clear edge, beats a large hazy one.
  • Fish where the fish are. Look in less crowded markets, industries and countries.
  • Own a share of the world. Real wealth is your slice of the world's buying power, held in great businesses for a long time.

The big ideas

  1. A share is a piece of a business. Value investing rests on four ideas.
  2. Know where your circle ends. Your circle of competence should be small and clearly drawn.
  3. The market serves you. Prices are there to use when they suit you.
  4. Margin of safety. Because the future is uncertain, buy at a price far below your cautious estimate of value.
  5. Research like an owner. Study a company as if you had just inherited it and had to run it.
  6. Why he gave up short selling. He calls short selling one of his worst mistakes.
  7. Patient, then decisive. Great opportunities are rare.
  8. A different road from Wall Street. Munger told him his problems were Wall Street's problems.
  9. Own a slice of the world. Real wealth is your share of the world's buying power.
  10. Mistakes, luck and compounding knowledge. He had a bad first year, sold winners too early and gave up shorting the hard way.

Before you buy: his ideas as questions

Before you buy: eight questions

  1. I can say what the business does and how it earns its money
  2. I would hold it even if no price were quoted for years
  3. The price is well below my cautious estimate of its value
  4. I know where my knowledge of it ends, and what would prove me wrong
  5. I have learned about it from people who work there, buy from it or compete with it
  6. Its managers think and act like owners
  7. I am buying to own it, not betting against anything
  8. Few other investors are looking at it closely

Our summary of his talks, as a study aid, not a recommendation.

His six principles, as he put them in 2026

  1. A share is ownership of part of a real business.
  2. The market serves you it does not instruct you.
  3. Margin of safety because the future cannot be predicted.
  4. Circle of competence and above all, knowing where it ends.
  5. Fish where the fish are in less crowded markets, industries and countries.
  6. Wealth is a share of the world's buying power held through great businesses in growing economies, for a long time.

The first three come from Benjamin Graham, the fourth from Buffett, the fifth from Munger; the sixth is his own.

Li Lu was a student leader in the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests. We mention it only as part of his life story; this site takes no political position.

Words to know

Value investing
Buying a share for less than the business is worth, and holding it while the business grows.
Circle of competence
The area where you genuinely understand businesses well enough to value them.
Margin of safety
The gap between a cautious estimate of value and the price you pay.
Mr Market
Benjamin Graham's picture of the market as a moody partner offering prices you may take or ignore.
Short selling
Borrowing shares to sell them, hoping to buy them back later at a lower price.
Long-only fund
A fund that only owns shares and never bets on prices falling.
Lock-up
A period during which investors agree not to withdraw their money.
Intrinsic value
What a business is really worth, judged from the cash it will produce.
Fiduciary
Someone trusted to act in another person's interest, before their own.
