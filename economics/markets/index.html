How Markets Work

Why lemonade costs more on a hot day, why two friends both win by trading, why a rival stand cuts your profit, why whole economies boom and bust, and why the stock market sometimes acts like a moody neighbour.

Chapter 1

Supply and demand 🍋

A market price is where the number of glasses people want to buy meets the number sellers want to sell.

Demand: the cheaper the lemonade, the more glasses people want. Supply: the higher the price, the more glasses Sunny is willing to squeeze, because each extra glass is worth the effort. Drag the price and watch what happens.

Set the price of a glass

Made-up numbers for teaching. Blue line: how many glasses people want at each price. Gold line: how many Sunny will make.

⬆️Price too highGlasses are left over. To sell them, Sunny cuts the price.
⬇️Price too lowA queue forms and Sunny runs out. Sunny can raise the price and still sell out.
☀️Demand shiftsA heatwave means more people want lemonade at every price. The price and the number sold both rise.
🥀Supply shiftsA bad lemon crop makes each glass dearer to make. The price rises but fewer glasses are sold.

Real life: tomatoes in 2023. NABARD, India's farm-development bank, reported that retail tomato prices roughly tripled in July 2023, from about ₹30 a kilo in June to ₹109 by the end of July. July and August are always a lean season for tomatoes, and that year heat and heavy rain also damaged crops. On 2 August the government's own price tracker showed ₹203 a kilo in Delhi. That is a supply shift: same appetite for tomatoes, far fewer tomatoes.

Opportunity cost and why trade helps

The real cost of anything is the best thing you gave up to get it.

If Sunny spends Saturday at the cinema, the cost is not just the ticket. It is also the lemonade profit Sunny would have made that afternoon. Economists call that the opportunity cost.

Opportunity cost explains one of the most surprising ideas in economics. In one hour, Sunny can make 20 glasses or 10 cookies. Pip can make 6 glasses or 6 cookies. Sunny is better at both. Should Sunny do everything?

For Sunny, 1 cookie costs2 glasses(the 2 glasses Sunny could have made instead)
For Pip, 1 cookie costs1 glassSo Pip is the cheaper cookie maker

Plan the day: 8 hours each

Without a plan, each spends 4 hours on lemonade and 4 on cookies: 104 glasses and 64 cookies in all. Can you beat that on both?

Glasses
Cookies
Against no plan

This is comparative advantage. Each person should do the job where their opportunity cost is lowest, then trade. The economist David Ricardo explained it in 1817 with England, Portugal, cloth and wine: even if one country is better at making everything, both can end up with more by specialising and trading. It is why India sells software services and buys oil, and why most of us buy our bread instead of baking it.

Competition and moats 🏰

Rivals push prices down towards cost. A "moat" is whatever stops them.

Each glass costs Sunny ₹5 in lemons, sugar and cups, plus ₹100 a day for the stall. Charge too little and there is no margin; charge too much and customers walk away. Find the most profitable price, then see what a rival does to it.

🎮 Pick your price

₹10Glasses –Profit a day –

A made-up model for teaching: people buy 200 − 10 × price glasses a day; with a rival, your share depends on how your price compares with theirs.

Warren Buffett calls a lasting advantage a moat, like the water around a castle: a brand people trust, a secret recipe, the best spot on the street, or customers who find it a pain to switch. In the game, the secret recipe lets Sunny charge more than the rival and still keep customers. Investors look for moats because without one, competition slowly eats the profit away. Chapter 4

Booms and busts

Economies do not grow in a straight line. They speed up, slow down and sometimes shrink.

The business cycle

In a boom, shops are busy, firms hire and borrow, and prices tend to rise. At the peak things overheat. In a recession, the economy produces less than the year before: sales fall, firms cut jobs and people spend less. Then comes the trough, and the recovery. A common rule of thumb calls two quarters in a row of shrinking output a recession. In America, a committee of economists makes the official call.

Chapter 5

The stock market and Mr. Market 📈

A share is a small slice of a business. Its price is set, second by second, by supply and demand.

Suppose Sunny's stand makes about ₹1,000 profit a year, and a sensible price for the whole stand is about ₹10,000. If Sunny sold it on a stock market, the price would move every day with the mood of buyers and sellers, even when nothing about the stand had changed.

Benjamin Graham, Buffett's teacher, described the market as a business partner called Mr. Market. Every day he offers to buy your share or sell you his. Some days he is gloomy and offers a silly low price; some days he is excited and offers a silly high one. You do not have to trade with him. You can wait for a good offer. See Graham's page.

🎮 Deal with Mr. Market

The stand is worth about ₹10,000. Buy when he is cheap, sell when he is dear, otherwise wait.

🙂

When the mood goes too far: bubbles

A bubble is when prices rise far above what the businesses are worth, because people buy simply because prices are rising. When the mood turns, prices can fall a long way.

💻The dot-com bubbleAmerica's tech-heavy Nasdaq index closed at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 and fell to 1,114.11 by October 2002. It took until April 2015 to close at a new high.
🏦The 2008 crashIndia's Sensex closed at 20,873 on 8 January 2008 and fell to about 8,160 in March 2009. It closed above 21,000 for the first time in November 2010.

Graham's lesson: over short periods the market behaves like a vote on what is popular; over long periods it works more like a scale that weighs what businesses actually earn. The great investors in the library mostly got rich by keeping calm when Mr. Market did not.

    Questions people ask

    What is supply and demand, in simple words?

    Demand is how much people want to buy at each price; supply is how much sellers want to sell. The market price settles where the two are equal. If the price is too high, goods are left over; too low, and there is a queue.

    What is opportunity cost?

    The best thing you give up when you choose something. An hour spent baking cookies costs you whatever else you could have made or done in that hour.

    What is comparative advantage?

    Being able to do something at a lower opportunity cost than someone else. David Ricardo showed in 1817 that two countries can both gain by specialising and trading, even if one is better at making everything.

    What is a moat in investing?

    A lasting advantage, such as a trusted brand or a cost edge, that stops rivals from competing away a company's profits. Warren Buffett made the term popular.

    What is a recession?

    A period when an economy produces less than before. India's economy shrank in 1979 and 2020; America's in six years between 1975 and 2025, including 2009 and 2020.

    Who is Mr. Market?

    A character invented by the investor Benjamin Graham. He stands for the stock market's mood swings: some days he offers silly low prices, some days silly high ones, and you are free to ignore him.
