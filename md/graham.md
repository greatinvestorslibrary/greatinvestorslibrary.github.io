# Benjamin Graham

> Taught Buffett, wrote the two founding books of value investing, and turned a near-ruinous 1929 into rules for never being ruined again.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/graham.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Investing, not speculating. An investment protects your money and earns a fair return. Anything else is speculation.

## Summary

Benjamin Graham taught Warren Buffett, wrote Security Analysis (1934) and The Intelligent Investor (1949), and is called the father of value investing. The 1929 crash nearly ruined him, and he spent the rest of his life working out how an ordinary person could invest without being ruined. His answers: know the difference between investing and speculating, treat the market as a moody partner, and always insist on a margin of safety.

## Record at a glance

- **−70%**: his account, 1929 to 1932, against −74% for the Dow: the crash nearly wiped him out
- **17.4% a year**: Graham-Newman, 1945 to 1956, leaving out GEICO; the S&P 500 did 18.3%, but with far bigger swings
- **≈ $0.7m**: paid in 1948 for half of GEICO ($712,500 to $720,000, by source); it outgrew everything else he owned
- **1934**: Security Analysis, with David Dodd; The Intelligent Investor followed in 1949

## Big ideas

### 1. Investing or speculating?

An investment, after careful study, protects your money and promises a fair return. Anything else is speculation, and you should know which you are doing.

- **His definition.** After thorough analysis, an investment promises safety of the money you put in and an adequate return. Whatever fails that test is speculative. (Book, 1934)
- **Two faces of a share.** A share has one investment side, the business growing over decades, and one speculative side, prices swinging with hope, fear and greed. (Interview, 1976)
- **Speculation isn't a crime.** He did not forbid speculation; he asked that you do it knowingly, with money you can afford to lose, and keep it apart from your investments. (Book, 1973)
- **Your worst enemy.** The investor's main problem is usually himself: his moods, not the market. (Book, 1973)

### 2. Mr Market

Picture a partner who offers every day to buy your share or sell you his, at a price that depends on his mood. You may take his offer or ignore it.

- **The parable.** Mr Market turns up daily with a price. Some days he is euphoric and asks too much; some days gloomy and sells too cheap. (Book, 1973 · Buffett, 1987)
- **Ignore him freely.** You are never obliged to trade. His offers are there to use when they suit you. (Buffett, 1987 · Book, 1973)
- **One meaning only.** For a true investor, price swings mean one thing: a chance to buy wisely after sharp falls and sell wisely after big rises. Otherwise, forget the market. (Book, 1973)
- **Buffett's debt.** Buffett wrote that this picture of Mr Market describes the attitude to price swings he finds most useful for investing. (Buffett, 1987)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-02-mr-market.html

### 3. Margin of safety

Pay well below what something is worth. The gap protects you from your own mistakes, bad luck and the unknown.

- **The central idea.** He called the margin of safety the central concept of investment, the closing chapter of his book. (Book, 1973)
- **Every purchase justified.** In his last interview: every purchase, and the price paid, should be justified by a margin of safety in value terms. (Interview, 1976)
- **Dollars for 40 cents.** Buffett summed up Graham's school as buying a dollar of value for 40 cents, then waiting. (Buffett, 1984)
- **Safety in numbers.** A margin of safety works on average, not every time, which is why he spread his money across many holdings. (Book, 1973)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-03-margin-of-safety.html

### 4. Know which investor you are

He split investors in two. Defensive investors want safety and little effort; enterprising ones will put in time and skill. Mixing the two is the danger.

- **Defensive.** A defensive investor wants freedom from bother and serious mistakes. Simple rules and a balanced portfolio suit him. (Book, 1973)
- **Enterprising.** An enterprising investor is willing to spend real time and care to earn more than average, and must have the knowledge to do it. (Book, 1973)
- **Effort, not courage.** The difference is the work you will do, not how much risk you will take. (Book, 1973)
- **Good enough is easy.** A sound, if unspectacular, result needs little effort. Doing better than that takes much more work and some wisdom. (Book, 1973)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-04-know-which-investor-you-are.html

### 5. Seven tests for the defensive investor

For investors who want safety, he set seven plain tests: size, strong finances, steady earnings, dividends, growth, and moderate prices against earnings and assets.

- **Size and strength.** A large company, with current assets at least twice current liabilities and long-term debt no larger than working capital. (Book, 1973)
- **Record.** Earnings in each of the past ten years, dividends paid without a break for twenty, and earnings up at least a third over ten years. (Book, 1973)
- **Price.** No more than 15 times average earnings of the past three years, and no more than 1.5 times book value, or a product of the two no higher than 22.5. (Book, 1973)
- **Strict on purpose.** Few companies pass all seven. The tests are meant to keep a defensive investor away from trouble, not to find the next great growth story. (Book, 1973)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-05-seven-tests-for-the-defensive-investor.html

### 6. Buying below working capital

His favourite bargain: shares selling for less than the company's current assets minus all its debts, giving nothing at all for factories or brands.

- **The method.** Buy shares priced below net current asset value: current assets minus every liability, with fixed assets counted as zero. (Interview, 1976)
- **His own result.** He said this method had earned about 20% a year over more than 30 years. (Interview, 1976)
- **They come and go.** Such bargains grew scarce in the boom years, then returned after the 1973 to 1974 crash: in January 1976 he counted over 300, about a tenth of listed shares. (Interview, 1976)
- **Buy a group.** He applied it to a spread of such shares, not one or two, so that the winners outweighed the disappointments. (Book, 1973)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-06-buying-below-working-capital.html

### 7. Simple rules, many stocks

Late in life he decided that detailed analysis rarely paid any more. Better to buy groups of cheap shares by simple rules, and to sell by rules too.

- **Analysis no longer pays.** Elaborate research rewarded investors forty years earlier; by 1976 he thought it rarely justified its cost. (Interview, 1976)
- **A second simple rule.** Buy groups of shares at no more than seven times the past year's earnings. He said tests of this kind showed 15% a year or better from 1925 to 1975. (Interview, 1976)
- **A rule for selling.** Set a profit target of 50% to 100%, and sell anything that has not reached it within two or three years. (Interview, 1976)
- **The small investor's edge.** Institutions are limited to some 300 to 400 huge companies; an individual can choose from about 3,000. (Interview, 1976)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-07-simple-rules-many-stocks.html

### 8. Stocks and bonds: the 25–75 rule

Keep at least a quarter of your money in shares and at least a quarter in bonds, always. Rebalance as markets move.

- **Floors on both sides.** Hold no less than 25% in shares and no less than 25% in bonds or their equivalent; a 50–50 split is a sensible default. (Interview, 1976 · Book, 1973)
- **Rebalance.** After a big rise in shares, move some into bonds; after a fall, do the reverse. (Interview, 1976)
- **Bonds of moderate length.** He suggested bond holdings averaging seven or eight years to maturity. (Interview, 1976)
- **A rule against moods.** A fixed rule stops you buying more just because prices rose and selling just because they fell. (Book, 1973)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-08-stocks-and-bonds-the-25-75-rule.html

### 9. The market catches up with value

Prices can stray far from value for a long time. But, as he told the Senate, the market eventually catches up.

- **A mystery, he admitted.** Asked by a senator how a cheap share ever reaches its value, he called it one of the mysteries of the business, but said experience shows it does. (Senate, 1955)
- **His students.** In 1984 Buffett showed nine investors from Graham's school beating the market over long periods with different stocks. (Buffett, 1984)
- **Not luck.** Buffett's point: if the winners all come from one small village of thinking, the method matters, not chance. (Buffett, 1984)
- **Index-level results.** Graham doubted most professionals could beat the averages, and thought clients should expect roughly index results. (Interview, 1976)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/graham-09-the-market-catches-up-with-value.html

### 10. Loss, luck and generosity

The crash taught him caution, GEICO taught him how much luck matters, and his friends remembered his generosity.

- **The crash.** From 1929 to 1932 his account fell about 70%. He blamed his own borrowing, not the crash. (Study, 1977)
- **The measured record.** From 1945 to 1956, leaving out GEICO, Graham-Newman earned 17.4% a year against 18.3% for the S&P 500, with far smaller swings. (Study, 1977)
- **One lucky decision.** GEICO, bought against his own rule of wide diversification, made more than all his other decisions together. He drew the lesson himself in his book. (Book, 1973 · Study, 1977)
- **A generous man.** He quietly helped refugees from Nazi Germany, read Greek and Latin, and translated a Spanish novel into English. (Study, 1977)

## In their own words (short quotes)

- “promises safety of principal and an adequate return” (Security Analysis, 1934, defining an investment)
- “The investor's chief problem—and even his worst enemy—is likely to be himself.” (The Intelligent Investor, 1973)
- “a margin of safety, in value terms, to protect his commitment” (Financial Analysts Journal interview, 1976)
- “We know from experience that eventually the market catches up with value.” (Testimony to the US Senate, 1955)

## Read the originals

Start with chapters 8 and 20 of The Intelligent Investor, on Mr Market and the margin of safety. Then read his short 1976 interview, free online.

- [Security Analysis](https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/bengrahaminvesting/resources/ben-graham-collection/): The textbook that founded the profession: how to study a company's accounts, and his definition of an investment.
- [The Intelligent Investor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Intelligent_Investor): His book for ordinary investors: investment versus speculation, Mr Market, the defensive and enterprising investor, and the margin of safety.
- [Testimony on the stock market](https://novelinvestor.com/graham-mysteries-stock-market/): Asked why the market was booming, he explained why prices wander from value, and why value wins in the end.

## Words to know

- **Speculation**: Buying in the hope that the price will rise, without a margin of safety in value.
- **Margin of safety**: The gap between a cautious estimate of value and the price you pay.
- **Mr Market**: Graham's picture of the market as a moody partner offering prices you may take or ignore.
- **Current ratio**: Current assets divided by current liabilities.
- **Working capital**: Current assets minus current liabilities.
- **Net current asset value**: Current assets minus all liabilities, with nothing for factories or brands.
- **Book value**: A company's assets minus its liabilities, as shown in its accounts.
- **Price to earnings (P/E)**: The share price divided by the profit per share.
- **Rebalancing**: Selling some of what has risen and buying what has fallen, to return to a set mix.
