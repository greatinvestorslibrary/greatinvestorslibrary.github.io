# Terry Smith

> Exposed creative accounting and lost his job for it, then built Fundsmith on three rules: buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/smith.html · Section: the United Kingdom · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Buy good companies. Companies that earn high returns on their capital, in cash, year after year, without much debt.

## Summary

Terry Smith exposed creative accounting in 1992 and was sacked for refusing to withdraw his book. In 2010 he founded Fundsmith on three rules: buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing. Since launch it has returned 13.8% a year against 12.1% for world shares, though it trailed the market every year from 2021 to 2025.

## Record at a glance

- **13.8% a year**: Fundsmith Equity, Nov 2010 to Dec 2025
- **12.1% a year**: world shares (MSCI World) over the same years
- **+613%**: total gain since launch, against +468% for world shares
- **+0.8%**: in 2025: his weakest year, and the fifth in a row behind the index

## Big ideas

### 1. Buy good companies

A good company earns a high return on the money tied up in it, in cash, and keeps doing so for decades.

- **Return on capital is the test.** A good business makes a high return on the capital it uses, in cash, and can sustain it. (Owner's Manual)
- **Hard to copy.** Its advantages should be difficult for rivals to replicate. (Owner's Manual)
- **Little debt needed.** It should not need heavy borrowing to earn its returns. (Owner's Manual)
- **Room to reinvest.** It should be able to reinvest its cash at high rates, so growth compounds. (Owner's Manual)
- **Built to last.** It should be resilient to change, especially technological change. (Owner's Manual)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-01-buy-good-companies.html

### 2. The numbers that show quality

Every year he publishes the same few measures for his companies beside the market's. Quality shows up in the numbers.

- **Returns.** In 2025 his companies averaged a 31% return on capital, against 17% for both the S&P 500 and the FTSE 100. (Annual letters)
- **Margins.** Gross margin 62% against 45% and 43%; operating margin 28% against 18% and 17%. (Annual letters)
- **Cash.** They turned 94% of their profits into cash. (Annual letters)
- **Safety.** Median interest cover was 29 times, against 9 and 8: profits dwarf interest bills. (Annual letters)
- **Durability.** The average company in the fund was founded in 1919. (Annual letters)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-02-the-numbers-that-show-quality.html

### 3. Don't overpay

Even the best company can be a bad investment at the wrong price. He checks how much cash you get for what you pay.

- **Cash for your money.** He tracks free cash flow yield, the cash a company produces against its price. At the end of 2025 his portfolio's was 3.7%, against 2.8% for the S&P 500. (Annual letters)
- **No greater fools.** Buy only what you would happily own at a price you accept. Profits that depend on someone paying more later rely on a greater fool. (FT article, 2013)
- **Quality earns its price.** He argues that the market tends to underestimate how long great companies keep compounding, so paying up for them can still work. (Book, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-03-dont-overpay.html

### 4. Do nothing

Once you own good companies at sensible prices, the hardest and best thing is to leave them alone.

- **Deal rarely.** Frequent trading adds costs that steadily eat into returns. (FT article, 2013)
- **Low turnover.** In 2025 the fund's turnover was 12.7%, and voluntary dealing cost 0.008% of the fund. (Annual letters)
- **Don't time the market.** Most people buy after rises and sell after falls. Guessing the turns rarely works. (FT article, 2013)
- **Switch off the screen.** If you own good companies at sensible prices and dislike what the price is doing, stop looking. (FT article, 2013)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-04-do-nothing.html

### 5. Read the accounts

His first fame came from showing how companies dressed up their profits. Reported profit can mislead; cash is harder to fake.

- **Profits that weren't.** In 1990 several big British companies went bust while reporting healthy profits. Their real problem was cash. (Book, 1992)
- **The book that cost his job.** In 1992 he expanded his research into a book. His employer, UBS Phillips & Drew, asked him to withdraw it; he refused and was sacked. (Book, 1992)
- **The usual tricks.** Acquisition accounting, deferred payments, exceptional items, off-balance-sheet debt, hidden liabilities, brand values, changed depreciation, capitalised costs and currency mismatches. (Book, 1992)
- **It changed the rules.** Many of the practices he exposed were later curbed or stopped. (Book, 1992)
- **Habits stick.** Managers who flatter their earnings rarely change, so treat it as a lasting warning sign. (Book, 1992)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-05-read-the-accounts.html

### 6. You are the biggest risk

Most damage to investors' returns is self-inflicted: chasing fashion, trading too much, buying what they don't understand.

- **Look in the mirror.** His Owner's Manual warns that the greatest threat to your returns is your own behaviour. (Owner's Manual)
- **Not because it's fashionable.** The fund will not own something because it is in fashion and might go up, because eventually it goes down, usually by a lot. (Owner's Manual)
- **Understand it or skip it.** If you can't explain how an investment works, don't buy it. Complexity often hides risk. (FT article, 2013)
- **Tax is not a reason.** Never invest just to avoid tax. Tax-driven schemes often carry high fees and weak returns. (FT article, 2013)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-06-you-are-the-biggest-risk.html

### 7. Costs come out of your pocket

Fees, charges and dealing costs are certain; returns are not. Keep the certain losses small.

- **Minimise fees.** Charges from advisers, platforms and funds can swallow most of an investment's income. (FT article, 2013)
- **Show the full bill.** Fundsmith reports a total cost of investment that includes dealing costs: 1.06% a year for its main share class in 2025. (Annual letters)
- **Small numbers compound.** A cost of 1% a year sounds small, but over decades it takes a large share of the final pot. (Book, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-07-costs-come-out-of-your-pocket.html

### 8. Don't over-diversify

Twenty or so well-chosen companies give most of the safety of spreading out. More than that, and you know each one less well.

- **About twenty is enough.** Around 20 well-chosen shares capture most of the benefit of diversification. (FT article, 2013)
- **A concentrated fund.** At the end of 2025 Fundsmith Equity held 29 companies. (Annual letters)
- **The 80th company.** His Owner's Manual asks how much a manager can really know about the 80th company in a portfolio. (Owner's Manual)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-08-dont-over-diversify.html

### 9. Avoid poor businesses and star bosses

Stay away from companies that earn poor returns, and from companies that only work with a genius in charge.

- **Never own poor quality.** Companies that earn weak returns on their capital destroy value over time; strong ones compound it. (FT article, 2013)
- **A business an idiot could run.** Avoid companies that depend on one brilliant or charismatic leader who may not stay. (FT article, 2013)
- **Everyday, repeated sales.** He favours businesses built on many small, predictable purchases, such as household goods and medical supplies. (Owner's Manual)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/smith-09-avoid-poor-businesses-and-star-bosses.html

### 10. When it doesn't work

From 2021 to 2025 his fund trailed the market five years running. How he explained it is a lesson in honesty and patience.

- **Five years behind.** The fund lagged world shares in every calendar year from 2021 to 2025. (Annual letters)
- **A weak 2025.** It returned 0.8% while world shares rose 12.8%; even cash did better. (Annual letters)
- **His explanation.** A market led by a handful of giant technology shares, money pouring into index funds, and a weak dollar. His biggest single drag was Novo Nordisk, which cost the fund about 3 points. (Annual letters)
- **Still ahead since launch.** Over 15 years the fund remains ahead: 13.8% a year against 12.1%. (Annual letters)
- **Judge over decades.** A sound approach can trail for years. Decide in advance how long you will give a strategy, and judge it honestly. (Owner's Manual)

## Words to know

- **Return on capital employed (ROCE)**: Operating profit as a percentage of the money tied up in a business. His main test of quality.
- **Gross margin**: Sales minus the direct cost of making them, as a percentage of sales.
- **Operating margin**: Profit after running costs, before interest and tax, as a percentage of sales.
- **Cash conversion**: How much of the reported profit actually arrives as cash.
- **Interest cover**: How many times profits cover the interest bill.
- **Free cash flow yield**: The cash a company produces after investment, as a percentage of its value.
- **Turnover**: The share of a portfolio bought and sold in a year.
- **Total cost of investment**: All the costs of owning a fund, including dealing costs, not just the headline charge.
- **Index fund**: A fund that simply buys a whole market index rather than choosing companies.
- **Creative accounting**: Legal but misleading ways of making profits look bigger or smoother than they are.
