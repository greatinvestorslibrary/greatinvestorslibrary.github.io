# Anthony Bolton

> Ran Fidelity Special Situations for 28 years, about 19.5% a year, by buying good companies the crowd had given up on.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/bolton.html · Section: the United Kingdom · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Go against the crowd, with a reason. He bought what others disliked, but only once he understood why the gloom was overdone.

## Summary

Anthony Bolton ran Fidelity Special Situations from December 1979 to December 2007, about 19.5% a year, well ahead of the UK market. His method: buy good companies the crowd has given up on, write down why you own each one, meet the managers, and sell when the reason breaks.

## Record at a glance

- **19.5% a year**: Fidelity Special Situations, Dec 1979 to Dec 2007
- **£1,000 → £147,000**: over his 28 years, dividends reinvested
- **about 13.5% a year**: the UK stock market over the same 28 years
- **£6bn**: the fund's size when it was split in two, 2006

## Big ideas

### 1. The contrarian's edge

The best bargains are usually in companies the crowd has given up on. The hard part is buying when it feels uncomfortable.

- **Popular means pricey.** When everyone likes a share, the good news is already in the price. When everyone hates it, a little good news can lift it a lot. (Podcast, 2025 · Morningstar)
- **Discomfort is a signal.** He bought recovery shares before all the information was in, a place he called the discomfort zone. (Morningstar)
- **But don't rush.** He warned that buying too early is a common mistake. Be patient with your entry, and add more only when conviction is high. (Morningstar)
- **The tech bubble test.** In the late 1990s he refused internet shares. His fund lagged the market by almost 20% before the bubble burst and proved him right. (Morningstar)
- **Unloved is not enough.** Going against the crowd only pays when you know why the crowd is wrong. A share can be unpopular for good reasons. (Book, 2009)

### 2. Write down why you own it

Every share you own needs a reason you can write in a few lines, and a written case against it.

- **A reason for every holding.** Know what you own and why, in words simple enough to explain to a friend. (Morningstar · Book, 2009)
- **Retest it.** He went back to each thesis at regular intervals to check that it still held. (Morningstar)
- **Write the counter-thesis.** He also wrote down what could make the share a bad investment, and worked out why he disagreed with the doubters. (Morningstar)
- **Seek out the other side.** He made a point of listening to people with the opposite view, to stress-test his own. (Podcast, 2025)
- **Forget what you paid.** The price you paid says nothing about what a share is worth today. (Book, 2009)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-02-write-down-why-you-own-it.html

### 3. What is already in the price

Look for a gap between price and value, measured in several ways, then give it time to close.

- **Hunt for anomalies.** He bought where the price looked out of line with what the company was worth, then waited for the gap to close. (Morningstar)
- **Several measures, no favourite.** Price to earnings, company value to cash profits, free cash flow yield, price to sales and cash returns on investment, each against history and against other companies. (Morningstar)
- **The right tool for the job.** For housebuilders he thought price to earnings nearly useless, because one-off land sales distort profits. Price to adjusted book value worked better. (Morningstar)
- **Spotting beats timing.** Seeing a mispricing is easier than knowing when it will correct. Some ideas took one year, some several. (Morningstar)
- **Stand firm through noise.** Share prices and profits can drift apart for a year or two. Keep testing the idea rather than giving up on it. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-03-what-is-already-in-the-price.html

### 4. Meet the managers

He learnt most by meeting the people who run companies, again and again, and writing everything down.

- **Two or three a day.** He met two or three companies every working day and valued hearing from managers directly. (Article, 2007)
- **Keep the notes.** He handwrote notes in every meeting and kept them all from the late 1980s onwards. (Article, 2007)
- **Candour counts.** He looked for managers who were candid and consistent. Like Buffett, he believed a boss who misleads shareholders may end up misleading himself. (Article, 2007)
- **The second meeting matters.** Repeated follow-up meetings, not first impressions, uncovered the occasional gem. (MoneyWeek)
- **Owners have a voice.** In the 2003 Carlton–Granada merger he pressed for an independent chairman. The press nicknamed him the Quiet Assassin, a name he disliked. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-04-meet-the-managers.html

### 5. What makes a good company

Start with the quality of the business, then ask whether it will be worth more in ten years.

- **Franchise first.** Begin by judging how strong the business franchise is. (Book, 2009)
- **Ten years from now.** Ask whether the company will still be here in ten years, and be more valuable. (Book, 2009)
- **Master of its own fate.** Prefer companies in control of their own destiny, rather than at the mercy of others. (Book, 2009)
- **Managers who know the detail.** Check that the people in charge understand the business strategically, operationally and financially. (Book, 2009)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-05-what-makes-a-good-company.html

### 6. Where the analysts are not

Medium and smaller companies, and those analysts have given up on, are where mispricing lives.

- **Less crowded corners.** He leaned towards medium and smaller companies, which fewer analysts followed. (Book, 2004)
- **No forecasts, no fans.** He liked companies that analysts had given up on, with few or no profit forecasts. (Morningstar)
- **Takeovers as a bonus.** Owning many cheap, mid-sized companies meant some would be bid for. His book has a whole chapter on why it is good to have your shares bought out. (Book, 2009)
- **Information is everywhere.** His book has chapters on where to find information and on the value of good brokers: do the reading others skip. (Book, 2009)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-06-where-the-analysts-are-not.html

### 7. Charts as a second opinion

Research comes first. He used price charts to confirm it and to time his buying and selling, never on their own.

- **Fundamentals first.** He used charts to confirm his research, not to replace it. (MoneyWeek)
- **Mostly for timing.** He found charts most useful on bigger companies, to time buying and selling once the research was done. (MoneyWeek)
- **A health check.** He compared checking a chart to a visit to the doctor: being too early in a share can be costly. (MoneyWeek)
- **Reading the crowd.** Charts, shareholder lists and directors' dealings all helped him judge where the crowd already stood. (Podcast, 2025)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-07-charts-as-a-second-opinion.html

### 8. When to sell

Sell when the reason for owning breaks, when the price reaches your target, or when you find something better.

- **Three reasons to sell.** Something breaks the thesis; the share reaches his valuation target; or he finds a better idea. (Morningstar)
- **Don't chase your losses.** Holding a loser just to win back what you lost, or to prove the original idea right, is dangerous. (Morningstar)
- **A broken thesis means sell.** Even if the share now looks cheap, a broken reason is a reason to go. (Morningstar)
- **Popularity is risk.** When a share becomes everyone's favourite, the risk has risen even if the story has not changed. (Podcast, 2025)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-08-when-to-sell.html

### 9. Know yourself

Temperament matters more than intelligence. Find an approach that fits your nature, then stick with it.

- **Temperament over IQ.** He believed emotional people do not make good investors, however clever they are. (Morningstar)
- **Your own style.** Find an approach that suits your personality, and keep to it through good and bad years. (Morningstar)
- **Ignore the benchmark.** He ran the fund on his own judgement rather than hugging the index, which is why it could look so different from the market. (Book, 2004)
- **Decide alone.** He drew on the widest possible range of facts and opinions, but the final call was always his own judgement. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/bolton-09-know-yourself.html

### 10. Even legends stumble

His China fund went badly at first. How he explained it teaches as much as his years of success.

- **The comeback.** In 2010 he came out of retirement to launch a China fund, a market he had not invested in before. (interactive investor)
- **What went wrong.** He blamed a focus on smaller companies, too much weight in consumer shares, and borrowing to invest, all of which hurt in a falling market. (Trustnet, 2012)
- **New market, new rules.** Accounting scandals at some Chinese companies listed in the US also hurt. Familiar methods need new checks in an unfamiliar market. (Trustnet, 2012)
- **He said sorry.** He apologised to investors for poor results. By the time he retired in 2014 the trust had just about turned a profit. (interactive investor)

## Read the originals

Both books are in print or easy to find second-hand. The Spectator article, the MoneyWeek interview and the 2025 podcast are free online.

- [Investing Against the Tide](https://www.pearson.com/store/p/investing-against-the-tide/P200000009635/9781292129280): His own book: the principles he used for 28 years, then stories of memorable meetings and of his best and worst investments. Short and practical.
- [Investing with Anthony Bolton](https://harriman-house.com/investing-with-anthony-bolton): A study of his methods and record by the financial writer Jonathan Davis, with a chapter of lessons in Bolton's own words.
- [Remember the wisdom of Keynes and Mark Twain](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/9th-june-2007/22/remember-the-wisdom-of-keynes-and-mark-twain): In his own words: meeting two or three companies a day, keeping notes, and what candid managers look like.
- [The guru who believes in charts](https://moneyweek.com/10642/anthony-bolton-the-guru-who-believes-in-charts): How he combined meetings with management, going against the herd, and charts as a second opinion.
- [What he learnt in three decades of investing](https://www.morningstar.in/posts/35426/investing-against-the-tide.aspx): His three big lessons, the valuation measures he used, recovery shares, and his three reasons to sell.
- [The mindset of a contrarian investor](https://www.nbim.no/en/news-and-insights/podcast/2025/the-mindset-of-a-contrarian-investor--anthony-bolton/): A long, free conversation on why popularity is risk, why the best opportunities feel uncomfortable, and how he reads the crowd.
- [The reasons for my underperformance](https://www.trustnet.com/News/386663/fund/sectors): Bolton explains what went wrong in his first years running a China fund.
- [Five fund managers who said sorry](https://ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/five-fund-managers-who-said-sorry-and-what-happened-next-ii515118): How his China fund ended, set beside other managers who apologised for poor results.

## Words to know

- **Contrarian**: An investor who deliberately goes against the crowd: buying what is unloved and selling what is loved.
- **Investment thesis**: The short written reason for owning a share.
- **Counter-thesis**: The written case against your own idea: what would make it a bad investment.
- **Recovery share**: A company in trouble whose shares are priced as if it will never recover.
- **Company value to cash profits (EV/EBITDA)**: The value of a whole company, shares plus debt minus cash, divided by profits before interest, tax and depreciation.
- **Free cash flow yield**: Cash a company has left after investing, as a percentage of its value.
- **Technical analysis**: Studying price charts and trading patterns, rather than the business itself.
- **Special situations**: Companies going through change: recovery, takeover, restructuring or neglect.
- **Gearing**: Borrowing money to invest more than you have. It magnifies both gains and losses.
