# Warren Buffett

> Ran Berkshire Hathaway for sixty years and explained every decision in a yearly letter to its owners.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/buffett.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

You are buying a claim on what a company will earn.

## Summary

Buffett ran Berkshire Hathaway for six decades and wrote to its owners every year. The letters are free, plainly written, and among the best education in investing there is.

## Big ideas

### 1. A share is a slice of a business

You are buying a claim on what a company will earn. The price on the screen is an offer from a stranger, and it carries no information about the business.

- **Pick businesses, not tickers.** He buys on a view of how the company will do over many years. He holds no view on where the share price or the market goes next, and does not act as if he had one. (2021 · 2022)
- **The farm and the shop.** In 1986 he bought a Nebraska farm, in 1993 a retail property in New York, knowing little about farming or real estate. He asked only two things: what will this asset earn over the years, and is the price sensible against that? Neither came with a daily quote, and he never missed having one. (2013)
- **Watch the field.** If you can enjoy a weekend without checking the price of something you own, you can do the same on a weekday. Results on the ground decide the outcome; the price catches up later. (2013)
- **Count the earnings you never see.** Your real income from a holding includes your share of the profit the company keeps. Accounts show only the dividend. At a business that reinvests well, the retained part is the more valuable half. (1999 · 2019 · 2020)
- **Popularity now, weight later.** Over months, price measures how liked a share is. Over years, it measures what the business is worth. This is Graham's idea, and he repeats it thirty years on because nothing has replaced it. (2022 · 2023)
- **Two doors into the same room.** Buying a whole company and buying 2% of a listed one are the same act of judgement. The listed route has two advantages: shares are sometimes offered at foolish prices, and a mistake can be sold in a day. (1995 · 2022)
- **Part of a gem beats all of a pebble.** A small piece of an outstanding business is worth more than full control of an average one, and it is far less work. (2013 · 2014 · 2020)
- **Keep an honest yardstick.** A rising market flatters everyone. The test is whether you beat a cheap index after costs and tax over several years, using a benchmark chosen in advance and never swapped after a bad stretch. (1997 · 2004 · 2009)

### 2. Moats: what makes a business worth owning

A great business earns a high return on the money inside it, and can keep doing so because something stops competitors from taking it away.

- **Three kinds of savings account.** Great: pays a very high rate and needs few new deposits to grow. Good: pays a fair rate, but only on large new deposits. Gruesome: grows fast, demands ever more money and pays little on it. He names See's Candies, FlightSafety and airlines as the three. (2007)
- **See's in numbers.** Bought in 1972 for $25 million, when it earned under $5 million before tax on $8 million of capital. By 2007 it earned $82 million on $40 million. Along the way it sent $1.35 billion to the parent after keeping only $32 million for itself. That cash bought other businesses. (2007)
- **The word that matters is enduring.** A moat that must be rebuilt every few years is not a moat, which rules out industries prone to rapid change. And if results depend on one superstar, the star is great, not the business. (2007)
- **The Inevitables.** A handful of companies will almost certainly dominate their fields for decades. A larger group will probably do so. Leaders in fast-moving industries belong to neither group. Even with an Inevitable you can overpay, and the usual way they get hurt is management drifting away from the core. (1996)
- **Is the moat wider this year?** He asks managers to strengthen the competitive position every year, even at the cost of current profit. Earnings bought by squeezing customers or skimping on the brand are a withdrawal from the moat. (2005 · 2008)
- **Lowest cost wins slowly.** Where customers buy mainly on price, the operator with the lowest costs keeps taking share. GEICO is his thirty-year example. (1995 · 2000 · 2013)
- **Some trades demand brilliance daily.** A retailer has to be clever every single day, because rivals copy each good idea at once. Other businesses run for decades on one right decision made long ago. He prefers the second kind. (1995)
- **Moats can drain.** Newspapers were close to a local monopoly. Once readers and advertisers had alternatives, capable managers could only slow the decline. When industry economics and management pull in opposite directions, economics wins. (2006 · 2012)
- **Growth is a number, not a virtue.** Growth that needs capital earning a poor return destroys value. Seeing that an industry will grow is easy. Working out who will keep the profit is the hard part, and when he cannot do that he passes. (2007 · 2009)
- **Later, he bought capital-heavy.** With too much cash for small gems, he bought a railroad and utilities: huge capital needs, steady returns on very large sums. In 2023 he said the railroad must spend more than its depreciation just to stand still, and that utility regulation had turned out worse than he assumed. (2009 · 2010 · 2023)

### 3. Price, value and the margin of safety

Value is the cash a business will hand its owners over time. Price is what you pay for that. The gap between the two is your return and your protection.

- **Aesop wrote the formula.** A bird in the hand against birds in the bush. To value anything you need three answers: how sure are you the birds are there, when will they appear and how many, and what does a risk-free bond pay meanwhile? Growth is one input among several, and it can subtract value. (2000)
- **A range, never a point.** Intrinsic value is an estimate. If the honest range is too wide to act on, move to something else. A rough figure that is about right beats an exact one that is wrong. (2000 · 2005 · 2010)
- **The two numbers are different things.** What you pay and what you get are separate questions. In the crisis he restated the aim: a good business at a fair price, ahead of a poor one at a low price. (2008)
- **Wonderful at fair beats fair at wonderful.** He began with statistically cheap, mediocre companies. Munger persuaded him that such bargains do not scale and seldom last. This is the largest single change in his method, and he credits it entirely to his partner. (2012 · 2014 · 2023)
- **Great companies at full prices earn ordinary returns.** Buy first-class businesses at rich valuations and you earn roughly their profit growth, nothing extra. A high price removes the margin of safety even when the business is superb. (2005 · 1997)
- **A stated hurdle.** He stayed out of shares unless he saw a high probability of at least 10% a year before tax. Idle cash was the accepted alternative. (2002)
- **Check expectations against arithmetic.** Shareholders together cannot earn more than businesses earn. Any return forecast has to fit economic growth plus inflation plus dividends, less costs. He ran this sum against the optimism of 1999 and against pension-fund assumptions in 2007. (1999 · 2007)
- **Value it as if it had no debt.** Cheap borrowing can make a poor purchase look fine per share. He prices every business on an all-equity basis, then decides. (2000 · 2017)
- **Size is an anchor.** As capital grows, the list of ideas that can move the total shrinks. A small investor has a real edge here, and it erodes with every doubling. (1997 · 1998)

### 4. Mr. Market and temperament

Prices swing far more than values do. The swings are there to be used or ignored. They are never instructions.

- **The hamburger quiz.** If you will be eating burgers for life and do not raise cattle, you should want beef to get cheaper. If you will be a net buyer of shares for years, the same logic applies. Only someone about to sell gains from high prices. (1997)
- **If you must time, time against the crowd.** His advice to those who insist on timing: be cautious when others are eager, and eager only when others are afraid. (2004)
- **Two kinds of fear.** Fear across the market helps you, because it produces bargains. Fear inside you hurts you. An investor who keeps costs low and sits with large, soundly financed businesses will almost certainly do well. (2016)
- **Berkshire itself has halved, more than once.** He printed his own stock's four worst falls (chart below) as the case against borrowing to own shares. Nobody can say how far a price may drop in the short run, and debt forces a decision at the worst moment. Without debt, your mind stays clear. (2017)
- **Cinderella at the ball.** In a boom everyone plans to leave just before midnight. The trouble is that nobody in the ballroom can see a clock. (2000)
- **Bubbles move wealth; they do not create it.** A company that loses money over its lifetime has destroyed value, whatever its market value was at the peak. The gains of promoters came out of the pockets of late buyers. (2000)
- **Being out is the bigger risk.** The danger of sitting out of the game is far larger than the danger of staying in it. He does not step in and out on forecasts. (2012)
- **Bad news is the buyer's friend.** Pessimism lowers prices. Large opportunities are rare, and when one arrives he acts in size. (2008 · 2009)
- **Ignore the chatter.** He spends no time on forecasts of the economy or the market, his own or anyone else's. Beyond the wasted hours, they crowd out the handful of facts about a business that decide the result. (2013)
- **Expect to trail in a boom.** A quality-first approach lags when markets roar and leads when they are flat or falling. Know this ahead of time so a strong year for others does not push you into changing method. (2012)

### 5. Activity, costs and patience

The return from owning good businesses is earned by sitting. Almost everything an investor does beyond that subtracts from it.

- **Newton's fourth law.** Newton gave physics three laws of motion and then lost a fortune in the South Sea Bubble. Buffett supplies the missing law: for investors as a group, the more they move, the less they keep. (2005)
- **The Gotrocks family.** Imagine one family owns every business in the country. Its wealth grows by what the businesses earn. Then helpers arrive: brokers to trade between cousins, managers to choose the brokers, consultants to choose the managers, and finally hedge funds with a share of profits. Each layer is paid out of the same earnings. He guessed these frictional costs might reach a fifth of all corporate profit. (2005)
- **Three ways investors beat themselves.** High costs from trading and fees. Decisions made on tips and fashion in place of valuation. And stop-start timing: getting in after a long rise and out after a long fall. (2004)
- **Wait for the fat pitch.** Ted Williams cut the strike zone into cells and swung only at balls in his best one. An investor has it easier, because there are no called strikes. Standing with the bat on your shoulder costs nothing. (1997)
- **Doing nothing is a decision.** When you own pieces of excellent businesses, inactivity is the intelligent course. Nobody sells a wonderful subsidiary because of a rate forecast, so why sell a minority stake in one? (1996)
- **The movies would have paid better.** After selling McDonald's and trimming other holdings, he told shareholders they would have done better had he spent market hours at the cinema. (1998)
- **Lumpy beats smooth.** He takes the higher, bumpier return over the lower, steadier one. Most managers choose the reverse because smooth is easier to explain. (1995 · 1998)
- **A dozen decisions.** Looking back over 58 years he counted about twelve really good decisions, roughly one every five years. The record rests on those, plus avoiding ruin in between. (2022)
- **The ten-year bet.** A plain index fund against five funds-of-funds chosen by professionals, over 2008 to 2017. The index fund won by a wide margin (chart below). The managers were not stupid. Layers of fees were the difference. (2016 · 2017)
- **One per cent is not small.** On his 77-year example, an annual fee of 1% would have cut the ending sum in half. (2018)
- **Idle cash is the lesser evil.** Cash earning little is uncomfortable. A purchase made to relieve the discomfort is worse. (1998 · 2003)

### 6. Risk, debt and survival

Risk is the chance of losing capital for good. Price swings are something else. Stay in a position where no single event can end the game.

- **The one rule.** Among everything that changed at Berkshire over six decades, he names one investment rule that has not and will not: do not put capital at risk of permanent loss. (2023)
- **Volatility is not risk.** Risk is the probability of ending your holding period with less purchasing power. Over a day or a year, shares are riskier than cash. Over decades, cash and bonds are the riskier choice. The horizon decides which is safe. (2011 · 2014)
- **Investors make shares risky themselves.** By trading actively, trying to time moves, diversifying too little, paying high fees and borrowing. Each can turn a good long-run asset into a poor result. (2014)
- **One zero is enough.** A long run of fine numbers multiplied by a single zero is zero. Leverage wins most of the time, which is why it is addictive, and the rare loss is total. (2010)
- **Never rely on strangers.** Keep so much liquidity that any conceivable cash need is small beside it. This costs return in ordinary years. It buys sleep, survival and the means to purchase when others must sell. (2009 · 2010 · 2021)
- **The tide.** Only when conditions turn do you learn who was running without protection. Calm years hide weak practice. (2001)
- **The Noah rule.** He had foreseen the risk of a mega-catastrophe and done too little about it. Forecasting the flood earns nothing. The ark does. (2001)
- **Exposure, not experience.** Judge a risk by what could happen. A quiet record says little about a danger that has not struck yet. (2001)
- **Look for hidden links.** Many small, unrelated risks are safe to carry. One concentrated exposure, or several that turn out to move together, is not. (2001 · 2005)
- **History-fed formulas.** Models built on past data give precise answers and false comfort when the conditions that produced the data have changed. He warns in particular against handing risk decisions to people with formulas. (2008)
- **Derivatives.** Hidden leverage, chains of counterparties, and profits booked on models years before any cash arrives. He wound down General Re's book and admitted he took too long. (2002 · 2003)
- **Small odds, long time.** A one-in-thirty chance each year becomes about 97% over a century. A risk you would dismiss in any single year is close to certain across an investing life. (2015)
- **Low rates are no excuse.** When safe yields are poor, the temptation is to accept weaker credit or longer maturities. He holds Treasury bills and waits. (2003 · 2019 · 2020)

### 7. Circle of competence

You do not need to understand many businesses. You need to know exactly which ones you do understand, and stay there.

- **Know the perimeter.** The size of the circle matters little. Knowing where it ends matters a great deal. You only ever have to value the companies you choose to value. (1996)
- **Why he skipped technology.** He did not think the industry was bad. He could not tell which companies would hold a durable advantage ten years out. In 1999 that discipline cost him his worst year against the index, and he gave his own stock-picking a D. He kept the rule. (1999)
- **Exciting is not the same as knowable.** The first item on his list of things Berkshire does not do: buy into businesses whose future he cannot assess, however thrilling the product. (2009)
- **Multiply the odds.** A plan that needs several separate things to go right has a low chance of success even if each step looks likely. He learned it from a zinc-recovery project. Simple propositions survive contact with reality better. (2004)
- **Passing is free.** If you cannot make a rough estimate of earnings five years ahead, move on. Nobody can call you out for not swinging. (1997 · 2013)
- **Know-nothing can beat know-something.** An investor who admits to knowing nothing about individual companies and buys a cheap index fund over time will beat most professionals. Trouble starts in between, with knowing a little and acting on a lot. (2013)
- **The circle can grow, slowly.** Apple entered the portfolio in 2016 and within five years was one of Berkshire's four giants. He treats it as a consumer franchise with loyal users and large buybacks, which is ground he knows. (2020 · 2021)
- **Keep learning.** Among the Munger sayings he passed on: the world changes, and your views must change with it. Staying inside the circle does not mean freezing it. (2022)

### 8. Capital allocation: what happens to retained profit

Over a decade, what management does with the profit it keeps counts for more than the profit itself.

- **An order of preference.** First, reinvest in existing businesses where that widens the moat. Second, buy other businesses. Third, repurchase shares when they sell well below value. Dividends come when none of these can earn a good return. (2012)
- **Dividend or sell a little?** He worked an example: owners who keep everything reinvested and sell a small slice each year can end with more cash and more capital than under a payout, provided retained money earns well. Each owner also sets his own level of income. (2012)
- **Buybacks need two conditions.** The company has more cash than the business needs, and the shares trade below a conservative estimate of value. With both, buying back is the surest use of money. Without the second, it destroys value for those who stay. (1999 · 2011 · 2016)
- **The IBM paradox.** If you own a company that keeps buying its own shares, and you are not selling, you should hope the price stays low for years. Each repurchase then lifts your share of the business by more. (2011)
- **Ownership that grows by itself.** Apple's repurchases raised Berkshire's percentage stake year after year without Berkshire buying a share. (2020 · 2021)
- **Issuing shares is selling the family silver.** Paying for an acquisition with stock means handing over a slice of everything you already own. He paid for Dexter Shoe in shares; by 2007 those shares were worth $3.5 billion, for a purchase of about $400 million. (1997 · 2005 · 2009)
- **The 1924 insight.** Edgar Lawrence Smith set out to show bonds beat shares and found the opposite, because companies keep part of their profit and compound it. Retained earnings are the engine. Everything else is commentary. (2019)
- **Deferred tax is an interest-free loan.** Tax owed on unrealised gains stays invested and working until the day you sell. Each sale ends that loan. (2018)
- **Options that reward hoarding.** A fixed-price option gains value when a manager simply retains earnings, with no skill involved. Watch how pay is set where profits pile up. (2005)

### 9. Acquisitions: a test for any company that buys others

Most deals help the seller and the adviser. Use his filters to judge every serial acquirer you own.

- **The seller knows more.** The seller picks the moment and writes the forecast. He treats sellers' projections as entertainment. Most acquisitions damage the buyer's shareholders. (1995)
- **Compare with the stock market.** Every proposed purchase has to beat the alternative of simply buying shares in the best listed companies. Many managements never run that comparison. (1995)
- **His six filters.** Large enough to matter. Consistent earning power already shown. Good returns on equity with little debt. Management in place. A simple business. A stated price. No turnarounds, no auctions, no hostile bids. (1999 · 2003 · 2008)
- **Be the buyer of choice.** Owners who care what happens to their company after the sale prefer a buyer with no exit plan and no debt-loading. That reputation took decades to build and wins deals money alone could not. (2000 · 2002 · 2008)
- **The spreadsheet never disappoints.** Once a chief executive wants a deal, the forecasts and the promised synergies will be found to support it. In 2017 he sat out because prices for decent businesses had reached records, pushed by cheap debt. (2017)
- **Who is advising?** An adviser paid only if the deal closes will favour closing. He proposed hiring a second adviser paid only if the deal fails. (2009 · 2019)
- **Like a marriage.** Surprises arrive after the ceremony. Most of his have worked out, and the good ones grow to become most of the value while the poor ones shrink in importance. (2019)
- **Overpaying, in his own words.** The $11 billion after-tax write-down on Precision Castparts was his error alone. He was too hopeful about its normal level of profit and so paid too much. Nobody misled him. (2020)
- **The handshake.** Some of his best purchases closed after one meeting with no formal checks. That works only when you know exactly who you are dealing with. (1997 · 2003)
- **Tuck-ins under proven managers.** Small additions to businesses run by tested people carry the least risk. They extend talent already proven. (2006)

### 10. Managers, pay and boards

He looks for people who run a company as if it were their family's only asset, and for pay and boards that keep them that way.

- **Three tests, in one sentence.** Good returns on the tangible capital the business needs, managers who are both able and honest, and a sensible price. (2019)
- **The hundred-year instruction.** His managers are told to run the business as if they owned all of it, it were the only asset their family would ever have, and it could not be sold for a century. (1998)
- **Choose carefully, then leave them alone.** He hires with great care and manages very little. Capital allocation and the choice of chief executive stay at the centre. Everything else is delegated. (2010)
- **Pay for what the manager controls.** Bonuses tied to the results of the manager's own unit. A share price moves for reasons outside any one person's control and makes a poor yardstick. (1996 · 1997 · 2006)
- **Options are a cost; bought shares are commitment.** Accounting long treated options as free. He counts them as pay and deducts them when valuing a company. He prefers managers who buy shares with their own money and carry the downside too. (1998 · 2001 · 2002)
- **Beware the chief executive who forecasts growth.** Very few large companies sustain 15% a year. A leader who promises it will eventually be tempted to manufacture it. (2000)
- **The company that always makes its number.** Business is too uneven for that. Consistently meeting expectations is evidence of management of the figures. (2002 · 2016 · 2022)
- **What a director is for.** Business sense, an owner's outlook, and shares bought with personal money. When the fee is a large part of a director's income, independence is hard to keep. The core job is choosing and overseeing the chief executive. (2002 · 2003 · 2019)
- **The ratchet.** Pay committees compare with peers, nobody wants to be below average, and so the average climbs every year. (2005)
- **Candour.** Spin travels inward: tell the public a flattering story for long enough and you start to believe it. He writes each letter as he would want to be written to if a relative ran the business and he were the absent owner. (1995 · 2023)
- **No thumb-sucking.** Munger's term for putting off the correction of a mistake. He calls delay the cardinal sin, worse than the original error. He adds that he never looks at where a candidate went to school. (2024)

### 11. Accounting: read like an owner

Reported numbers are where analysis starts. His letters are a thirty-year course in where they mislead.

- **Depreciation is real.** It is a worse cost than most, because the cash goes out first and the benefit arrives later. He is scathing about measures that leave it out. (2002)
- **Adjusted earnings.** Share-based pay is a cost. Restructuring that recurs every few years is a cost. Figures that strip them out flatter management. (2015 · 2016 · 2021)
- **Standing still costs more than the books say.** For capital-heavy businesses, real maintenance spending runs above the depreciation charge. Owner earnings are lower than reported profit. (2016 · 2018 · 2023)
- **The big bath.** Large one-time charges and reserves set up at acquisitions can be fed back into later years to smooth profit. He named the practice and declined to use it. (1998)
- **Not all amortisation is a cost.** Writing down software is a true expense. Writing down purchased customer lists or goodwill often is not, because the asset may be growing in value. Separate the two before judging earnings. (1999 · 2012)
- **The chosen base year.** A growth table that starts in a terrible year or ends in a wonderful one can make ordinary performance look brilliant. Check the endpoints. (2003)
- **Bottom-line noise.** Since unrealised gains on shares began flowing through profit, reported net income swings wildly and says little. He directs readers to operating earnings. (2017 · 2018 · 2019)
- **Book value retired.** After three decades as his yardstick, he dropped book value. It understates operating businesses, and buybacks above book reduce it while raising value per share. (2018)
- **Three warning signs.** Weak accounting choices, footnotes you cannot follow, and management that broadcasts profit forecasts. If you cannot understand a note, he assumes that was intended. (2002)
- **Self-graded exams.** An insurer's profit depends on its own estimate of future claims. Such estimates are usually too low. The same caution applies to any business where today's profit rests on a guess about tomorrow's losses. (1999 · 2001 · 2017)
- **Four questions for the auditor.** He drafted them for audit committees. The first: had the auditor alone prepared these accounts, what would have been done differently? (2002)

### 12. Insurance: float and the discipline of saying no

Every letter returns to insurance. The ideas carry over to any business that takes money today against a promise to pay later, such as lenders.

- **Float.** Premiums arrive now and claims are paid later. Meanwhile the insurer invests money it holds and does not own. If premiums cover claims and expenses, the float is free, or better than free. (1995 to 2024)
- **Three questions about float.** How much is there, what does it cost, and what is the long-run outlook for both? Size alone tells you nothing. (2002)
- **Four disciplines.** Understand every exposure. Assess the likelihood and cost of loss conservatively. Set a price that leaves a profit. Walk away if that price is not available. (2011 · 2014)
- **The fourth is where most fail.** National Indemnity let its volume shrink for years when prices were poor, and promised no layoffs so that staff would not write bad business to keep their jobs. Most insurers cannot bear to watch competitors take the business. (2004 · 2012)
- **Easy in, hard out.** A badly priced contract takes a moment to sign and can take decades to pay for. (1995)
- **Surprises go one way.** In long-dated promises, bad surprises far outnumber good ones. Reserve and price with that in mind. (2005)
- **Profit that is not profit yet.** Under-priced catastrophe cover can look like easy money for years before one event takes it all back. Judge such a business over a long span. (1996 · 1997)
- **A weak reinsurer is no protection.** Cheap cover from a party that may not pay is expensive. The same holds for any guarantee. (2002)
- **A revolving fund.** Accounts show float as a debt due. In practice it is replaced as it is paid out, so its economic burden is far lower than the stated figure, as long as underwriting holds. (2012 to 2016)
- **The long tail.** The true cost of some policies is not known for decades. Optimism has no place in pricing them. (2024)

### 13. Mistakes: his own list

Read what he took from each one. His costly errors fall into four groups: misjudging how long an advantage would last, paying with undervalued shares, paying an optimistic price, and waiting too long once the facts changed. Market crashes do not appear on the list. The other half of the picture came in 2022: about one good decision every five years has carried the record.

### 14. Productive assets, inflation and the long view

Wealth compounds in things that produce: businesses, farms, property. Claims on money and objects that produce nothing do not compound.

- **Three kinds of asset.** Claims on currency, such as deposits and bonds, look safe and lose purchasing power quietly. Assets that produce nothing depend on a later buyer paying more. Productive assets keep yielding whatever happens to the currency. He chooses the third by a wide margin. (2011)
- **The gold cube.** All the world's gold, about 170,000 tonnes, would make a cube 68 feet a side. At that year's price it equalled all the farmland in America plus sixteen Exxon Mobils, with about $1 trillion left over. A century on, the farms and companies will have produced enormous output. The cube will be the same cube. (2011)
- **The $114.75 example.** His first share purchase, in 1942, cost $114.75. Put into a no-fee index fund with dividends reinvested, that sum would have reached $606,811 by early 2019. In gold it would have been about $4,200. (2018)
- **What holds up in inflation.** The ideal asset can raise its prices with the currency and needs little fresh capital to do so. Farms, property and businesses such as See's pass both tests. (2011)
- **Cash: fortress, not home.** He holds very large amounts of Treasury bills so that Berkshire can never be forced. In his last letter he also wrote that he will never prefer cash-equivalents to ownership of good businesses. Both statements hold together. (2003 · 2024)
- **The tailwind.** Productivity gains have raised living standards for two centuries, and he expects that to continue. He says much of Berkshire's record came from this, as distinct from his own skill. (2015 · 2018 · 2020)
- **Japan.** Stakes in five trading houses, bought from 2019 and largely funded by borrowing in yen, so the currency risk is neutral. Simple businesses, low prices, sensible managements. The position was disclosed on his ninetieth birthday, a late lesson in looking abroad. (2020 to 2024)

## Words to know

- **Intrinsic value**: The cash a business will hand its owners over its remaining life, valued today. Always an estimate, best held as a range.
- **Margin of safety**: The gap between that value and the price you pay. It protects you when your estimate is wrong.
- **Moat**: Whatever stops competitors from taking a business's profits: a brand, low costs, a network, a licence.
- **Mr. Market**: Benjamin Graham's imaginary partner who offers to buy or sell every day at prices set by his mood. Use his offers; never take his advice.
- **Circle of competence**: The businesses you genuinely understand well enough to value. Knowing its edge matters more than its size.
- **Retained earnings**: Profit a company keeps and reinvests instead of paying out. What it earns on that money decides the next decade.
- **Buyback**: A company buying its own shares. Sensible only with spare cash and a price below value.
- **Float**: Money an insurer holds between collecting premiums and paying claims, invested in the meantime.
- **Owner earnings**: Profit after the spending needed just to keep the business where it is.
- **Book value**: Assets minus liabilities in the accounts. Buffett's yardstick for decades, retired in 2018.
