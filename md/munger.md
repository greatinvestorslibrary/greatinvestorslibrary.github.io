# Charlie Munger

> Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/munger.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Hang what you learn on a latticework of big ideas from many fields, and invert: ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid it.

## Summary

Charlie Munger was Buffett's partner and Berkshire's vice chairman from 1978 until his death in November 2023, at 99. He trained as a lawyer, and his talks are as much about how to think and live as about money.

## Big ideas

### 1. A latticework of models

Isolated facts do not stick and do not help. Hang them on a frame built from the big ideas of many fields, then use whichever ideas fit the problem.

- **The frame comes first.** If facts do not hang together on a latticework of theory, you cannot use them when you need them. Build a frame of models from several fields and attach your reading and experience to it. (USC 1994)
- **The man with a hammer.** A person who knows one model forces every problem into its shape. Narrow training makes this error common. (USC 1994)
- **Each field has a few big ideas.** The truly big ideas in each discipline, learned in essence, carry most of the load. Learning those is far less work than mastering any one field. (HLS 1998)
- **Learn it the way pilots do.** Pilots get training wide enough to cover nearly everything useful, use checklists every time, and keep skills fresh in simulators. He wanted professionals educated the same way. (HLS 1998)
- **Prefer the most basic explanation.** Give priority to the explanation from the most fundamental discipline, and credit each idea to the field it came from. (HLS 1998)
- **Be fluent with numbers.** Numerical fluency was one of his five helpful notions. Arithmetic shows what a goal really requires. (Practical 1996)
- **Big results have several causes.** Expect extreme outcomes from combinations of factors acting together, not from one lever. (Practical 1996)
- **Simple maths on the odds.** He judged bets with the elementary probability of Fermat and Pascal. Accounting helps as well, though it stops describing reality in some cases, such as a shrinking business. (USC 1994)
- **Don't worship what can be counted.** Almost everyone overweighs what can be numbered and neglects what cannot. (UCSB 2003)
- **Physics envy.** Hoping that economics will behave with the precision of physics gets clever people into terrible trouble. (UCSB 2003)
- **And then what?** Economics pays too little attention to second- and higher-order effects. Follow consequences further than the first step. (UCSB 2003)

### 2. Invert, always invert

Many problems that resist a direct attack give way when turned round. Ask what would guarantee failure, and then avoid it.

- **A graduation speech about misery.** Asked to advise school leavers, he listed sure routes to a miserable life, adding his own to three that Johnny Carson had given years before. Avoiding them is most of the work. (HS 1986)
- **The rustic's question.** A countryman wished he knew where he was going to die, so he would never go there. Munger treats the joke as a serious method. (HS 1986)
- **Jacobi's rule.** The mathematician Carl Jacobi advised turning problems around. Munger points to Darwin and Einstein, who made breakthroughs by thinking in reverse. (HS 1986)
- **Forward and backward.** Get the needed models into your head, then think each problem through both forward and backward. (Stanford 1996)
- **Decide the no-brainers first.** Settle the big, obvious questions first. With those fixed, the rest of a problem often becomes simple. (Practical 1996)
- **Think in reverse.** When you cannot see how to reach a goal, ask what would make it impossible, and remove that. (Practical 1996)
- **Twenty-one years later.** He told law graduates the same thing: turn a problem around and you often think better. (USC Law 2007)
- **What to avoid comes first.** He usually decided what not to do before choosing what to do. Avoiding large errors beats occasional flashes of brilliance. (Almanack)
- **Three baskets.** For investments he kept three baskets: yes, no, and too tough to understand. The third basket is large, and he felt no duty to empty it. (Almanack)

### 3. Incentives

To predict what people will do, look first at what they are rewarded for. He put this first in his list of 25 human tendencies.

- **The first tendency.** People respond to rewards and punishments far more strongly than they expect, and their beliefs drift to fit what pays. (PHM 2005)
- **Incentive-caused bias.** Decent people reach conclusions that serve their own income, often without knowing it. Take special care with advice from anyone paid by the action they recommend. (PHM 2005)
- **Pay for the result.** His example: a delivery company's night shift ran slow until workers were paid per shift and could go home once all the planes were loaded. Pay for time and you get time. (PHM 2005)
- **Controls are a kindness.** He called the cash register a great moral instrument. Systems that make cheating hard keep honest people honest. (PHM 2005)
- **Stop slop early.** Small dishonesty that is tolerated spreads, and becomes very hard to reverse. Stop it at the start. (Stanford 1996)
- **Granny's rule.** Make the pleasant thing the reward for doing the unpleasant thing first. (PHM 2005)
- **Keep advisers working for you.** Whoever pays the fees must control the advisers. Layers of consultants bring perverse incentives. (Foundations 1998)
- **Reasons work.** People comply more when given reasons, even weak ones. Give real reasons, and test the reasons you are given. (PHM 2005)
- **Choose your system.** Avoid perverse incentive systems, and avoid working directly under someone you do not admire and do not want to be like. (USC Law 2007)
- **Systems before villains.** He traced much corporate misconduct to standard incentive systems working on people below conscious awareness. Wrongdoing that is rewarded dies hard. (Scandal 2000)

### 4. The psychology of misjudgment

He built a list of 25 mental tendencies that lead sensible people into error, and used it as a checklist. The danger peaks when several act at once.

- **Three talks became one.** Talks given in 1992, 1994 and 1995 became one. He rewrote it for the book in 2005, at 81, from memory and without fresh research. (PHM 2005)
- **Use it as a checklist.** He ran through his own list, tendency by tendency, to explain events that puzzled him. Do the same before an important decision. (PHM 2005)
- **Lollapalooza.** Extreme outcomes come from several tendencies pushing the same way. One of his examples is an airliner evacuation test, run in a darkened hangar with older volunteers, that ended in many injuries. (PHM 2005)
- **Authority can be fatal.** People defer to whoever is in charge, even when that person is plainly wrong. In one of his examples, that deference ended in a plane crash. (PHM 2005)
- **Know it; don't abuse it.** Not everything you know about psychology should be used to manipulate people. Anyone who sees through it will hate you for it. (Stanford 1996)
- **Crowd folly.** Under some conditions people behave like lemmings. He said this explains much foolish thinking, in markets as elsewhere. (Roundtable 2000)
- **Priced like paintings.** He described shares being valued partly like Rembrandt paintings: bought mostly because their prices had already risen so far. (Roundtable 2000)

### 5. The inner life: envy, resentment, self-pity

Much of his life advice is about thoughts to refuse. A few emotions do more damage than bad luck, and they are under your control.

- **Three from Carson.** He began with Johnny Carson's prescriptions for misery: mood-altering chemicals, envy and resentment. (HS 1986)
- **Resentment costs the carrier.** He cites Disraeli, who learned to give up revenge as a motive, though he kept an outlet for resentment. A grudge mostly harms the person who carries it. (HS 1986)
- **Four to refuse.** Envy, resentment, revenge and self-pity: he named them together as disastrous ways of thinking. (USC Law 2007)
- **Envy is everywhere.** He said envy and jealousy are extreme in modern life. Expect them in others, and watch for them in yourself. (PHM 2005)
- **Defer the reaction.** The standard cure for overactive hostility is to train yourself to delay your response. (PHM 2005)
- **Denial.** The mind bends facts that are too painful to accept. Notice when you are doing it. (PHM 2005)
- **Light stress, heavy stress.** Light stress can slightly improve performance; heavy stress causes dysfunction. Make big decisions when calm. (PHM 2005)
- **Being right can cost you.** You can cause great offence by being right in a way that makes someone lose face in front of others. (USC Law 2007)
- **Avoid intense ideology.** He warned that extremely intense ideology clouds the mind. (USC Law 2007)
- **How to bear hard times.** Have low expectations, keep a sense of humour, and surround yourself with the love of friends and family. (Stanford 1996)

### 6. Reliability, trust and deserving

He thought most of what people want in life is earned by being someone others can rely on. Trust that is deserved is the strongest arrangement there is.

- **Be reliable.** First on his own list of routes to misery was being unreliable: not faithfully doing what you have promised. He said it can outweigh all your other virtues combined. (HS 1986)
- **Learnable by almost anyone.** In a 2006 note he repeated that reliability is essential for progress in life, and that almost anyone can learn it to great advantage. (HS 1986)
- **Deserve what you want.** The safest way to try to get what you want is to try to deserve it. (USC Law 2007)
- **Admiration-based love.** He thought no love is so right as love grounded in admiration, and that it should extend to the instructive dead. (USC Law 2007)
- **Three rules for a career.** Sell only what you would buy yourself. Work only for people you respect and admire. Work only with people you enjoy. (Stanford 1996)
- **A seamless web of deserved trust.** In your own life, what you want to maximise is trust that has been earned, all the way round. (USC Law 2007)
- **Choose clients like friends.** A rule from his years as a lawyer, and one that applies to partners, managers and investments too. (Almanack)
- **Liking distorts.** We overlook the faults of people and things we love. Love admirable people, and still check their work. (PHM 2005)
- **Records over impressions.** A face-to-face meeting carries too much weight. Underweigh the impression and overweigh the past record. (PHM 2005)
- **Fairness matters.** People react strongly when they are treated unfairly. Arrangements that feel unfair breed resentment that outlasts the gain. (PHM 2005)

### 7. Learning for life

He saw lifelong learning as a duty, and as the main reason ordinary people overtake cleverer ones over a working life.

- **Learning machines.** He kept seeing people rise who were not always the smartest, but who kept learning. They went to bed each night a little wiser than they were that morning. (USC Law 2007)
- **Wisdom is a moral duty.** He held that acquiring wisdom is an obligation, not a hobby. (USC Law 2007)
- **Skills expire.** Without lifetime learning you will not do well. Berkshire's skills from one decade would not have carried it through the next. (USC Law 2007)
- **Learn from other people's mistakes.** Learning everything from your own experience was on his list of routes to misery. The errors of others are cheaper teachers. (HS 1986)
- **The instructive dead.** Admire and learn from the great minds of the past. He counted them among the right objects of love. (USC Law 2007)
- **Keep curiosity alive.** Curiosity drives learning and protects against many other errors. (PHM 2005)
- **Use it or lose it.** Skills fade without practice. Skills practised to fluency last much longer. (PHM 2005)
- **Learning gets harder with age.** Ageing makes new, complex skills harder to acquire. Keep learning and practising, and learn the difficult things while you can. (PHM 2005)
- **Hunt for disconfirming evidence.** Darwin trained himself early to weigh seriously any evidence against his own hypotheses. Do the same with your beliefs. (PHM 2005)
- **Maintain what you know.** Pilots are made to practise in simulators so skills do not decay. Schedule your own refreshers. (HLS 1998)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/munger-07-learning-for-life.html

### 8. Objectivity and the edge of what you know

Knowing the limits of your competence is worth more than its size. Self-regard and strong ideology are the usual ways people step over the edge without noticing.

- **Circle of competence.** Everyone has a circle of competence. Play where you have an edge, and stay out of games where others clearly have it. (USC 1994)
- **Know what you don't know.** Know what you know and what you do not. When you lack competence, say so. (Stanford 1996)
- **Use your advantages.** Work out where your talents lie and use them. Berkshire avoided high technology partly because it had no edge there. (Stanford 1996)
- **Change your mind.** He said he and Buffett were very good at changing their prior conclusions. (Stanford 1996)
- **Earn the right to an opinion.** He did not feel entitled to an opinion until he could state the arguments against it better than its supporters could. (USC Law 2007)
- **Self-serving bias.** It is in everyone. Use routines and checklists to keep yourself objective. (USC Law 2007)
- **Don't fool yourself.** He quoted Feynman: the first principle is not to fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool. (Foundations 1998)
- **Don't rush to decide.** Uncertainty is uncomfortable, so the mind grabs an answer early. Slow down before a big decision. (PHM 2005)
- **Overoptimism.** People expect better outcomes than the odds support. The antidote is habitual use of simple probability. (PHM 2005)
- **Vivid is not important.** What comes to mind easily gets too much weight. Look for the evidence that is dull but large. (PHM 2005)
- **Crowds and authority.** People copy others and defer to authority, most of all when confused or rushed. Those are the moments to slow down. (PHM 2005)

### 9. Investing: few, great, and then sit

The big money comes from a handful of great businesses, bought when the odds are clearly favourable, then held for a long time.

- **The great businesses did it.** He said the great bulk of their money came from the great businesses. (USC 1994)
- **Bet heavily when the odds are clear.** The wise ones bet heavily when the world offers them the opportunity. The rest of the time, they wait. (USC 1994)
- **Twenty punches.** Imagine a ticket with only 20 slots for every investment you will ever make. You would think hard and bet big on each one. (USC 1994)
- **Quality compounds.** Over long periods a share earns roughly what the business earns. A business earning 18% on capital for 20 or 30 years gives a fine result even bought at an expensive-looking price. (USC 1994)
- **Taxes favour sitting.** The way income tax works alone gives a large edge to holding for a long time. (USC 1994)
- **The industry adds nothing, net.** The investment management business as a whole adds no value for all its clients combined. A select few managers can. (USC 1994)
- **Opportunity cost.** Judge every use of money against the next-best use available. (Almanack)
- **Resist the urge to act.** Patience means resisting the natural human bias to do something. (Almanack)
- **Concentration can be rational.** Someone with nearly all their wealth held long-term in three fine companies is securely rich. Heavy concentration can be a rational choice. (Foundations 1998)
- **Lumpy results are the price.** In super-cat reinsurance, huge swings in annual results, including some very unpleasant years, are inevitable. Judge it over many years. (Wesco 1996)
- **Need less.** In a 2006 note he worried about institutions imitating Harvard and Yale's investing out of envy, and favoured reducing one's perceived needs over taking more risk. (USC 1994)
- **Omissions cost most.** Among Berkshire's mistakes he named the failure to buy Walmart early. (BRK 2014)

### 10. Business models, moats and scale

He studied why some businesses compound for a century while others never earn their keep, and taught it through plain examples.

- **Scale isn't enough.** Sears's advantages of scale did not stop it losing heavily to Walmart. Bureaucracy did Sears terrible damage. (USC 1994)
- **Who keeps the gain?** In a commodity business, the advantages of great improvements flow through to customers, not to owners. (USC 1994)
- **Razor and blades.** Gillette began selling blades in multiple packages, with the razor handle a one-time purchase. Repeat purchases built the business. (USC 1994)
- **A gem under the mess.** GEICO had a magnificent business submerged in a mess, but still working. Clearing the mess released its value. (USC 1994)
- **Coca-Cola from scratch.** It is 1884 in Atlanta. You have $2 million and must build a business worth $2 trillion. How would you do it? (Practical 1996)
- **Start from the arithmetic.** He set the target first: 2.92 trillion eight-ounce servings in 2034. Netting 4¢ a serving would earn $117 billion. The numbers show what the business must become. (Practical 1996)
- **Many factors at once.** A strong trademark, Pavlovian conditioning, social proof and being always available, all working together. (Practical 1996)
- **Owners can wreck a brand.** With New Coke, the company's own executives came to the brink of destroying the most valuable trademark in the world. (Practical 1996)
- **Inflation sorts businesses.** Some businesses produce little cash even when volume holds steady. Borrowing short and lending long, the savings and loan model, can stop working and go into reverse. (Blue Chip 1982)
- **No jewel.** He told owners plainly that his savings and loan, as it then had to be run, was no jewel. Careful operators in that business earned very modest returns. (Wesco 1985)
- **Owners and managers.** In that industry, managers were mostly not owners, and their rewards were tied to size and recent numbers. (Wesco 1985)
- **A weaker vehicle, said plainly.** Each dollar of Wesco's book value, he wrote, gave much less intrinsic value than a dollar at Berkshire. (Wesco 2006)
- **Respect rivals you dislike.** People underrate both the competence and the morals of competitors they dislike. (PHM 2005)

### 11. Costs, consultants and febezzlement

Fees and frictional costs look small each year and become enormous over time. He attacked them with arithmetic and with a word he coined.

- **Layers of consultants.** Large institutions hired consultants to choose managers, adding layers with perverse incentives and little clear benefit. (Foundations 1998)
- **Three per cent a year.** Counting every layer, total investment costs could easily reach 3% of a foundation's net worth each year. (Foundations 1998)
- **Half must be below the median.** After the croupiers' take, exactly half of investors get a below-median result. Yet nearly every expert says he is above average. (Foundations 1998)
- **Leverage plus overconfidence.** Long-Term Capital Management collapsed through overconfidence in its highly leveraged methods. (Foundations 1998)
- **Indexing for the average foundation.** He judged indexing a wiser choice for the average foundation than what it was doing. (Foundations 1998)
- **Febezzle.** The economist J. K. Galbraith wrote of the 'bezzle', wealth that has been embezzled but not yet missed. Munger coined 'febezzle' for wealth quietly lost to wasteful investment fees. (Roundtable 2000)
- **Switching is expensive.** Constantly pulling money from lagging managers is folly. He put investors' real performance lag at over 5% of assets a year. (Roundtable 2000)
- **Wealth effects.** Rising share prices make people feel and spend as if richer, which he warned could produce much mischief. (Roundtable 2000)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/munger-11-costs-consultants-and-febezzlement.html

### 12. Accounting, honesty and candour

He wrote as a lawyer who distrusted flattering numbers. The test is always what an absent owner would want to be told.

- **A parable written in 2000.** He imagined Quant Tech, a prosperous company whose founder died in 1982, and traced how the managers who followed him ruined it by chasing reported earnings. (Scandal 2000)
- **Options counted as free.** Because share options were not counted as a cost, managers could add phony earnings to real ones. (Scandal 2000)
- **Dollops.** A moderate dollop of phony earnings each year is less likely to be noticed than one large one. (Scandal 2000)
- **A chain letter inside.** Inflated earnings brought a Ponzi or chain-letter effect into the share price, rewarding present holders at later buyers' expense. (Scandal 2000)
- **Professions pay the bill.** After the scandal, the public turned hard against the professions that had made it possible. (Scandal 2000)
- **Write for the absent owner.** Each year they tried to tell shareholders what they would want to be told if the roles were reversed. (Blue Chip 1982)
- **Repeat what matters.** Repetition is right where facts stay true and analytically important over many years. (Blue Chip 1982)
- **Flag the unusual.** He labelled a large gain as highly unusual and not likely to recur. (Wesco 1985)
- **Book profit when it is earned.** Super-cat underwriting profit was recognised only after the contracts expired. (Wesco 1996)
- **Ignore the windfall.** He told owners to ignore a large non-cash investment gain when judging the year's earnings. (Wesco 2006)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/munger-12-accounting-honesty-and-candour.html

### 13. Adversity, patience and hard work

Setbacks are certain, and many are unfair. What decides a life is the response to them, and the steady work done in between.

- **Don't stay down.** Staying down after a setback was on his list of routes to misery. (HS 1986)
- **Unfair blows will come.** Life is very likely to deliver terrible, unfair blows. Some people recover and others do not. He cited Epictetus on using each setback well. (USC Law 2007)
- **Assiduity.** He liked the word because it meant sitting down and staying there until the job is done. (USC Law 2007)
- **Losses bite harder.** Losses and near misses provoke outsized reactions. The most addictive forms of gambling are built on near misses. (PHM 2005)
- **Small steps go unnoticed.** Change that arrives in small steps escapes notice, because each step is a small contrast with the last. (PHM 2005)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/munger-13-adversity-patience-and-hard-work.html

### 14. The Berkshire system, in his words

In his 2014 essay, written directly to Berkshire shareholders for the first time, he explained why the company had worked.

- **Four causes.** He credited the constructive peculiarities of Buffett, the constructive peculiarities of the Berkshire system, good luck, and the intense devotion of some shareholders and admirers. (BRK 2014)
- **Focus, kept for 50 years.** Buffett's decision to limit himself to a few kinds of activity, and keep at them for 50 years, worked like a lollapalooza. Munger compared it to Roger Federer's tennis. (BRK 2014)
- **Each company keeps its own systems.** There is no group-wide personnel, stock option, incentive or retirement system. Subsidiaries run their own. (BRK 2014)
- **Keep head office small.** Buffett set out to minimise the bad effects that almost always come from a large bureaucracy at headquarters. (BRK 2014)
- **Win/win.** He wanted win/win results everywhere, for instance gaining loyalty by giving it. (BRK 2014)
- **A narrow job at the top.** The chairman keeps few tasks: managing nearly all the security investments, writing a long, useful letter as he would want it as a passive shareholder, answering questions for hours, and admiring what others achieve. (BRK 2014)
- **Rare elsewhere.** No other large company he knew had half these elements. Most have heavy bureaucracy and chief executives who arrive at about 59 and are soon retired by an age limit. (BRK 2014)
- **Successors.** He named Ajit Jain and Greg Abel as proven performers. (BRK 2014)

## Words to know

- **Worldly wisdom**: Practical judgment built from the big ideas of many fields, used together on real problems.
- **Latticework of models**: The connected set of those big ideas you think with. Facts are hung on it so they can be used.
- **Man with a hammer**: The error of forcing every problem into the one model you know.
- **Inversion**: Solving a problem backwards: ask what would cause failure, then avoid it.
- **Circle of competence**: The area where you really know enough to judge. Knowing its edge matters more than its size.
- **Incentive-caused bias**: Drifting towards conclusions that serve your own pay, often without noticing.
- **Lollapalooza**: An extreme result produced when several psychological forces push the same way at once.
- **Too tough to understand**: His third basket for ideas, beside yes and no. You never have to decide on those.
- **Opportunity cost**: Judging any use of money against the next-best use available.
- **Febezzle**: His word for wealth quietly lost to wasteful investment fees, modelled on Galbraith's 'bezzle'.

## Sources and scope

The talks are read from Poor Charlie's Almanack, the collection of Munger's talks edited by Peter D. Kaufman, in the Stripe Press edition, which the publisher has put online in full. The Wesco letters and the 1982 Blue Chip Stamps letter are read from published copies of the original annual letters. His 2014 essay appears in Berkshire Hathaway's 2014 annual report. Items tagged Almanack come from the book's own account of his methods. Every exact phrase and every date and figure was checked a second time against these sources. The many Munger quotations that circulate online were not used unless they appear in them. The wording is the compiler's own, apart from the short phrases marked as his, one per source.

Munger wrote no yearly letter like Buffett's. This section uses the eleven talks collected in Poor Charlie's Almanack, his letters to Wesco Financial and Blue Chip Stamps shareholders, and his 2014 essay for Berkshire. Remarks reported from meetings are left out, because no official transcripts exist and many circulating quotes are wrong.
