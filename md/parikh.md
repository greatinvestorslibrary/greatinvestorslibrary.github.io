# Parag Parikh

> Started as a sub-broker in 1979, became India's voice of behavioural finance, and built a fund with one scheme and his own money in it.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/parikh.html · Section: India · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Study the mind. Investor behaviour matters as much as the balance sheet.

## Summary

Parag Parikh started as a sub-broker on Dalal Street in 1979; later his wife and mother sold some of their jewellery to help him grow. He became India's pioneer of behavioural finance: the study of why investors make the same mistakes again and again. In 2013 he launched a mutual fund with just one scheme, put his own money in it, and later closed his broking business to avoid conflicts. He died in 2015 in Omaha, after attending Berkshire Hathaway's annual meeting.

## Record at a glance

- **1979**: began as a sub-broker; his wife and mother later sold some of their jewellery to help fund him
- **1 scheme**: all his mutual fund offered at launch in 2013; he called fund houses' many schemes a fad
- **2 books**: Stocks to Riches (2005) and Value Investing and Behavioural Finance (2009)
- **1999**: kept telling clients to sell technology shares at the height of the boom, and lost business for it

## Big ideas

### 1. From toothpaste tubes to shares

As a young man he wanted to make toothpaste tubes. Chandrakant Sampat told him to buy shares in good businesses instead. He started as a sub-broker, with his family's help.

- **Sampat's advice.** When his manufacturing idea was rejected as unviable, Sampat pointed him to listed businesses: share the growth without running the factory. (BS, 2015)
- **Family jewellery.** He started as a sub-broker in 1979, aiming to become a full broker. When he needed capital, his wife and mother sold some of their jewellery to help. (BS, 2015 · BS, 2015)
- **Research first.** His firm was one of the first Indian brokerages to set up a formal equity research desk. (Talk, 2014)
- **Sampat's student.** He said that whenever he sat with Sampat, he walked away wiser. (BS, 2015)

### 2. Study the mind, not just the accounts

He believed investor behaviour moves markets as much as company results. Understanding emotions, yours and others', is half the job.

- **What makes investors tick.** Stocks to Riches set out to discover what makes investors tick, and what keeps the stock market alive. (Book, 2005)
- **Profit from biases.** Investors can profit from the mental and emotional mistakes of others, if they avoid their own. (Talk, 2014)
- **India's pioneer.** His two books applied behavioural finance to Indian investors and Indian market history. (Moneylife, 2015 · Book, 2009)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-02-study-the-mind-not-just-the-accounts.html

### 3. Leave the herd

Greed and fear make Indian markets swing. He urged investors to buy when others are fearful, and paid a price for it in the technology boom.

- **Greed and fear.** His second book argues that successful investing means restraining and channelling emotions like greed and fear. (Book, 2009)
- **1999.** In the technology boom he suffered not because he owned such shares, but because he kept telling clients to sell them. (Talk, 2014)
- **Self-doubt.** As prices kept rising he admitted he was unsure whether he or the world was sane, but he held to his principles. (BS, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-03-leave-the-herd.html

### 4. Mistakes with Indian faces

His books name the traps: loss aversion, sunk costs, the endowment effect, mental accounting, decision paralysis. He showed them in Indian investors and Indian markets.

- **The list.** Stocks to Riches covers speculation, loss aversion, the sunk cost fallacy, the endowment effect, decision paralysis, mental accounting and mental shortcuts. (Book, 2005)
- **Indian examples.** His second book uses Indian market history and Indian companies to show these mistakes at work. (Book, 2009)
- **Traps by name.** It gives chapters to IPOs and to the growth trap: paying too much for exciting growth. (Book, 2009)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-04-mistakes-with-indian-faces.html

### 5. The law of the farm

Value investing is boring. You plant, you wait for years, you harvest. He called it the law of the farm.

- **Boring on purpose.** Value investing, he said, is boring: you have to wait for years. (Talk, 2014)
- **No shortcuts.** You cannot sow today and reap tomorrow. He called this a law made by God, not by men. (Talk, 2014)
- **A prepared mind.** He kept a watchlist of sound companies and bought when they became cheap. Fortune favours the prepared mind. (Talk, 2014)
- **A business, not paper.** Buy a business, not a piece of paper whose price you hope will rise. (Talk, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-05-the-law-of-the-farm.html

### 6. What a good business looks like

He looked for four things: honest management that treats minority shareholders well, a simple business, a strong moat and pricing power.

- **Four qualities.** Credible management that is fair to minority shareholders, a business you can understand, strong competitive moats, and pricing power. (Talk, 2014)
- **Use and exchange.** He separated a thing's value in use, what it does for you, from its value in exchange, what someone will pay for it. (Talk, 2014)
- **No greater fool.** Never pay more than intrinsic value; doing so means relying on a greater fool to buy from you. (Talk, 2014)
- **Buffett's way.** He believed Buffett's style of value investing, buying with a margin of safety, works best in India. (Talk, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-06-what-a-good-business-looks-like.html

### 7. Skin in the game

He insisted that those who manage money should invest alongside their clients, and he removed every conflict he could, even at a cost.

- **Co-investors.** He promised that his senior team, including himself, would invest alongside outside investors in the fund. (Cafemutual, 2015)
- **Family and staff too.** He, his family and key employees put personal money into the fund. (Cafemutual, 2015 · Value Research, 2015)
- **Closing the broking.** He shut his broking business and surrendered the licence to avoid conflicts of interest with the mutual fund. (Value Research, 2015 · Moneylife, 2015)
- **What he refused.** He rejected quick-gain tactics such as futures and options trading, and upfront commissions to attract clients. (Talk, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-07-skin-in-the-game.html

### 8. One fund, not a grocery shop

While fund houses launched scheme after scheme, he launched one. One clear way of investing, he argued, serves investors better.

- **One scheme.** PPFAS launched just one fund, managed in a go-anywhere style, so as not to confuse investors. (Cafemutual, 2015 · Value Research, 2015)
- **One right way.** There is only one right way of investing, he said; you can't have twenty. (Cafemutual, 2015)
- **Look abroad.** His fund could also buy shares overseas, including in the US. (Value Research, 2015)
- **Dividends and AGMs.** He saw a fund's dividend as just your own money handed back, and his fund was the first to hold unitholder meetings like company AGMs. (Value Research, 2015 · Moneylife, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-08-one-fund-not-a-grocery-shop.html

### 9. Standing up to the system

He was outspoken about the failings of India's finance industry, and argued with the regulator when he thought a rule would hurt competition.

- **An iconoclast.** His firm's tribute page calls him an iconoclast. (PPFAS)
- **The 2010 fight.** When a SEBI committee proposed sharply raising the money needed to run a mutual fund, he argued it would shut out new, smaller competitors. (BS, 2015)
- **A pass-through.** A mutual fund, he insisted, merely passes investors' money through; it is not a bank. The proposal was shelved. (BS, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/parikh-09-standing-up-to-the-system.html

### 10. A firm built to outlast him

He died suddenly in 2015, in Omaha, after Warren Buffett's annual meeting. The firm he built, run on his principles, carried on without him.

- **Omaha.** He died in a road accident on 3 May 2015, travelling with colleagues after the Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting. (Moneylife, 2015 · Cafemutual, 2015)
- **Built to last.** He set out to build an organisation and a team able to run without him. (PPFAS)
- **A long commitment.** His fund asked investors to think in terms of at least five years, holding quality through market cycles. (Value Research, 2015)
- **Against the odds.** Business Standard described his life as a journey from a childhood with polio to one of India's best-regarded value investors. (BS, 2015)

## Special section

**Which bias is this? Tap your answer**

- A WhatsApp group says a small company will double. Everyone you know is buying, so you buy too. Answer: Herd behaviour. Buying because others are buying. His books warn that the crowd is usually late.
- You bought at ₹500. It is ₹300 and the business has got worse, but you won't sell until it's back to ₹500. Answer: Loss aversion. Refusing to take a loss. The market doesn't know or care what you paid.
- Your Diwali bonus feels like free money, so you gamble it on options instead of investing it. Answer: Mental accounting. A rupee is a rupee, wherever it came from. Treat a bonus like any other savings.
- You keep shares your father left you, though you would never buy them at today's price. Answer: Endowment effect. Valuing something more because you own it. Ask: would I buy this today?
- You paid ₹50,000 for a trading course, so you keep trading to make it worthwhile, though you keep losing. Answer: Sunk cost fallacy. Money already spent is gone. Only future costs and benefits should decide.
- There are so many mutual fund schemes that you can't choose, so your savings sit idle for years. Answer: Decision paralysis. Too many choices freeze you. One reason he launched only one fund.

**His four marks of a good business**

- Credible management that treats minority shareholders fairly.
- A simple business you can understand.
- A strong moat that keeps competitors out.
- Pricing power, so profits can keep up with costs.

## Words to know

- **Behavioural finance**: The study of how emotions and mental shortcuts shape investors' decisions.
- **Sub-broker**: An agent who dealt through a registered stockbroker, often a first step into broking.
- **AMC**: Asset management company: the firm that runs a mutual fund.
- **PMS**: Portfolio management service: a manager running a separate portfolio for each wealthy client.
- **Herd behaviour**: Doing what everyone else does because they are doing it.
- **Loss aversion**: Feeling a loss about twice as strongly as an equal gain.
- **Mental accounting**: Treating money differently depending on where it came from, such as a bonus.
- **Endowment effect**: Valuing something more just because you own it.
- **SEBI**: The Securities and Exchange Board of India, the market regulator.
