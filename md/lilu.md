# Li Lu

> Heard Buffett speak by chance, founded Himalaya Capital, and became the only outside manager Charlie Munger said he ever invested with.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/lilu.html · Section: China · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

A share is part of a business. Returns come from the business growing in value, not from guessing prices.

## Summary

Li Lu reached the United States in 1989 with little money and less English, heard Warren Buffett speak at Columbia in 1993, and founded Himalaya Capital in 1997. Charlie Munger later called him the only outside manager he had ever invested with. His method: treat a share as part of a business, know exactly where your knowledge ends, buy with a margin of safety, and wait for the rare great bet.

## Record at a glance

- **29%+ a year**: his fund, January 1998 to 2009, after all expenses, in his own words (not independently audited)
- **36% a year**: the same fund from late 2004, when Munger joined, to 2009
- **−19%**: his first year, on paper; a large investor left the following year
- **10% of BYD**: bought by Berkshire for about $230m in 2008, after Li Lu brought the company to Munger

## Big ideas

### 1. A share is a piece of a business

Value investing rests on four ideas. The first is that a share is part-ownership of a real business, so your return comes from the business growing.

- **Four ideas, one road.** He sums up value investing as four ideas: a share is part of a business; the market is there to serve you; buy with a margin of safety; stay inside your circle of competence. (Lecture, 2015 · Lecture, 2019)
- **The lecture that changed his life.** A classmate took him to hear Buffett at Columbia. He found the case clear and convincing: shares are pieces of businesses, and over time prices follow what those businesses are worth. (Foreword, 2010 · Interview, 2013)
- **Two years of reading.** He then spent nearly two years reading about Buffett, Berkshire and Munger before buying his first share, while his own net worth was still below zero. (Foreword, 2010)
- **Owning changes how you look.** Once he owned a company he became curious about every part of it, and that curiosity taught him the whole industry. (Lecture, 2019)
- **Win-win, not zero-sum.** He likes investing in businesses because owner and company can both win. Trading against other traders is a zero-sum game. (Interview, 2013 · Talk, 2026)

### 2. Know where your circle ends

Your circle of competence should be small and clearly drawn. The skill is knowing where it ends, and what would prove you wrong.

- **The edge matters most.** What counts is not how much you know but knowing exactly where your knowledge stops. (Lecture, 2015 · Talk, 2026)
- **Say when you would be wrong.** You should be able to state the conditions under which your view of a company would fail. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Small and hard-won.** A real circle is built over years and stays small. Most people cannot see five or ten years ahead for most companies. (Lecture, 2015 · Lecture, 2019)
- **Don't fool yourself.** Intellectual honesty comes first: you are the easiest person to fool, and people tend to believe whatever suits them. (Lecture, 2015 · Lecture, 2019)
- **Argue the other side first.** From Munger he took the rule of holding no opinion until he can state the opposing case better than its supporters. (Foreword, 2010)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-02-know-where-your-circle-ends.html

### 3. The market serves you

Prices are there to use when they suit you. The market cannot tell you what a company is worth, and it punishes anyone who forgets that.

- **Servant, not master.** Use market prices to buy and sell when they suit you; never take them as a verdict on value. (Lecture, 2015 · Talk, 2026)
- **You cannot have both.** Many investors want the benefits of value investing and good results every quarter. Chasing both pushes you into speculation. (Talk, 2026)
- **The market finds your weak spots.** Act outside what you understand, or let fear and greed run you, and the market will expose it sooner or later. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Few play this game.** He estimates that only about one market participant in twenty invests this way. That is why the opportunity lasts. (Lecture, 2015 · Lecture, 2019)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-03-the-market-serves-you.html

### 4. Margin of safety

Because the future is uncertain, buy at a price far below your cautious estimate of value. He started with very cheap shares and moved, over time, to great businesses at fair prices.

- **Price is what you pay.** Since value can only be estimated, buy at the lowest price you can, and study the parts of a business that bear on your margin of safety. (Lecture, 2019)
- **His first big find.** As a student he bought a holding company whose stake in a large cable firm was alone worth about three times the whole company's market value. (Lecture, 2019)
- **Sold when the gap closed.** He sold once the margin of safety had gone. The shares kept rising for years afterwards. (Lecture, 2019)
- **From cheap to good.** A beginner with little money, he first bought very cheap shares, the safest choice. In time he moved to strong businesses at reasonable prices. (Interview, 2013)
- **Go and look.** Early on he visited nearby companies to check that their assets were real and the business was running. (Lecture, 2019)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-04-margin-of-safety.html

### 5. Research like an owner

Study a company as if you had just inherited it and had to run it. Talk to everyone, from the boss to the security guard.

- **Imagine you inherited it.** He suggests picturing that the company has just become yours and you must learn everything about it. (Lecture, 2019)
- **Talk to everyone.** He talks to people at every level, down to security guards, and asks about hiring, pay and how staff are treated. (Lecture, 2019)
- **The two petrol stations.** Two identical petrol stations drew very different custom, one about four times the other. The difference was an owner who ran his with care. So ask: do managers think like owners? (Lecture, 2019)
- **Talk to operators.** His ideas come from wide reading, newspapers and annual reports, and from people who run businesses, more than from other investors. (Interview, 2013)
- **When you can't judge the boss.** Management matters but is hard to assess. When he cannot judge it, he leans on the quality of the business and the price. (Interview, 2013)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-05-research-like-an-owner.html

### 6. Why he gave up short selling

He calls short selling one of his worst mistakes. The maths, the frauds and the distraction all work against you.

- **His worst mistake.** He ran a long-short fund in his early years, then stopped shorting around 2004 and calls it one of his worst mistakes. (Interview, 2013 · Foreword, 2010)
- **Small gain, unlimited loss.** A short can make at most 100% but can lose without limit, and you can be forced out before you are proved right. (Interview, 2013 · Foreword, 2010)
- **Frauds can last.** The best shorts often involve fraud, and frauds can survive long enough to squeeze you out. (Interview, 2013)
- **It steals your attention.** Constant short-side trading left no time to study his best long-term ideas. (Foreword, 2010 · Interview, 2013)
- **Growth favours owners.** Over long periods, a growing economy works for those who own businesses, not for those betting against them. (Interview, 2013)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-06-why-he-gave-up-short-selling.html

### 7. Patient, then decisive

Great opportunities are rare. Wait calmly while nothing happens, then act firmly when one appears.

- **One idea in decades.** Munger read Barron's for decades and found just one big idea in it. That is how rare the best chances are. (Lecture, 2019)
- **Both at once.** Patience and decisiveness have to live together: wait without fidgeting, then move in size. (Lecture, 2019)
- **Talent takes years to show.** It can take around fifteen years to tell real skill from luck; short-term results are often luck. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Each shot stands alone.** Like golf, a good hole does not guarantee the next. Judge each decision on its own merits. (Lecture, 2019)
- **BYD.** He was drawn to the battery and car maker by its founder's engineering record, its rise in a fierce market, and a margin of safety. Berkshire bought 10% in 2008 for about $230m and sold out by 2025 after a more than twentyfold rise. (Interview, 2013 · News, 2025)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-07-patient-then-decisive.html

### 8. A different road from Wall Street

Munger told him his problems were Wall Street's problems. He rebuilt his fund around long-term partners and a sense of duty to their savings.

- **Thanksgiving 2003.** He took Munger through every company he owned or followed. Munger said his problems came from the way Wall Street thinks, and offered to invest if he would take a different path. (Foreword, 2010)
- **A new structure.** With Munger's help he rebuilt the fund like Buffett's early partnerships: no shorting, long lock-ups for partners who stayed, and no new investors. (Foreword, 2010)
- **Your parents' savings.** He says a manager should treat clients' money like the lifetime savings of their own parents, and that people without that instinct should not be in the business. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Fees that favour managers.** He criticises the common hedge fund fee of 2% a year plus 20% of profits as serving managers better than clients. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Deserved trust.** He recalls Munger's ideal: a seamless web of deserved trust. Keep partners who share your approach, rather than chasing the most money. (Talk, 2026)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-08-a-different-road-from-wall-street.html

### 9. Own a slice of the world

Real wealth is your share of the world's buying power. Over two centuries, owning businesses beat cash, gold and bonds by a vast margin.

- **Fish where the fish are.** From Munger he takes the rule to look where others are not: less crowded markets, industries and countries. (Talk, 2026)
- **Wealth is a share of the world.** He measures wealth as your percentage of global buying power, not gold, cash or any currency, and keeps it by owning great businesses in growing economies. (Talk, 2026)
- **Two centuries of returns.** He cites US figures from 1802: a dollar in cash lost about 95% of its value, gold roughly tripled after inflation, and shares grew about a million times, roughly 6.7% a year after inflation. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Civilisation 3.0.** In his book he describes history in stages: hunting and gathering, farming, then free markets plus modern science, the stage in which businesses can compound. (Book, 2020 · Lecture, 2015)
- **China too.** In 2015 he argued value investing could work in China: its markets had risen about fifteenfold from 1991 to 2014, while short-term trading still dominated. (Lecture, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lilu-09-own-a-slice-of-the-world.html

### 10. Mistakes, luck and compounding knowledge

He had a bad first year, sold winners too early and gave up shorting the hard way. He credits luck, and says knowledge compounds like money.

- **A bad start.** His first year ended 19% down on paper, and a large investor left the next year. (Foreword, 2010)
- **Sold too soon.** He was an early investor in Capital IQ, the data company, and sold; it later became far more valuable. (Interview, 2013)
- **Every mistake teaches.** He treats each mistake as a lesson, and names short selling as his biggest. (Interview, 2013)
- **Luck counts.** He credits part of his success to luck. (Talk, 2026)
- **Knowledge compounds.** Knowledge and trusted relationships compound like capital. Learning faster is how money, eventually, grows faster. (Talk, 2026 · Lecture, 2019)

## Special section

**Before you buy: eight questions**

- I can say what the business does and how it earns its money
- I would hold it even if no price were quoted for years
- The price is well below my cautious estimate of its value
- I know where my knowledge of it ends, and what would prove me wrong
- I have learned about it from people who work there, buy from it or compete with it
- Its managers think and act like owners
- I am buying to own it, not betting against anything
- Few other investors are looking at it closely

**His six principles, as he put them in 2026**

- A share is ownership of part of a real business.
- The market serves you it does not instruct you.
- Margin of safety because the future cannot be predicted.
- Circle of competence and above all, knowing where it ends.
- Fish where the fish are in less crowded markets, industries and countries.
- Wealth is a share of the world's buying power held through great businesses in growing economies, for a long time.

## Words to know

- **Value investing**: Buying a share for less than the business is worth, and holding it while the business grows.
- **Circle of competence**: The area where you genuinely understand businesses well enough to value them.
- **Margin of safety**: The gap between a cautious estimate of value and the price you pay.
- **Mr Market**: Benjamin Graham's picture of the market as a moody partner offering prices you may take or ignore.
- **Short selling**: Borrowing shares to sell them, hoping to buy them back later at a lower price.
- **Long-only fund**: A fund that only owns shares and never bets on prices falling.
- **Lock-up**: A period during which investors agree not to withdraw their money.
- **Intrinsic value**: What a business is really worth, judged from the cash it will produce.
- **Fiduciary**: Someone trusted to act in another person's interest, before their own.
