# Peter Cundill

> Read Graham on a plane in 1973, then hunted the world for shares below their liquidation value: the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/cundill.html · Section: the wider world · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Below liquidation value. Buy a dollar of assets for 40 or 50 cents.

## Summary

Peter Cundill was a Canadian accountant who read Ben Graham's Security Analysis on a flight home in December 1973 and changed his life. He took over a small fund that had lost two-thirds of its value and turned it into the Cundill Value Fund. He hunted the world for shares selling below their liquidation value, from Japan to Europe, and compounded about 22% a year for his first fifteen years without a losing year. They called him the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.

## Record at a glance

- **22%**: a year: the Cundill Value Fund's first fifteen years, with no losing year
- **30%+**: a year: the fund's compound return in its first decade, to 1985
- **37%**: of the fund in Japan in 2004, with a third in cash, when bargains were scarce elsewhere
- **$500**: his first investment, in a mining tip, lost within 48 hours

## Big ideas

### 1. A $500 lesson

His first investment, as a summer office boy, was a mining tip. He lost all $500 in under two days, and never forgot it.

- **Montreal.** He was born in Montreal in 1938 and studied commerce at McGill, graduating in 1960. (McGill, 2011)
- **The tip.** As an office boy in 1959 he put $500 into a speculative mining share and lost it all within 48 hours. (Globe, 2011)
- **An accountant.** He qualified as a chartered accountant before moving into investment work. (McGill, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-01-a-500-lesson.html

### 2. The eureka moment

On a flight home in December 1973 he read Graham's Security Analysis. It gave him the method he had been missing.

- **The flight.** Flying from Toronto to Vancouver for Christmas in 1973, he read Graham and found his road. (Book, 2011)
- **A fund to fix.** In 1975 he took control of a small fund whose shares had fallen from $6 to $2. (Book, 2011 · Biography, 2014)
- **A new name.** He renamed it the Cundill Value Fund and ran it on Graham's rules. (McGill, 2011 · Book, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-02-the-eureka-moment.html

### 3. A dollar for forty cents

He bought shares priced below the company's liquidation value: what it would be worth if it closed and sold everything.

- **Liquidation value.** A share is cheap when its price is below what the business would fetch if wound up. (Morningstar · Book, 2011)
- **Margin of safety.** That gap was the margin of safety he swore by. (Morningstar)
- **A dollar for forty cents.** He popularised the idea of buying a dollar of value for 40 cents. (Morningstar)
- **Net-nets.** His favourite was a share below working capital minus all debts, Graham's net-net. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-03-a-dollar-for-forty-cents.html

### 4. His six tests

He wrote down his criteria: cheap against value and book, profitable, paying dividends, well off its high, and on a low P/E.

- **Below value and book.** The price must be below intrinsic value and book value, ideally below net working capital less long-term debt. (Morningstar)
- **Profitable.** Ideally five years of growing earnings. (Morningstar)
- **Dividends.** Ideally a regularly rising dividend. (Morningstar)
- **Out of favour.** A price under half its former high, preferably near its all-time low, and a P/E under ten. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-04-his-six-tests.html

### 5. Hidden assets

He read accounts for what they hid: buildings at old costs, valuable leases, power plants. JWT was his textbook case.

- **JWT.** The advertising agency J. Walter Thompson listed above $20 in 1972 and fell to about $4. (Morningstar)
- **Not on the books.** Its book value of $18 a share left out freehold buildings in Tokyo and Paris and a London lease. (Morningstar)
- **The result.** He bought at about $8 on average and sold a year later above $20. (Morningstar)
- **Homework.** Very few investors do the homework, which is the edge. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-05-hidden-assets.html

### 6. Buy more as it falls

His best investment, Cleveland-Cliffs, fell by more than half after he bought. He kept buying.

- **Unloved.** An iron-ore pellet maker with a large market share, it fell from his first price of $15 to $6. (Morningstar · Globe, 2011)
- **He kept buying.** He bought and bought as the price dropped, though the supply seemed endless. (Morningstar · Globe, 2011)
- **Sold, then bought back.** He sold much of it in the 1986–87 rally and bought back after the October 1987 crash. (Globe, 2011)
- **The return.** When he finally sold in 1991 he had compounded at over 30% a year. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-06-buy-more-as-it-falls.html

### 7. Indiana Jones

He searched the world for bargains, often starting with the country whose market had done worst that year.

- **The nickname.** He was nicknamed the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers. (Morningstar)
- **The worst market first.** He would look at the country with the year's worst stock market and put some money there. (McGill)
- **Japan.** After the 1997 crash he found hundreds of Japanese shares selling below net cash, with strong balance sheets. (Interview, 2004)
- **37%.** In 2004 over a third of his fund was in Japan. (Interview, 2004)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-07-indiana-jones.html

### 8. Cash is a position

When few shares met his tests he simply held cash, sometimes up to 40%, and waited.

- **A third in cash.** In 2004 he held about 33% in cash because he was finding few bargains. (Interview, 2004)
- **Up to 40%.** In the past his cash had been as high as 40%. (Interview, 2004)
- **Flexibility.** Cash kept him free to buy when the next crisis came. (Interview, 2004)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-08-cash-is-a-position.html

### 9. The one that went wrong

His worst investment, Cable and Wireless, met every Graham test and still failed. Careful analysis guarantees nothing.

- **By the book.** He bought after the dot-com bust: a low price, no debt, lots of cash, profitable networks. (Globe, 2011)
- **It failed.** He called it the investment where he had his head handed to him. (Globe, 2011)
- **The lesson.** Even strict adherence to Graham's rules is no guarantee. (Globe, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-09-the-one-that-went-wrong.html

### 10. Patience, history and giving back

He ended advising patience, picking good managers, and studying history, and left a large prize for history writing.

- **Patience.** His mantra was patience, patience and more patience; the big rewards come from compounding. (Globe, 2011)
- **Good managers.** After selling his firm to Mackenzie in 2006 he picked few shares himself and backed managers who used his value approach. (Globe, 2011 · Inv. Executive)
- **History.** He saw history and investing as alike: both study the past to understand the present. (McGill, 2011)
- **The prize.** His foundation created the Cundill History Prize at McGill in 2008, the largest of its kind. (McGill, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/cundill-10-patience-history-and-giving-back.html

## Special section

**Would it pass the Cundill screen?**

- The price is below your estimate of intrinsic value
- The price is below book value, ideally below working capital less long-term debt
- The company is profitable, ideally with five years of growing earnings
- It pays a dividend, ideally a rising one
- The price is under half its former high, ideally near its low
- The P/E is under ten

**Three trades, three lessons**

- J. Walter Thompson: the accounts left out buildings and a lease; homework found them.
- Cleveland-Cliffs: he kept buying as it halved, and earned over 30% a year.
- Cable and Wireless: every test passed, and it still failed.

## Words to know

- **Liquidation value**: What a company would fetch if it stopped trading and sold everything, after paying its debts.
- **Net-net**: A share priced below current assets minus all liabilities: Graham's deepest bargain.
- **Working capital**: Current assets, such as cash, stock and money owed by customers, minus current liabilities.
- **Book value**: Assets minus liabilities, as shown in the accounts.
- **P/E**: Price to earnings: the share price divided by yearly earnings per share.
- **Net cash**: Cash minus all debt.
- **Margin of safety**: The gap between price and value that protects you if you are wrong.
