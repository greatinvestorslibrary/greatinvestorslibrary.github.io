# John Templeton

> Bought 104 cheap shares as war broke out in 1939, then searched the whole world for bargains at the point of maximum pessimism.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/templeton.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Maximum pessimism. The best bargains appear when almost everyone is selling.

## Summary

John Templeton grew up in a small town in Tennessee, won a Rhodes scholarship, and in 1939 borrowed $10,000 to buy every cheap share he could as war broke out. His Templeton Growth Fund, started in 1954, looked for bargains across the world long before that was usual. His creed: buy at the point of maximum pessimism, search everywhere, stay humble, and never think you know it all.

## Record at a glance

- **14.5% a year**: Templeton Growth Fund, 1954 to 1992, as reported by AP and the New York Times
- **$10,000 → $2m**: what $10,000 put into the fund in 1954 had grown to by 1992
- **104 stocks**: bought in 1939 at $1 or less, 34 of them in bankruptcy (some say 37); only 4 became worthless
- **$90m**: profit on bets against overpriced technology shares in 2000, according to his great-niece

## Big ideas

### 1. Buy at maximum pessimism

Shares only become bargains when people sell them, and people sell when they are gloomy. So the best time to buy is when almost everyone else is pessimistic.

- **Buy low.** Buy when others are despondently selling, and sell when they are greedily buying. Following the crowd means buying what is already expensive. (Rules, 1993)
- **Why bargains exist.** The only way a share gets cheap is that people sell it, and the usual reason they sell is pessimism. (Book, 2008)
- **The four moods.** A line widely credited to him describes a bull market's life: born on pessimism, growing on scepticism, maturing on optimism, dying on euphoria. (Book, 2008)
- **Prepare in calm times.** He kept a wish list of quality companies, with orders ready to buy if prices collapsed, so fear could not stop him acting. (Morningstar)

### 2. The 1939 basket

As war broke out in Europe, he borrowed $10,000 and bought 100 shares in each of 104 companies trading at $1 or less. Nearly all of them made money.

- **The order.** In 1939 he bought 100 shares in each of 104 companies selling at $1 or less, including 34 in bankruptcy. (Morningstar · Irish Times, 2008)
- **Only four failed.** Just four of the 104 let him down; after repaying the loan he had a handsome profit. (Morningstar · Irish Times, 2008)
- **Why war helped.** He reasoned that in wartime, things that were in surplus and unprofitable become scarce and profitable. (Morningstar)
- **A borrowed stake.** He borrowed the money, a big risk for a young man; he spread it across 104 companies so no single failure could sink him. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-02-the-1939-basket.html

### 3. Search the whole world

He looked for bargains in every country, not just at home. That was rare in the 1950s, and it gave him far more choices.

- **A global fund.** He launched the Templeton Growth Fund in 1954, incorporated in Canada, and it searched for bargains across the world. (Obituary, 2008)
- **Japan.** He bought large holdings in Japan while its economy was still recovering from the war, and sold them in the boom years of the 1980s. (interactive investor)
- **More places, more bargains.** Diversify by country as well as industry: more markets mean more chances to find something cheap. (Rules, 1993)
- **Travel first.** After Oxford he spent seven months travelling the world with a friend, on very little money, before starting work in New York. (Irish Times, 2008)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-03-search-the-whole-world.html

### 4. Bargains among quality

Cheap is not enough. He looked for good businesses, with strong market positions and capable managers, at low prices.

- **Quality first.** Search for bargains among quality companies: a strong market position, capable management and enough capital. (Rules, 1993)
- **Earnings five years out.** He judged price against estimated earnings about five years ahead, and avoided shares above roughly 12 to 14 times. (LGT)
- **About four years.** He held shares for about four years on average. (LGT)
- **Ford near bankruptcy.** From 1978, when Ford looked headed for bankruptcy, he bought and kept buying as the shares fell further. By 1987 they were worth many times his price. (Morningstar)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-04-bargains-among-quality.html

### 5. Value, not forecasts

Buy individual companies on their own merits, not on what you think the economy or the market will do.

- **Not the outlook.** Buy value, not market trends or the economic outlook. Individual shares can move against the market. (Rules, 1993)
- **Do your homework.** Study a company carefully before you buy, or hire wise experts to do it for you. (Rules, 1993)
- **Flexible.** No type of investment is always best. Stay open to shares, bonds and cash, and hold cash when bargains are scarce. (Rules, 1993)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-05-value-not-forecasts.html

### 6. Diversify, and keep watching

There is safety in numbers. Spread your money by industry, by risk and by country, and keep checking what you own.

- **Safety in numbers.** Diversify by industry, by risk and by country, so that one setback cannot ruin you. (Rules, 1993)
- **Not buy and forget.** Monitor your investments: markets change, companies rise and fall, and no share should be bought and forgotten. (Rules, 1993 · Morningstar)
- **Real returns.** Aim for the best total return after tax and inflation; anything else is not a real return. (Rules, 1993)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-06-diversify-and-keep-watching.html

### 7. Don't panic

When markets crash, don't sell in fear. And don't be gloomy too often: over time, optimism has paid.

- **Hold your nerve.** In a crash, don't sell just because everyone else is. Sell only if you find something clearly more attractive. (Rules, 1993)
- **This time is different?** He warned against investors who think this time is different when it is really a repeat of the past. (Rules, 1993)
- **Not too fearful.** His last rule: do not be fearful or negative too often. Over decades, shares have risen despite many falls. (Rules, 1993)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-07-dont-panic.html

### 8. Selling into euphoria

The mirror of buying at maximum pessimism is selling at maximum optimism. In 2000, aged 87, he bet heavily against overpriced technology shares.

- **Insane prices.** He said technology shares had reached 100 times earnings, or 20 times sales where there were no earnings. (Morningstar)
- **The trade.** He bet against dozens of newly listed technology shares that had soared, timed for just before insiders were free to sell. (Financial Advisor)
- **The result.** According to his great-niece, he put about $185 million into these bets and made about $90 million. (Financial Advisor)
- **A rare move.** He usually bought; betting on falls was something he did only when prices looked truly absurd. (Book, 2008)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-08-selling-into-euphoria.html

### 9. Humility

Invest, don't speculate. Learn from mistakes, accept there is no free lunch, and never think you know it all.

- **Invest, don't trade.** Frequent trading and speculation tend to lose money in costs and mistimed moves. (Rules, 1993)
- **Learn from mistakes.** Study your errors, forgive yourself, and don't try to win losses back with bigger risks. (Rules, 1993)
- **No free lunch.** Don't invest on tips or sentiment; there is no free lunch. (Rules, 1993)
- **Begin with a prayer.** An active Presbyterian, he began meetings with a prayer, which he believed clears the mind and reduces mistakes. (Rules, 1993 · Irish Times, 2008)
- **All the answers.** An investor who has all the answers doesn't even understand all the questions. (Rules, 1993)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/templeton-09-humility.html

### 10. Distance, thrift and giving

He did his best work far from Wall Street, lived frugally, and gave much of his fortune to the study of the spiritual side of life.

- **Away from the noise.** His record improved after he moved away from Wall Street to the Bahamas in 1968, where he worked largely by fax. (Blog · Irish Times, 2008)
- **Thrift.** He paid part of his way through Yale with poker winnings after the Depression hit his father, and in the Bahamas he drove old cars. (Irish Times, 2008)
- **The Prize.** In 1972 he created the Templeton Prize, meant as a Nobel-style award for progress in religion. (AP, 2008)
- **Giving.** He was knighted in 1987 for his philanthropy, and set up the John Templeton Foundation the same year. (Obituary, 2008 · AP, 2008)

**His 16 rules, in brief**

- Aim for real returns, after tax and inflation.
- Invest; don't trade or speculate.
- Stay flexible about types of investment.
- Buy low.
- Look for bargains among quality companies.
- Buy value, not trends or forecasts.
- Do your homework, or hire wise experts.

- Don't panic.

- Begin with a prayer.

- Don't be fearful or negative too often.

## Words to know

- **Contrarian**: Deliberately going against the crowd.
- **Bargain hunting**: His own name for his style: buying shares far below what they are worth.
- **Real return**: Your return after tax and inflation.
- **Short selling**: Borrowing shares to sell them, hoping to buy them back later at a lower price.
- **Lock-up**: A period after a share's first listing when insiders may not sell.
- **Initial public offering (IPO)**: A company's first sale of shares to the public.
- **Mutual fund**: A pooled fund that invests many people's money together.
- **Diversification**: Spreading money across many holdings to reduce the harm of any one mistake.
