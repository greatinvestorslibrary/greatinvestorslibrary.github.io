# Paul Tudor Jones

> Lost most of his clients' money on one cotton trade in 1979, built his rules around defence, and made a reported 125.9% after fees in 1987, the year of the crash.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/ptj.html · Section: the traders · Updated 2026-11-28

**For adults (18+). Trading is risky.** This page is about a professional trader who uses futures, leverage and derivatives. It is for education, not a trading course or a recommendation. Most individuals who trade lose money. In India, SEBI found that 93% of individual equity F&O traders lost money between FY22 and FY24. Past results, his or anyone's, do not predict future ones. Never trade with money you cannot afford to lose.

## Core idea

Defence first. Protect what you have; profits follow.

## Summary

Paul Tudor Jones learned to trade in the cotton pits of the 1970s, nearly wiped himself out in 1979, and decided never again to risk everything on one trade. He founded Tudor Investment in 1980, expected the 1987 crash, and his main fund is reported to have made 125.9% after fees that year, the year the market fell 22.6% in a single day. His rules are about defence first: cut losses, never add to losers, and look for trades that could pay five times what they risk.

## Record at a glance

- **125.9%**: his flagship fund's reported gain after fees in 1987, the crash year
- **14 for 14**: years up in the employee fund he managed alone: part of 1990 (from July), then every year 1991 to 2003
- **5:1**: the reward-to-risk ratio he looks for before taking a trade
- **200 days**: the moving average he uses as his line between offence and defence

## Big ideas

### 1. From Memphis to the cotton pit

After university a relative in the cotton business sent him to the great cotton trader Eli Tullis, who hired him and taught him on the floor of the New York Cotton Exchange.

- **Memphis.** He was born in Memphis, Tennessee, in 1954 and graduated from the University of Virginia in 1976. (Wikipedia)
- **An introduction.** Billy Dunavant, a cotton merchant in his family (he calls him his uncle; Wikipedia says cousin), sent him to Eli Tullis in New Orleans. (Robbins, 2014 · Wikipedia)
- **The floor.** Tullis offered him a job on the floor of the New York Cotton Exchange. (Market Wizards)
- **Fired.** Tullis later fired him for falling asleep at his desk after a night out in New Orleans. (Wikipedia)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-01-from-memphis-to-the-cotton-pit.html

### 2. Mr. Stupid: the 1979 cotton loss

In 1979 a cotton trade went badly wrong and he lost 60 to 70% of his clients' money. It nearly ended his career and shaped every rule he made afterwards.

- **The loss.** He traded too many cotton contracts; the market went against him, costing 60 to 70% of the accounts he managed. (Benzinga · 7 Circles)
- **The question.** He said the trade nearly broke him, and asked himself why he would risk everything on one trade. (Market Wizards)
- **The change.** He decided to make trading a pursuit of happiness rather than pain, and to learn discipline and money management. (Market Wizards · 7 Circles)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-02-mr-stupid-the-1979-cotton-loss.html

### 3. Tudor Investment, 1980

In 1980 he started his own firm. Dunavant and Tullis were among his first clients, and Commodities Corporation gave him $30,000 to manage.

- **1980.** He founded Tudor Investment Corporation at 26. (Wikipedia)
- **First clients.** Dunavant and Tullis were among his first clients; Commodities Corporation gave him $30,000 to manage. (Wikipedia)
- **Long practice.** SEC filings say he traded for his own account from 1977 and for customers from 1981. (SEC, 2004)
- **A one-man fund.** For the employee fund on this page he was the only person making trading decisions. (SEC, 2004)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-03-tudor-investment-1980.html

### 4. 1987: the crash he expected

In 1987 his deputy Peter Borish mapped that year's market against the run-up to the 1929 crash, and the two expected a collapse. In October the Dow fell 22.6% in a day. His main fund is reported to have made 125.9% that year.

- **On camera.** The 1987 PBS documentary Trader showed him, at 32, managing $125 million; he later persuaded PBS to stop showing it. (CNBC)
- **The 1929 map.** Peter Borish laid the 1987 market over the market before the 1929 crash. (Wikipedia)
- **Flat at the top.** He told Tony Robbins that prices had fallen below his long-term trend line, and he was out at the top. (Robbins, 2014)
- **Black Monday.** On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% to 1,738.74, its worst day ever. (24/7 Wall St, 2020 · CNBC)
- **The month and the year.** Market Wizards reported 62% for his Tudor Futures Fund in October alone; his main fund reportedly made 125.9% after fees in 1987. (GuruFocus · CNBC, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-04-1987-the-crash-he-expected.html

### 5. Defence first

His most repeated lesson: protect what you have before trying to make more. Never add to a losing position.

- **Losers average losers.** Never add to a position that is losing money. (Benzinga)
- **Trade smaller when it hurts.** Cut your trading when you are trading badly; increase it when you are trading well. (Benzinga)
- **Stay in control.** Avoid trades where you cannot control your risk. (Benzinga)
- **A shallow worst month.** In nearly 14 years of SEC-filed monthly results, the employee fund's worst month was a fall of 9.62%, in July 1990. (SEC, 2004)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-05-defence-first.html

### 6. Five to one

He looks for trades that could make five times what they risk. That means he can be wrong most of the time and still come out ahead.

- **The ratio.** He risks one dollar to make five. (Robbins, 2014)
- **The arithmetic.** At five to one, he says, you can win just 20% of the time; one win pays for four losses, with a little left over. (Robbins, 2014 · 25iq)
- **Humility built in.** The rule assumes you will often be wrong, and plans for it. (Robbins, 2014 · 25iq)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-06-five-to-one.html

### 7. The 200-day line

He watches the 200-day moving average of closing prices. If something falls below it, he gets out and plays defence.

- **His metric.** The 200-day moving average of closing prices is his metric for everything. (Robbins, 2014 · 25iq)
- **Why.** He has seen too many stocks and commodities go to zero. (25iq)
- **The rule.** Get out of anything that falls below it. (Robbins, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-07-the-200-day-line.html

### 8. Everything is connected

He is a global macro trader: he reads currencies, bonds, stocks and commodities as one system and bets on big shifts.

- **Directional and short-term.** SEC filings describe his approach as directional, short-term and discretionary, guided by strict risk control. (SEC, 2004)
- **Japan, 1990.** He bought put options on Japan's stock market in February 1990; his main fund reportedly made 87.4% that year as the market collapsed. (GuruFocus, 2017 · CNBC, 2014)
- **The cotton chair.** He chaired the New York Cotton Exchange from 1992 to 1995. (SEC, 2004)
- **The fastest horse.** In May 2020 he argued that money printing could bring inflation, and said his main fund might trade bitcoin futures as a hedge. (The Block, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-08-everything-is-connected.html

### 9. Two eras

His flagship fund averaged about 26% a year from 1987 to 2007, then about 4.7% a year from 2008 to 2016. Even legends have long flat stretches.

- **The golden era.** Investor documents showed about 26% a year from 1987 through 2007. (Bloomberg, 2017)
- **The slowdown.** From 2008 through 2016 the average fell to about 4.7% a year, and clients withdrew money. (Bloomberg, 2017)
- **2008.** In the financial crisis his fund lost nearly 5%, about 32 points better than the S&P 500. (GuruFocus, 2017)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-09-two-eras.html

### 10. Robin Hood, and a reputation

After the 1987 crash he co-founded the Robin Hood Foundation to fight poverty. His record also includes a guilty plea over wetlands, an SEC settlement, and words he later regretted.

- **Robin Hood.** After the crash, which hurt so many others, he and friends set up the Robin Hood Foundation, in 1988. (Bridgespan · Wikipedia)
- **Wetlands, 1990.** He pleaded guilty after protected wetlands on his Maryland hunting estate were filled without a permit, paying a $1 million fine and $1 million to a wildlife foundation. (NWF, 1993)
- **An SEC settlement.** In 1996 Tudor paid $800,000 to settle SEC claims about short sales in 1994, without admitting or denying them. (SEC, 1996)
- **Regrets.** In 2013 he apologised for saying motherhood hurts women's trading; in 2017 he said he had defended Harvey Weinstein too long. (NPR, 2013 · CNBC, 2017)
- **Know the odds.** Most individuals who try trading lose money, as SEBI's 2024 study showed in India. (SEBI, 2024)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/ptj-10-robin-hood-and-a-reputation.html

## Special section

**What does five to one really mean?** Put in how often your trades win and how much they make compared with what they risk. The calculator shows the average result per trade and the win rate you need just to break even.

**His rules, in short**

- Defence first: protect what you have.
- Never average losers: do not add to a losing trade.
- Trade smaller when it is going badly, bigger when it is going well.
- Look for five to one: risk one to make five.
- Respect the 200-day line: below it, get out.
- Never risk everything on one trade.

**Defence or offence? Tap your answer**

- A trade is down 20%. You buy more so your average price is lower. Answer: Offence gone wrong. Adding to a loser is exactly what he calls averaging losers.
- Before you enter, you know where you will get out if you are wrong, and the target is five times that distance. Answer: Defence. Knowing your exit and your reward-to-risk first is his 5:1 discipline.
- Your account is down three weeks running, so you double your position size to win it back. Answer: Offence gone wrong. He does the opposite: trade smaller when trading badly.
- An index you hold falls below its 200-day average. You sell and wait. Answer: Defence. That is his 200-day rule: below the line, play defence.

**An honest note.** In 1990 he pleaded guilty in a federal case after 86 acres of protected wetlands on his Maryland hunting estate were filled without a permit; he paid a $1 million fine and $1 million to the National Fish and Wildlife Foundation. In September 1996 Tudor Investment Corporation settled an SEC proceeding about alleged breaches of the 'uptick rule' in short sales over two days in March 1994, paying $800,000 without admitting or denying the findings. In April 2002 a private lawsuit claiming 'short-swing' profits under Section 16(b) was settled, with the Tudor defendants paying $1.45 million. In 2013 he apologised for saying that motherhood keeps women from being great traders, and in 2017, after a supportive email he had sent Harvey Weinstein was reported, he told staff he had believed and defended Weinstein too long. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

## Words to know

- **Futures**: Contracts to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date; traded with borrowed money (leverage).
- **Leverage**: Using borrowed money so gains and losses are larger than your own money alone.
- **Short selling**: Selling something you do not own, hoping to buy it back cheaper.
- **Global macro**: Trading on big economic shifts across currencies, bonds, stocks and commodities.
- **Moving average**: The average closing price over a set number of days, redrawn each day.
- **Reward-to-risk**: How much a trade could make compared with how much it could lose.
- **Drawdown**: How far an account has fallen from its last high point.
- **Uptick rule**: A US rule, since changed, limiting short sales to prices above the last trade.
