# Wahei Takeda

> Made Japan's favourite baby biscuit, then became a patient shareholder in over a hundred companies, treating each as a field to tend.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/takeda.html · Section: Japan · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Investing, not speculating. Owning a company to support it is not the same as betting on its price.

## Summary

Wahei Takeda built Takeda Seika into Japan's biggest maker of tamago boro, the little egg biscuits Japanese babies grow up on. From 1956 he invested the company's spare cash, and after a painful lesson moved to small and mid-sized companies, becoming a large shareholder in more than a hundred of them. Japan called him its Warren Buffett. He called being a shareholder being the company's danna, its patron.

## Record at a glance

- **1956**: the year he began investing the company's spare cash, at 23
- **100+**: listed companies in which he became a large individual shareholder
- **P/E ≈ 10**: the price he liked to pay: about ten times earnings
- **Gold**: medals he sent companies to thank them for their dividends

## Big ideas

### 1. The biscuit maker

He was a confectioner first. He turned a sweet sold loose by weight into a bagged, machine-made product, and built Japan's biggest tamago boro maker.

- **A confectioner's son.** Born in Aichi in 1933 to a family of sweet makers, he was his father's right hand while still a teenager after the war. (MUFG, 2024)
- **A simple idea.** Tamago boro was sold loose by weight in sweet shops. He saw it could be sold in bags, and automated production before rivals did. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Takeda Seika.** He founded the company in 1952; it became Japan's largest tamago boro maker, also known for the Mugi Fah wafer. (Obituary, 2016 · MUFG, 2024)
- **A poor craftsman.** His interviewer described him as a once-poor confectioner who became Japan's biggest individual investor. (Book, 2008)

### 2. From speculator to investor

He began by trading famous shares for quick profit. Later he called that speculation, and drew a sharp line between betting on prices and owning a business to support it.

- **Spare cash.** In 1956, at 23, once the company was stable, he began investing its surplus cash. (MUFG, 2024)
- **A friend's tip.** A friendship with a securities branch manager led to his first purchase, which rose quickly. (Interview, 2015)
- **Short-term at first.** He started with big blue chips and traded for quick gains, which he later called speculation, not investment. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Similar, not the same.** Gambling-style speculation and investing to support a company look alike, he said, but are different things. (Interview, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-02-from-speculator-to-investor.html

### 3. The lesson of a collapse

In the 1990s a large company in which he was the top individual shareholder went bankrupt, its managers insisting all was well to the end. He sold his large companies and turned to smaller ones he could understand.

- **Fine until it wasn't.** Management kept saying the business was fine right up to the bankruptcy. (MUFG, 2024)
- **A clean break.** He sold all his large-company shares and moved to small and mid-sized listed companies. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Faces, not size.** He came to prefer companies where you can see the manager's face over big, impressive ones. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Plain accounts.** He valued simple, easy-to-read financial statements and knowing the management directly. (MUFG, 2024)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-03-the-lesson-of-a-collapse.html

### 4. Be the danna

In old Japan a danna was a patron who stood behind a business or an artist. He said that becoming a shareholder means becoming the company's danna.

- **His motto.** To be a shareholder is to be the company's danna, its patron. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Nurture, then wait.** He held with a nurturing attitude, waiting for the price to rise; if it didn't, he was content with dividends from a cheap purchase. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Cheer them on.** He encouraged struggling managers to run their business joyfully and recover, so dividends would follow. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Gold for thanks.** To thank companies for their dividends he sent them pure gold medals, a habit he kept all his life. (MUFG, 2024)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-04-be-the-danna.html

### 5. Fields and harvests

He saw investing as farming. Shares are the field and dividends the harvest. A good farmer watches the harvest, not the price of the land.

- **The farm.** Stocks are the field and dividends the harvest; watch the yearly harvest, not the price of the land. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Sow and replant.** Plant seeds in companies likely to bloom, take your share of the fruit, then plant again. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Never withdrawn.** He reinvested his dividends again and again, and his holdings grew past 10 billion yen. (MUFG, 2024)
- **The right season.** His interviewer titled the book with a gardener's rule: don't sow flower seeds in midsummer. (Book, 2008)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-05-fields-and-harvests.html

### 6. Cheap, sound and generous

He wanted shares that were cheap against earnings, companies with strong balance sheets, and managers who shared profits with shareholders.

- **About ten times.** Ideally he paid about ten times earnings, which he linked to a roughly 10% earnings yield. (Interview, 2015)
- **Two numbers.** He watched the payout ratio, how much profit goes to dividends, and the equity ratio, a sign of financial strength. (MUFG, 2024)
- **Skill shows.** He used return on equity to find companies that were both profitable and cheap. (Interview, 2015)
- **Never overpay.** He warned that buying overpriced shares always loses money in the end. (Interview, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-06-cheap-sound-and-generous.html

### 7. Dividends first

Dividends, not price gains, were the core of his returns. He noted that dividends hold up far better than prices in bad times.

- **The main source.** Dividends were his main return; in 2015 he expected about 350 million yen of tax on them. (Interview, 2015)
- **Steadier than prices.** Even if a share price halved, he said, dividends usually fell by no more than about 10%. (Interview, 2015)
- **Steady growth.** He aimed for companies with steady future growth that would pay good dividends. (Profile, 2015)
- **Glad for them.** He chose companies whose rising profits he could be truly happy about. (Profile, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-07-dividends-first.html

### 8. Calm in storms

He used only spare money, never borrowed to the limit, and refused to panic. A share price, he said, may go up or down; either is fine.

- **Up or down.** He shrugged off falls with a short verse: a share price is fine going down and fine going up. (Interview, 2015)
- **Lehman.** In the 2008 crash his holdings showed large paper losses, and he held on. (Interview, 2015)
- **Spare money only.** He invested only surplus funds and warned never to use margin to the limit. (Interview, 2015)
- **One book.** He said his only information source was the Shikiho company handbook, with no special inside sources. (Interview, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-08-calm-in-storms.html

### 9. When to sell

He held for the long run, but sold when a share looked overvalued, and lost patience with companies that hoarded cash instead of rewarding owners.

- **No small gains.** He held for the long term and did not sell quickly to lock in small profits. (Interview, 2015)
- **No greed.** Sell when a share looks overvalued; don't wait for the top. (Interview, 2015)
- **Hoarders.** Profitable companies that keep piling up retained earnings show poor prospects and little respect for shareholders, he said. (Interview, 2015)
- **Fewer, later.** At 82 he had cut from over 130 holdings to about 25, citing a proposed tax on large holders, the need for global reach, and the time research takes. (Interview, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-09-when-to-sell.html

### 10. Sincerity, and giving back

He believed sincerity passed from person to person brings success and happiness, ran a circle to develop young people, and gave back much of what he gained.

- **A chain of sincerity.** His philosophy: a chain of sincerity brings success and happiness. (Interview, 2015)
- **Marowa-ko.** He led a group called Marowa-ko to develop people, and returned his experience and wealth to society. (MUFG, 2024)
- **A hundred heroes in gold.** He had gold coins struck of a hundred figures from Japanese history, displayed in a hall he planned in Nagoya. (Talk, 2011 · His book, 2005)
- **Remembered.** He died on 21 July 2016, aged 83; fans thanked him for the biscuits and investors called him a model. (Obituary, 2016)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/takeda-10-sincerity-and-giving-back.html

## Special section

**Would he be this company's danna?**

- The share is cheap: around ten times earnings, or less
- It pays a healthy share of its profit as dividends
- Its equity ratio is strong: little reliance on debt
- You can see the manager's face, and you know who runs it
- Its accounts are simple enough to read yourself
- Its success would make you genuinely glad
- It does not pile up cash it cannot use
- You are buying with spare money, not borrowed money

**The farmer's year**

- Sow in companies likely to bloom, when the season is right.
- Tend as a patron: encourage, don't harass.
- Harvest the dividends, year after year.
- Replant the harvest, and let it grow.

## Words to know

- **Danna (旦那)**: A patron who supports a business or an artist; also a master of a household.
- **Tamago boro**: Small egg biscuits that melt in the mouth, a classic Japanese baby snack.
- **Shikiho (会社四季報)**: Japan's quarterly handbook of listed companies, a staple for individual investors.
- **Payout ratio**: The share of after-tax profit a company pays out as dividends.
- **Equity ratio**: Shareholders' equity as a share of total assets; higher means sturdier.
- **P/E**: Price to earnings: the share price divided by yearly earnings per share.
- **Retained earnings**: Profits kept in the company rather than paid out (naibu ryūho).
- **Margin trading**: Buying shares with borrowed money.
