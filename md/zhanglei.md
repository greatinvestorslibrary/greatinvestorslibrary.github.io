# Zhang Lei

> Learned investing at Yale under David Swensen, founded Hillhouse with $20 million, and preaches long-term bets on great entrepreneurs.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/zhanglei.html · Section: China · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Long-termism. Put time and conviction into things that create value over many years.

## Summary

Zhang Lei learned investing as an intern under David Swensen at Yale's endowment, then founded Hillhouse in 2005 with $20 million from Yale. He backed Tencent early, put $300 million into JD.com in 2010, and built one of Asia's largest investment firms. His creed: think long term, research deeply, back entrepreneurs with great vision, and be a friend of time.

## Record at a glance

- **$20m**: Yale's seed money when he founded Hillhouse in 2005
- **$2.4bn**: gains Hillhouse had made for Yale by 2020, according to David Swensen
- **$300m**: invested in JD.com in 2010, in one go
- **¥41.66bn**: paid in 2019 for 15% of Gree Electric; the shares were still below that price in early 2026

## Big ideas

### 1. Long-termism

Put your time and conviction into things that create value over many years. A long horizon is an edge from the starting line.

- **His compass.** Long-termism means putting time and conviction into things that keep creating value, learning the most effective ways of thinking, and always seeking the truth. (Preface, 2020)
- **An edge from the start.** A long horizon gives you an advantage before you begin, because so few investors can afford to wait. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Not about the shortest path.** Yale taught him that investing is not a race for quick returns but working with the best people to build lasting organisations. (Yale Daily News)
- **Holding long is a result.** Long holding periods are the result of backing companies that keep creating value, not a goal in themselves. (Book, 2020)
- **A friend of time.** He sums up his approach in three phrases: long-termism, walking with people of great vision, and being a friend of time. (Book, 2020)

### 2. Swensen's lessons

David Swensen was his first teacher. He learned what it means to be a fiduciary, to think truly long term, and to build an organisation with a soul.

- **A student who couldn't pay.** Short of money for his second year at Yale, he got an internship in Swensen's office. His first task was translating Swensen's book into Chinese. (Tribute, 2021)
- **Hundreds of basic questions.** Swensen answered hundreds of his questions, on the trading floor and over squash and softball. (Tribute, 2021)
- **Fiduciary.** Swensen showed him what a fiduciary is: someone who puts the interests of those who trust him first. He struggled to find a Chinese word for it. (Tribute, 2021)
- **A bet on a person.** Yale backed him when he was 32 and had never managed a portfolio. Swensen's reason: work with the right people and good things tend to follow. (Tribute, 2021)
- **A soul.** Swensen spoke of organisations with a soul, driven by a mission. Zhang came to believe such teams innovate more and last longer. (Tribute, 2021)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-02-swensens-lessons.html

### 3. Research is the base

Deep research comes first, and few bets. Understand real businesses the way a field researcher studies a village.

- **Few, deep bets.** Make deep fundamental research the basis of every decision, and place a few bets rather than chase many ideas. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Like fieldwork.** Value investing is not maths on paper. Like a sociologist doing fieldwork, you must see how people really live and buy. (Book, 2020)
- **Generalists.** His team works as generalists, and he counts himself one of the analysts. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Ask the basic questions.** Success comes down to stripping an investment to its most basic questions. (CFA Institute, 2018)
- **Ahead of the crowd.** Qiu Guolu recalls Zhang convening top scientists in 2015 on cancer immunotherapy, before most Chinese investors knew the field, and backing BeiGene in eight rounds. (Review, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-03-research-is-the-base.html

### 4. People first

Hillhouse's growth came mainly from people: the managers it hires and the entrepreneurs it backs.

- **Owners aligned with customers.** Before investing, find out whether owners, directors and managers are long-term, intelligent and aligned with their customers' needs. (CFA Institute, 2018)
- **Who he hires.** Intelligence, independent thinking and honesty count for more than experience or background. (CFA Institute, 2018)
- **Three intellectual habits.** Curiosity, independence and honesty, plus empathy: qualities he looks for in people. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Quality time with quality people.** Swensen's reason for backing him became Hillhouse's motto: spend quality time with quality people. (Tribute, 2021)
- **Entrepreneurs who invest.** He describes Hillhouse as entrepreneurs who happen to be investors. (Lecture, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-04-people-first.html

### 5. Backing great vision

He looks for entrepreneurs with great vision, which he defines in four parts.

- **Four signs.** Long-term thinking amid uncertainty; deep insight into the industry; focused execution; and strong empathy that brings people and resources together. (Excerpt, 2020)
- **The secret handshake.** He calls great vision Hillhouse's secret handshake with companies: founders who want to build something great make the best partners. (Excerpt, 2020)
- **Ten or twenty years.** Good entrepreneurs see ten or twenty years ahead, sometimes beyond their own lives. (Excerpt, 2020)
- **Choose the person, then the project.** When Swensen backed him, it was a bet on a person. Zhang applies the same logic to founders. (Tribute, 2021 · Excerpt, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-05-backing-great-vision.html

### 6. Moats must keep moving

Static moats erode. The lasting edge belongs to companies that keep innovating and rebuilding their advantage.

- **A dynamic moat.** Long-termism helps companies escape zero-sum games and keep rebuilding a dynamic moat through constant innovation. (Preface, 2020)
- **Value investing evolves.** As markets grow more efficient, deeply undervalued companies become rare. Investors must find new, forward-looking drivers of value. (Book, 2020)
- **Invest in change.** He looks for industries being changed by technology and new ways of doing business. (Lecture, 2015 · Review, 2020)
- **Technology and tradition.** He invests in both new technology and traditional industries, public or private, large or small. (CFA Institute, 2018)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-06-moats-must-keep-moving.html

### 7. Create value, don't just find it

Hillhouse tries to help good companies get better, rather than planning the exit first.

- **The exit looks after itself.** Unlike buyout firms that plan the exit first and lean on debt and cost cuts, he backs good people and solid cash flow and lets the exit take care of itself. (CFA Institute, 2018)
- **Belle.** In 2017 Hillhouse led the $6.8 billion purchase of the shoe retailer Belle, aiming to use technology for online sales, stock control and better service in shops. (CFA Institute, 2018)
- **Not the format, the substance.** With freedom to invest in public shares, venture and private equity, he focuses on the substance of a deal, not its form. (Lecture, 2015)
- **Go big when you know.** In 2010 he put $300 million into JD.com in one go, backed by years of studying retail around the world. (Review, 2020)

### 8. Fire cannot burn it

As a student he decided that what fire can burn, money and houses, matters least. What lasts is knowledge, ability and values.

- **Three fires.** Things fire can burn, such as money and houses, are not what matters. What matters is what fire cannot burn: knowledge, ability and values. (Book, 2020)
- **First principles.** Don't reason by analogy. Peel back the surface to see what a thing really is, then build up from there. (Book, 2020)
- **Education never ends.** He has given generously to Yale and, inspired by Swensen, founded an institute to teach long-term investing. (Yale Daily News · Tribute, 2021)
- **A teacher's example.** Swensen saw teaching as one of his most important duties; Zhang took that as the clearest sign of a long-term mind. (Tribute, 2021)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-08-fire-cannot-burn-it.html

### 9. Patience with big bets

Long-term bets can stay underwater for years. Gree shows both the patience and the risk.

- **Gree.** In 2019 a Hillhouse fund paid 41.66 billion yuan, partly borrowed, for 15% of Gree Electric and became its biggest shareholder. (Caixin, 2019–26)
- **Still underwater.** By early 2026 Gree's shares were nearly 20% below the purchase price, a paper loss partly offset by about 15 billion yuan of dividends. (Caixin, 2019–26)
- **Debt has a cost.** In 2026 Hillhouse planned to sell about 2% of Gree to repay bank loans from the deal. (Caixin, 2019–26)
- **Yale's gain.** By 2020, Swensen said, Hillhouse had made about $2.4 billion of gains for Yale. (Bloomberg, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/zhanglei-09-patience-with-big-bets.html

### 10. The LONGi investigation

In 2023 China's securities regulator investigated a Hillhouse unit over undisclosed share sales. Here are the facts, so you can judge for yourself.

- **What happened.** HHLR, Hillhouse's public-markets unit, owned 5.85% of the solar company LONGi. In March 2023 it lent out shares, taking its stake below 5%, the level above which sales must be announced. (News, 2023–24)
- **The gap.** When the loan ended, its stake was only 4.98%, implying it had sold about 0.9% of the company without an announcement. (News, 2023–24)
- **The probe.** In November 2023 the regulator opened an investigation for suspected breaches of share-transfer rules, and the exchanges soon closed the loophole. (News, 2023–24)
- **The response.** In April 2024 HHLR began buying the shares back, promising to hand any gain to LONGi. We have found no published final penalty decision. (News, 2023–24)

## Special section

**Four signs of an entrepreneur with great vision**

- Long-termism: seeking the long run amid uncertainty.
- Deep insight: into the industry, spotting key trends through constant innovation.
- Focused execution: doing things to the highest standard.
- Strong empathy: bringing people and resources together to make ideas real.

**What he looks for in people**

- Curiosity.
- Independence of mind.
- Intellectual honesty.
- Empathy.
- Long-term and aligned with customers, for the owners and managers he backs.

**An honest note.** In November 2023 China's securities regulator opened an investigation into HHLR, the public-markets unit of Hillhouse. HHLR owned 5.85% of the solar company LONGi. In March 2023 it lent out shares, taking its stake below 5%, the level above which sales must be announced; when the loan ended its stake was only 4.98%, implying it had sold about 0.9% of the company without an announcement. In April 2024 HHLR began buying the shares back and promised to hand any gain to LONGi. The case concerned the rules on announcing share sales; we have seen no allegation that clients' money was misused, and we have found no published final penalty decision. ## Words to know

- **Endowment**: A fund given to a university or charity and invested so it can pay for its work for ever.
- **Fiduciary**: Someone trusted to act in another person's interest, before their own.
- **Private equity**: Buying stakes in companies, often unlisted, usually with a view to improving them.
- **Venture capital**: Investing in young companies in return for a stake.
- **Take-private**: Buying all the shares of a listed company so it leaves the stock market.
- **Dynamic moat**: A competitive edge that a company keeps renewing through innovation, rather than a fixed advantage.
- **First principles**: Reasoning from what a thing fundamentally is, rather than by comparison with something else.
- **Disclosure threshold**: The shareholding, 5% in China, above which an investor must announce changes in its stake.
- **Securities lending**: Lending shares to another investor for a fee, to be returned later.
