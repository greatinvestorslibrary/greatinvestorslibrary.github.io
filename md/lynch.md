# Peter Lynch

> Ran the Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, beat the market by a mile, and showed ordinary people how to find good stocks.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/lynch.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

An ordinary person who keeps their eyes open often spots a good company years before the professionals, but must still do the homework.

## Summary

Peter Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, averaging 29.2% a year, about double the S&P 500. He retired at 46 and spent the following decades teaching ordinary people to pick stocks the way he did: look around, sort, check, explain, then own.

## Record at a glance

- **13 years**: May 1977 to May 1990
- **29.2%**: average a year, about double the S&P 500
- **$18M → $14B**: fund size when he started and left
- **$1,000 → $28,000**: what $1,000 became over his tenure

## Big ideas

### 1. The amateur's edge

An ordinary person who keeps their eyes open often spots a good company years before the professionals do.

- **You start ahead.** He argued that any normal person can pick stocks as well as the average Wall Street expert, using what they already see at work and in the shops. (1989 One Up I)
- **Professionals play it safe.** Many fund managers fear looking bad more than they want to win, so they avoid small, unknown companies until everyone already knows them. (1989 One Up I)
- **Your edge is where you work and shop.** Your job, your industry and the products you use every day can show you which companies are winning before the numbers reach Wall Street. (1989 One Up I · 1993 Beating the Street)
- **Spotting is only the start.** He never said to buy a stock just because you like the product or the store. You still have to study the company's earnings, finances and competition. (2000 New intro)
- **Know what you own.** He called this the single most important thing, and reckoned about 80% of people own stocks they couldn't explain. (1994 Press Club)
- **Step one, then step two.** In 2023 he repeated that he never told people simply to put money into the market. Knowing a company is step one; reading its balance sheet is step two. (2023 Interview)

### 2. Is it gambling? Stocks and your stomach

Stocks reward patient owners of good companies; the real test is your stomach, not your brain.

- **Owning, not betting.** Stocks are a gamble only if you pick them like lottery tickets. Behind every stock is a business, and businesses that earn more become worth more. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Why stocks beat bonds.** With a bond, the best you can hope for is your money back plus interest. Over the long run, stocks with dividends returned about 11% a year, against 5 to 6% for bonds. (1989 One Up I · 1996 PBS interview)
- **Profits are the engine.** Company profits have grown about 8% a year over time, which is why stocks rise in the long run, even after flat decades. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Bad picks are worse than cash.** Over the long run, a portfolio of poorly chosen stocks won't beat money left under the mattress. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **The stomach decides.** Markets fall often: he counted 53 declines of 10% or more in 95 years, 15 of them of 25% or more. If you can't sit through that, don't own stocks. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Only money you won't need.** Buy, hold and add when the market falls, but only with money you won't need soon. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Temperament as much as homework.** If a sharp drop would keep you awake at night, he said in 2023, you shouldn't be investing in stocks. (2023 Interview)
- **Start with how the world works.** His beginner's book opens with the history of companies and capitalism, because investing makes sense only once you see what a company is. (1995 Learn to Earn)

### 3. Look in your own backyard

The best leads come from everyday life: the store you like, the product people keep buying, the trend at your job.

- **Start close to home.** He believed the best place to start looking for the next winner is your own backyard: home, work and the shops. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Watch what people keep buying.** A product that friends, family or colleagues keep coming back to is a clue worth following up. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **If you like the store…** One of his principles: if you like the store, chances are you'll love the stock. Then check the numbers. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Small companies, big gains.** His biggest wins often came from small companies with room to grow. In 2023 he cited Au Bon Pain, which bought St. Louis Bread for $23 million in 1993; it later sold for $7.5 billion. (2023 Interview)
- **A hit product is not enough.** The Cabbage Patch doll was a huge hit, but it couldn't save a mediocre company with a bad balance sheet. (1989 One Up I)
- **Turn over more rocks.** The more companies you look at, the more bargains you find. His early winners came from industries others ignored. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Know a few well.** An individual can do well by knowing three to five companies thoroughly and buying when one becomes attractive. (1996 PBS interview)

### 4. The six kinds of stock

Before buying, decide which of six kinds of stock you are looking at; that tells you what to expect and when to sell.

- **Sort before you buy.** Every company fits one of six kinds: slow growers, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclicals, turnarounds and asset plays. (1989 One Up II)
- **Slow growers.** Large, ageing companies growing about as fast as the economy. You own them mainly for the dividend: low risk, low gain. (1989 One Up II)
- **Stalwarts.** Big, established companies growing earnings about 10 to 12% a year: low risk, moderate gain. He expected perhaps 30 to 50% from them, and liked them for protection in recessions. (1989 One Up II)
- **Fast growers.** Small, aggressive companies growing 20 to 25% a year. His favourites and the land of ten- to forty-baggers, but the riskiest when growth stops. (1989 One Up II)
- **Cyclicals.** Companies whose profits rise and fall with the economy. With these, timing matters more than anywhere else. (1989 One Up II)
- **Turnarounds.** Battered companies that might recover. They can make big gains, as Chrysler did in the early 1980s, but many never come back. (1989 One Up II · 1996 PBS interview)
- **Asset plays.** Companies sitting on something valuable the market has missed, such as property, cash or a business inside the business. (1989 One Up II)
- **Kinds change.** Fast growers eventually slow down and become stalwarts or slow growers, so check the label again from time to time. (1989 One Up II)

### 5. The perfect stock, and the ones to avoid

He looked for dull, overlooked companies in boring niches, and stayed away from the hottest story in town.

- **Dull is beautiful.** The perfect company has a boring, even ridiculous name and does something dull or disagreeable, so the crowd ignores it. (1989 One Up II)
- **Overlooked is better.** Big institutions don't own it and analysts don't follow it. A spinoff, or a company with a depressing or rumour-ridden image, often qualifies. (1989 One Up II)
- **Niche and repeat buyers.** A no-growth industry, a protected niche and a product people have to keep buying are all good signs. (1989 One Up II)
- **Insiders and buybacks.** When insiders buy their own shares, or the company buys back stock, management is showing confidence. Heavy insider buying makes a near-term bankruptcy very unlikely. (1989 One Up II)
- **Use technology, don't sell it.** He preferred companies that use technology to cut their costs over companies racing to sell it. (1989 One Up II)
- **Avoid the hottest stock.** The hottest stock in the hottest industry, the one everyone talks about, is the one he would avoid most. (1989 One Up II)
- **Beware “the next” and diworseification.** Stocks touted as the next big name rarely are, and profitable companies that buy unrelated businesses usually make themselves worse. (1989 One Up II)
- **Beware whispers and exciting names.** Long shots with thrilling stories and no earnings, and companies with exciting names in hot fields, attract buyers for the wrong reasons. (1989 One Up II)
- **Fewer colour photos.** Half joking: all else equal, invest in the company with the fewest colour photographs in its annual report. (1993 Beating the Street)

### 6. Earnings are the engine

Over months the price can do anything; over years it follows the company's earnings.

- **Short term, no link; long term, a full link.** Over months or even years there is often no connection between how a company does and how its stock does. Over the long term, the connection is complete. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Find what drives the profits.** Something has to keep the profits growing; the more you know about that something, the better. (1989 One Up II)
- **Price is not proof.** A rising price doesn't mean you were right, and a falling one doesn't mean you were wrong. Only the business can tell you that. (1989 One Up III)
- **Time favours good companies.** Time is on your side when you own shares of superior companies, and against you when you own options. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Even ten years late can be early.** Investors who bought Wal-Mart ten years after its 1970 listing could still have made about 30 times their money by following its expansion. (1996 PBS interview)
- **The stock doesn't know you own it.** Don't fall in love with a share. Stay while the company's fundamentals hold, and leave if they break. (1994 Press Club)

### 7. The famous numbers

A few simple numbers tell you whether a good company is also a good price.

- **P/E equal to growth is fair.** A fairly priced company's P/E roughly equals its earnings growth rate. A P/E well below the growth rate may be a bargain; well above it, a warning. (1989 One Up II)
- **Half is attractive, double is dear.** A P/E around half the growth rate looked very attractive to him; around twice the growth rate, very unattractive. (1989 One Up II)
- **Add the dividend.** For dividend payers, add the yield to the growth rate and divide by the P/E. Below 1 is poor, about 1.5 is okay, and 2 or better is what you want. (1989 One Up II)
- **Cash minus debt.** Check how much cash the company holds against its long-term debt. Plenty of net cash cushions the bad years. (1989 One Up II)
- **Who owns it?** The fewer institutions own it and the fewer analysts follow it, the more room there is for discovery. (1989 One Up II)
- **Do the arithmetic, even without earnings.** For a company with no profits yet, work out what it would need to earn later to justify today's price. (2000 New intro)
- **Cheap is not safe.** A $3 stock can go to zero just like a $50 one. A lousy cheap stock is as risky as a lousy expensive one. (1989 One Up III)

### 8. Balance sheet first

The biggest losses come from weak balance sheets; know a company's finances before you risk money on it.

- **Finances before faith.** Never invest in a company without understanding its finances. The biggest losses come from companies with poor balance sheets. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Two losers, one bargain.** In 2023 he compared two loss-making companies: one with $300 million of debt, one with no debt and $200 million in cash. (2023 Interview)
- **Debt decides who survives.** In a downturn, a company with little debt can wait; one with heavy debt may not. That matters most for turnarounds and cyclicals. (1993 Beating the Street · 2023 Interview)
- **Read the numbers yourself.** His beginner's book teaches how to read a stock table, an annual report and a balance sheet, before trusting anyone else's summary. (1995 Learn to Earn)
- **The homework list.** Before buying, check the earnings prospects, financial condition, competitive position and plans for expansion. (2000 New intro)
- **Fancy offices are a warning.** A lavish head office can signal managers who care more about themselves than about shareholders. (1993 Beating the Street)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lynch-08-balance-sheet-first.html

### 9. The two-minute drill

If you can't explain why you own a stock in two minutes, simply enough for a child, you don't know enough to own it.

- **Give the monologue.** Before buying, he talked himself through why he was interested, what had to happen for the company to succeed, and what could get in the way. (1989 One Up II)
- **Simple enough for a child.** If you can't explain to a ten-year-old, in two minutes or less, why you own a stock, he said, you shouldn't own it. (1994 Press Club)
- **The crayon test.** If you couldn't draw the idea with a crayon, he wouldn't invest in it. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Each kind has its own story.** For a slow grower the story is the dividend; for a fast grower, where it can still expand; for a turnaround, how it will fix itself; for an asset play, what the assets are worth. (1989 One Up II)
- **Write it down.** For beginners in 2023 he suggested a paper portfolio of at least ten stocks, a few written reasons for each, reviewed every three months. (2023 Interview)
- **“It's going up” is not a reason.** Pressed, many owners admit their real reason is that the stock is rising, which is no reason at all. (1994 Press Club)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lynch-09-the-two-minute-drill.html

### 10. Ten-baggers: let the winners run

You only need a few big winners in a lifetime, so let them run and don't cut the flowers to water the weeds.

- **A ten-bagger.** His baseball term for a stock that goes up tenfold: two home runs and a double. (1996 PBS interview)
- **A few are enough.** You don't need many. A handful of tenfold winners in a lifetime makes investing worthwhile. (1996 PBS interview · 1993 Beating the Street)
- **Right six times in ten.** A good stock picker is right about six times in ten, so the winners must be allowed to outgrow the losers. (1996 PBS interview · 2023 Interview)
- **Flowers and weeds.** Selling your winners and keeping your losers is like pulling out the flowers and watering the weeds. Buffett liked the image enough to use it in his 1988 letter. (1989 One Up III)
- **Add to the winners.** He did well by owning many stocks, letting winners run and adding to them, even though Magellan missed more than a hundred ten-baggers. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Look at what you own first.** The best stock to buy may be one you already own; check it before hunting for something new. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Lose 100%, gain 900%.** Whatever you pay, picking the wrong stock costs at most the same 100%. A ten-bagger gains 900%. (1989 One Up III)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lynch-10-ten-baggers-let-the-winners-run.html

### 11. Don't time the market

Nobody can predict the economy or the next fall; study companies, not forecasts.

- **Thirteen minutes.** Spend little time on economic forecasts: he joked that 13 minutes a year on economics wastes ten of them. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Facts, not forecasts.** Watch the facts in the industries you own, such as used-car prices for carmakers or new hotel building for hotel chains. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Nobody saw 1982 coming.** In 1982 inflation was 14%, the prime rate 20% and over half his companies were losing money. The great bull market began that year. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Timing barely matters.** Investing every year at the year's high from 1965 to 1995 earned about 10.6% a year; at the year's low, about 11.7%. (1996 PBS interview)
- **Declines are normal.** Falls happen and no one knows when. People who say they predicted one predicted many others too. (1994 Press Club)
- **The cocktail party.** Half joking, he described four stages of a market, from guests avoiding him at parties to everyone giving him tips. He did not use it to predict. (1989 One Up I)
- **Pessimism rarely pays.** Unless you sell short for a living, it rarely pays to be a pessimist about the long run. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **History says: markets fall.** Study history to learn that markets go down, and decide in advance that a fall is a chance to buy, not a reason to run. (1994 Press Club)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lynch-11-dont-time-the-market.html

### 12. Keep checking the story

Hold as long as the story holds; recheck it every few months, and sell when the story, not the price, changes.

- **Check the story regularly.** Even a long-term owner has to keep following the company. Stories end, for example when a business saturates its market. (2023 Interview)
- **Know the stage.** Companies pass through stages, from start-up to fast growth to maturity. The key question is which stage yours is in. (2023 Interview · 1995 Learn to Earn)
- **Sell by kind.** The right exit depends on the kind of stock: a stalwart after a solid rise, a fast grower when its room to expand runs out, a turnaround once it has turned around. (1989 One Up II)
- **Boredom is expensive.** Give up on a stock because nothing is happening and, all too often, things start to happen the day after you sell. (1989 One Up III)
- **Don't be a trader.** Cheap commissions are no reason to trade. Buying three stocks a day and selling them on Friday is gambling, not investing. (2023 Interview)
- **Few holdings for part-timers.** A part-time picker can follow eight to twelve companies, with no more than five in the portfolio at any one time. (1993 Beating the Street)
- **Beware buying after the leap.** In 2000 he warned that few dot-com buyers get the full ride, because the early gains happen before most people can buy. (2000 New intro)
- **Stocks are like children.** Don't take on more than you can handle. (1993 Beating the Street)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/lynch-12-keep-checking-the-story.html

## Words to know

- **Ten-bagger**: A stock that rises tenfold. His baseball term: two home runs and a double.
- **Slow grower**: A large, mature company growing about as fast as the economy, owned for its dividend.
- **Stalwart**: A big, established company growing earnings about 10 to 12% a year.
- **Fast grower**: A small, aggressive company growing 20 to 25% a year. His favourite kind.
- **Cyclical**: A company whose profits rise and fall with the economy.
- **Turnaround**: A battered company that might recover.
- **Asset play**: A company sitting on something valuable that the market has missed.
- **PEG ratio**: The P/E divided by the earnings growth rate. Around 1 is fair; well below 1 may be a bargain.
- **Diworseification**: His word for a profitable company buying unrelated businesses and making itself worse.
- **Two-minute drill**: A short spoken case for a stock: why you like it, what must happen, what could go wrong.
