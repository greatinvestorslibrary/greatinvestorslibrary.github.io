# Philip Fisher

> Ran his own firm for 68 years, found great companies by talking to everyone around them, and held Motorola for nearly half a century.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/fisher.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Scuttlebutt. Learn about a company from its customers, suppliers, rivals and former staff.

## Summary

Philip Fisher ran his own investment firm in San Francisco from 1931 until he retired at 91. Where Graham looked for cheap shares, Fisher looked for outstanding companies, found out everything about them by talking to the people around them, and then held on, in Motorola's case for nearly fifty years. Buffett once said he was 15% Fisher.

## Record at a glance

- **1955 → 2004**: bought Motorola when it made radios; he still owned it when he died
- **15 points**: his checklist for an outstanding company, from his 1958 book
- **68 years**: running his own firm, from 1931 until he retired at 91 in 1999
- **1958**: Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits, one of the first investing books on the New York Times bestseller list

## Big ideas

### 1. Scuttlebutt

Find out about a company from the people around it: customers, suppliers, competitors, former employees and scientists. Only then visit the managers.

- **The grapevine.** He found the business grapevine remarkably informative: put together many small views and a clear picture forms. (Book, 1958)
- **Ask about the others.** Ask five companies in an industry about the strengths and weaknesses of the other four, and you will learn a great deal about all five. (Book, 1958)
- **Then the managers.** Meet management only after the scuttlebutt, so you know which questions matter. (Book, 1958)
- **Buffett learned it.** Buffett said that from Fisher he learned the value of the scuttlebutt approach. (Obituary, 2004)
- **Learned at Stanford.** His habit began at business school, visiting companies each week with a professor. (Book, 1980)

### 2. The fifteen points

He listed fifteen questions an outstanding company should answer well. A company need not pass all, but must pass most.

- **Room to grow.** Products or services with enough market to lift sales sizably for years, and a management determined to find the next ones. (Book, 1958)
- **Research and selling.** Effective research for its size, and a sales organisation better than average. (Book, 1958)
- **Margins.** A worthwhile profit margin, and a plan to keep or improve it. (Book, 1958)
- **Inside the company.** Good relations with staff and among executives, depth of management, and sound cost controls. (Book, 1958)
- **Most, not all.** A company that fails one or two points can still be outstanding; one that fails many is not. (Book, 1958)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-02-the-fifteen-points.html

### 3. Ability and integrity

Two traits in the people who run a company are essential: business ability, and honesty. Without both, he would not invest.

- **Two traits.** He learned early that the people running a company need two things: business ability and integrity. (Book, 1980)
- **Unquestionable integrity.** His last point asks whether management has unquestionable integrity. Managers are far closer to the assets than shareholders are. (Book, 1958)
- **Candour when it hurts.** Beware managers who talk freely when things go well but clam up when trouble comes. (Book, 1958)
- **No needless dilution.** Ask whether growth will need so many new shares that existing owners gain little. (Book, 1958)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-03-ability-and-integrity.html

### 4. Growth that lasts

Look for companies that can keep growing for years, because they keep developing new products. Fortunes come from seeing big changes early.

- **The next product.** Ask what the company will sell once today's products are fully exploited. (Book, 1958)
- **Big shifts.** Now and then a deep change, in technology or in society, lifts or sinks a whole group of companies at once. (Book, 1960)
- **Early is everything.** The big gains go to those who see what such a change means before everyone else does. (Book, 1960)
- **Motorola.** He bought Motorola in 1955, when it was still a radio maker, and held it for the rest of his life. (Obituary, 2004)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-04-growth-that-lasts.html

### 5. Conservative investors sleep well

A conservative investment keeps your purchasing power with the least risk. He defined it in four dimensions.

- **Low costs and skill.** To be truly conservative, a company must be the lowest-cost producer, or close to it, and strong in marketing, research and finance. (Book, 1975)
- **People.** Second, the people: able and honest managers. (Book, 1975)
- **Above-average earnings.** Third, a business whose nature lets it earn better than average returns. (Book, 1975)
- **A sensible price.** Fourth, the price: a great company can still be a poor buy if the market already prices it for perfection. (Book, 1975)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-05-conservative-investors-sleep-well.html

### 6. When to sell: almost never

If you bought the right company, the time to sell is almost never. He gave three reasons to sell, and dismissed the rest.

- **Reason one.** You made a mistake: the company is not what you thought. (Book, 1958)
- **Reason two.** The company has changed and no longer passes the fifteen points as it did. (Book, 1958)
- **Reason three.** A clearly better opportunity appears; he thought this should be rare. (Book, 1958)
- **Not a reason.** A big rise in price, or fear of a market fall, is not a reason to sell an outstanding company. (Book, 1958)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-06-when-to-sell-almost-never.html

### 7. Own the best, not the most

Over-diversifying into companies you don't know well is riskier than owning a few you know thoroughly.

- **Don't overstress diversification.** Owning too many companies means owning ones you cannot follow properly. (Book, 1958)
- **Quality over count.** Spread enough that one mistake cannot cripple you, then take care to own the best, not the most. (Book, 1958)
- **His own practice.** He held a small number of companies for very long periods, Motorola above all. (Obituary, 2004)
- **Buffett's mix.** In 1969 Buffett described himself as 85% Graham and 15% Fisher: cheapness from one, quality from the other. (Forbes, 1969)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-07-own-the-best-not-the-most.html

### 8. Don't follow the crowd

Prices swing with fashion. Look years ahead, buy when fear runs high, and judge price by tomorrow's earnings, not today's.

- **War scares.** Don't be afraid of buying when war fears push prices down. (Book, 1958)
- **Time as well as price.** With a true growth company, waiting for a perfect price can cost more than paying a fair one. (Book, 1958)
- **Years ahead.** He came to see that a company's price against its earnings a few years out matters far more than today's figure. (Book, 1980)
- **Against fashion.** Good moments to buy often come when a passing problem makes the market think less of an outstanding company than it deserves. (Book, 1958)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-08-dont-follow-the-crowd.html

### 9. Dividends matter less

He thought investors made too much fuss about dividends. A company that can reinvest profits well usually serves owners better by doing so.

- **The hullabaloo.** He devoted a chapter to what he saw as excessive attention to dividends. (Book, 1958)
- **Reinvestment first.** A fast-growing company that reinvests profits at high returns may do far more for owners than one that pays them out. (Book, 1958)
- **A clear policy.** What matters is a dividend policy that is clear and consistent, so owners know what to expect. (Book, 1958)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/fisher-09-dividends-matter-less.html

### 10. Lessons from his own mistakes

He saw the 1929 crash coming and still lost money. He started his firm in the Depression with almost nothing, and built his method from mistakes.

- **A first job he disliked.** He later called his first job, as a bank's securities analyst, intellectually dishonest, and resolved to work differently. (Book, 1980)
- **Right, and still wrong.** In August 1929 he warned his bank of the worst bear market in 25 years, then bought shares he thought cheap and lost money in the crash. (Book, 1980)
- **A tiny start.** He opened his firm with costs of $25 a month and made $3 a month in the first year. (Book, 1980)
- **Judge me in three years.** He told clients to fire him if he had not produced worthwhile results within three years. (Book, 1980)

## Special section

**Tick the points a company passes**

- Its market can lift sales sizably for years
- Management is set on finding the next products
- Its research is effective for its size
- Its sales organisation is better than average
- It earns a worthwhile profit margin
- It has a plan to keep or improve that margin
- Relations with its staff are outstanding
- Its executives work well together
- It has depth of management, not one star
- It controls and understands its costs
- It is strong in the ways that matter in its industry
- It thinks long term about profits
- Growth won't need so many new shares that owners gain little
- Managers speak as openly about bad news as good
- Management has unquestionable integrity

**His three reasons to sell**

- A mistake: the company was never what you thought.
- A change: it no longer passes the points as it did.
- A much better use: a clearly superior company appears. This should be rare.

**His ten don'ts, in brief**

- Don't buy promotional companies with no record yet.
- Don't ignore a good share just because it trades off the main exchange.
- Don't buy for the tone of the annual report.
- Don't assume a high P/E means the growth is already priced in.
- Don't haggle over tiny sums on an outstanding company.
- Don't overstress diversification.
- Don't fear buying on a war scare.
- Don't be swayed by irrelevant past prices and earnings.
- Don't forget time as well as price for a true growth share.
- Don't follow the crowd.

## Read the originals

Start with the first three chapters of Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits: the scuttlebutt method and the fifteen points.

- [Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits](https://www.fool.com/specials/2001/01/18/fishers-investing-criteria-sp010118.aspx): His main book: the scuttlebutt method, the fifteen points, when to buy and sell, dividends, and ten don'ts for investors.
- [Paths to Wealth through Common Stocks](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Philip_Arthur_Fisher): How big changes in technology and society affect whole groups of companies, and how inflation shapes investing.
- [Conservative Investors Sleep Well](https://www.morningstar.com.au/personal-finance/bookworm-an-essential-quality-conservative-investments-phil-fisher): What makes an investment conservative: low costs and strong skills, good people, a business that earns above average, and a sensible price.
- [Developing an Investment Philosophy](https://www.fool.com/archive/portfolios/rulemaker/2001/03/23/eight-principles-of-investing.aspx): His own story: Stanford, a dishonest first job, the 1929 crash, starting his firm, and the lessons behind each part of his method.
- [Philip A. Fisher, 96, is dead; wrote key investment book](https://www.nytimes.com/2004/04/19/business/philip-a-fisher-96-is-dead-wrote-key-investment-book.html): His life and family, Motorola and Texas Instruments, and what Buffett said he learned from him.
- [How Omaha beats Wall Street](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/forbes-warren-buffett-omaha-beats-wall-street/): The young Buffett explains his approach, and how much of it comes from Graham and how much from Fisher.

## Words to know

- **Scuttlebutt**: Information gathered informally from people who deal with a company.
- **Growth stock**: A share in a company expected to grow sales and profits much faster than average.
- **Profit margin**: Profit as a share of sales.
- **Research and development**: Spending on new products and processes.
- **Dilution**: When a company issues new shares, each existing share owns less of it.
- **Diversification**: Spreading money across many holdings to reduce the harm of any one mistake.
- **Low-cost producer**: A company that can make its product more cheaply than its rivals.
- **Dividend**: Part of a company's profit paid out to shareholders.
