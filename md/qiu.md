# Qiu Guolu

> Boiled investing down to three questions, why cheap, why good, why now, and built one of China's largest private fund firms on them.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/qiu.html · Section: China · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Cheapness is the hard truth. Even an ordinary company can pay well if bought cheaply enough.

## Summary

Qiu Guolu formed his approach during six years at Wedge Capital in the United States, ran investment at China Southern Asset Management from 2008, and in 2014 founded Gaoyi Asset Management, a home for several star managers that grew past 100 billion yuan. His bestselling book boils investing down to three questions: why is it cheap, why is it good, and why buy now?

- **3 questions**: why is it cheap, why is it good, why now: his whole method
- **about 10**: truly great companies, by his count, among roughly 2,500 then listed in China; so price must do much of the work

## Big ideas

### 1. Three questions

All his analysis comes down to three questions: why is it cheap, why is it good, and why buy now?

- **Cheap, good, now.** Every analysis answers three questions: why the company is cheap, why it is good, and why now is the time to buy. (Book, 2014)
- **Valuation is the easy part.** Price to earnings, to book value and to sales are the most scientific measures, and the easiest to learn. (Book, 2014)
- **Quality is hard.** Few people can spot a truly great company before the market does, and many once-great companies lost their edge. (Book, 2014)
- **Good industry, good company, good price.** His whole approach: find good companies in good industries, then wait for a good price. (Book, 2014)
- **Simple tools.** Porter's five forces for the industry, DuPont analysis for how profits are made, and comparisons with peers and history for the price. (Book, 2014)

### 2. Cheapness is the hard truth

A low price is the investor's first protection. Even an ordinary company can pay well if bought cheaply enough.

- **Buy cheap to sell cheap.** He borrows a retailer's rule, Sam Walton's: you can only sell cheaply if you buy cheaply. In investing, buying cheaply matters even more. (Book, 2014)
- **Ordinary but cheap.** An ordinary company bought at a low enough price can still give a fine return. (Book, 2014)
- **Few true greats.** He reckoned that among about 2,500 Chinese listed companies perhaps ten were truly great, so most of the time price must do the work. (Book, 2014)
- **Wary of the next great thing.** He is suspicious of stories about the next great company, and admits he struggles even to pay a fair price for great ones. (Book, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-02-cheapness-is-the-hard-truth.html

### 3. Pricing power is the core advantage

A company with a real edge can raise prices without losing customers. That power comes from doing what you can repeat and others cannot copy.

- **Two tests.** A company with a real edge does something it can repeat again and again, and something others cannot copy. (Book, 2014)
- **Concentration is not enough.** He cares less about how few rivals there are than about the bargaining power and irreplaceability that brings. (Lecture, 2017)
- **Easy money versus hard work.** That power is the dividing line between businesses that earn easily and those that work hard for little. (Lecture, 2017)
- **Where it comes from.** Scarce resources, a strong brand, patents, a dominant position or a licence: in the end each shows up as pricing power. (Book, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-03-pricing-power-is-the-core-advantage.html

### 4. Count the moons, not the stars

Look at the whole industry, not just the company. The best skies have one bright moon; the worst are crowded with stars.

- **Best to worst.** One dominant firm is best; then one leader with a few strong rivals; then two or three equals; worst is a sky full of small competitors. (Lecture, 2017)
- **Define the sky.** The skill is drawing the right boundary: which firms are real rivals and which belong to a different sky. (Lecture, 2017)
- **Watch for invaders.** Firms from other industries can enter any sky. (Lecture, 2017)
- **A rising moon.** The prize is a company whose pricing power the market has not yet noticed. (Lecture, 2017)
- **Right track, wrong horse.** Investors often pick the right industry but the wrong company. (Lecture, 2017)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-04-count-the-moons-not-the-stars.html

### 5. Win first, then fight

Crowded, fast-growing industries make poor hunting grounds. Wait until the battle is over and back the winner.

- **Wait for the winner.** In a crowded industry it is hard to know whom to back, however fast it grows. Wait until the fighting ends and a winner emerges. (Book, 2014 · Lecture, 2017)
- **Fast change destroys moats.** In fast-moving technology, rivals simply build a new castle elsewhere. Televisions went through tubes, projection, LCD, plasma, LED and 3D in a few years; air conditioners changed far less. (Article)
- **A formula for a century.** Coca-Cola's formula has hardly changed in a hundred years, and China too has traditional products that last for centuries. (Article)
- **Scandals help leaders.** After food scandals, industry sales recovered within months and the big brands gained share, as buyers trusted known names. (Article)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-05-win-first-then-fight.html

### 6. Take what others discard

Go where the crowd is not, and be careful where it is packed. But cheap can stay cheap: check why the crowd left.

- **Twice a year.** Every January and July he adds up the industry weights of all fund companies, then grows cautious on the crowded industries and more hopeful on the shunned ones. (Article)
- **He has seen manias.** He lived through the 1992 rush for share certificates and the late-1990s Nasdaq boom and crash. (Profile, 2015)
- **Out of the crowded trade.** In 2020–21 he stayed out of the crowded trade in richly valued favourite shares, which then fell about 40% from early 2021. (Letter, 2022)
- **Beware value traps.** His book warns that some cheap shares stay cheap for good reasons, and describes the kinds most likely to become traps. (Book, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-06-take-what-others-discard.html

### 7. A lucky win is not a right bet

Judge your method, not this year's result. A sound process shows itself over five to ten years.

- **Two different things.** Winning a bet and betting right are not the same: a good process can give poor results for a while, and a bad one can get lucky. (Book, 2014)
- **Five to ten years.** A sound method gives good results with high probability over five to ten years, not six months. (Book, 2014)
- **Six or seven in ten.** He reckons a sound method beats the market in about six or seven years out of ten, a poor one in three or four. (Book, 2014)
- **Why it keeps working.** Citing Joel Greenblatt: value investing works over time precisely because it doesn't work every year. (Book, 2014)
- **Don't keep switching.** Investors who keep changing methods tend to fail; successful ones stick with one. (Book, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-07-a-lucky-win-is-not-a-right-bet.html

### 8. Clients first

A fund firm must put compliance before growth, clients before itself, and results before size.

- **Three priorities.** In 2017 he wrote to all staff: when growth clashes with compliance, compliance wins; when the firm's interest clashes with clients', clients win; when size clashes with results, results win. (Interviews, 2018–19)
- **Patient money.** Many Gaoyi funds lock investors in for two or three years, some for five or eight, because stable money lets managers think long term. (Interviews, 2018–19)
- **A home for managers.** He built Gaoyi to handle everything except investing, so its managers can concentrate on picking shares. (Interviews, 2018–19)
- **Shaped in America.** His first job was as a strategist and quantitative analyst; his approach took shape during six years at Wedge Capital. (Interviews, 2018–19)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-08-clients-first.html

### 9. Value investing works in China

He has tried most methods. For most people, he concluded, only value investing can really be learned and used, in China as anywhere.

- **First money in 1992.** At 19 he queued overnight for share subscription certificates and made his first stock market gains. (Profile, 2015)
- **Many methods tried.** Tips, charts, quant trading, concentrated picking, growth investing: he tried them all, and concluded that for most people only value investing can be learned and used. (Book, 2014)
- **Six strong years.** The 2015 profile credits him with six strong years at China Southern, using value methods in China's A-share market. (Profile, 2015)
- **The traits behind it.** The same profile sums up his habits: independent judgement, faith in common sense, a contrarian streak and patience for delayed rewards. (Profile, 2015)
- **Simple, not easy.** The point of the sequel: the principles are simple; holding to them, and applying them flexibly in a noisy market full of traps, is not. (Book, 2018)
- **Always learning.** He enjoys investing as a search for the truth, with new industries to learn every year. (Interviews, 2018–19)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-09-value-investing-works-in-china.html

### 10. Even legends stumble

After two hard years he wrote to investors about his mistakes, and what he would watch more closely.

- **Three lessons.** Too much in Hong Kong shares, cutting holdings too early in 2020, and too little attention to policy, especially the regulation of internet companies. (Letter, 2022)
- **Buying too soon.** After China's internet shares halved in late 2021 he added more, and they fell further. (Letter, 2022)
- **Look up at the sky.** His conclusion: keep your eyes on the road, but look up at the sky too, meaning watch policy as well as companies. (Letter, 2022)
- **Still a value investor.** He kept his faith in low valuations and his refusal to chase crowded favourites. (Letter, 2022)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/qiu-10-even-legends-stumble.html

## Special section

**Three questions, eight checks**

- Cheap: low against its own history and its peers, on more than one measure
- Good: it does something it can repeat, and others cannot copy
- Good: its industry has one or two clear leaders, and it is one of them
- Good: its products don't change every two or three years
- Good: I know how it makes its profits, and its strategy fits
- Now: the crowd is avoiding it, not chasing it
- Now: the industry's battle is over, and I am buying the winner
- I will judge this decision over five to ten years, not six months

**Count the moons: industries from best to worst**

- One moon. A single dominant firm.
- A moon with bright stars. One leader and a few strong rivals.
- Two or three moons. A handful of equals.
- A sky full of stars. Many small firms fighting on price.

**His four principles**

- Cheapness is the hard truth.
- Pricing power is the core advantage.
- Win first, then fight.
- Take what others discard.

## Words to know

- **A shares**: Shares of Chinese companies listed in Shanghai and Shenzhen, traded in yuan.
- **Price to earnings (P/E)**: The share price divided by the profit per share.
- **Price to book (P/B)**: The share price divided by the company's net assets per share.
- **Pricing power**: The ability to raise prices without losing customers.
- **Porter's five forces**: A way to judge an industry: rivals, new entrants, substitutes, and the bargaining power of suppliers and customers.
- **DuPont analysis**: Splitting return on equity into profit margin, asset turnover and borrowing.
- **Value trap**: A share that looks cheap but stays cheap, because the business keeps getting worse.
- **Contrarian**: Deliberately going against the crowd.
- **Private fund**: A fund sold only to qualified investors, with fewer public reporting rules than a public fund.
