# Howard Marks

> Has written memos since 1990 that Buffett opens first: on risk, cycles, and thinking differently from the crowd.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/marks.html · Section: the United States · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Second-level thinking. Ask not just what is good, but what everyone else already thinks.

## Summary

Howard Marks co-founded Oaktree Capital in 1995 and is best known for his memos to clients, which he has written since 1990. Buffett says they are the first thing he opens. Marks's message: you cannot predict the future, but you can know where you stand in the cycle, avoid big losses, and think differently from the crowd.

## Record at a glance

- **1990**: his first memo to clients; he has written them ever since, and they are free online
- **4th percentile**: where a pension fund ranked over 14 years though never in the top quarter in any year: the lesson of his first memo
- **≈ $6 billion**: invested by his partner's team from mid-September to the end of 2008, about $400 million a week, as panic peaked
- **1995**: co-founded Oaktree Capital in Los Angeles

## Big ideas

### 1. Second-level thinking

To do better than others you must think differently, and better. First-level thinking asks whether something is good; second-level thinking asks what is already in the price.

- **The first chapter.** His book opens with second-level thinking: a simple, obvious view cannot beat the market, because everyone already holds it. (Book, 2011)
- **Different actions.** If you think like everyone else you will act like them and get the same results; you have to think differently from the norm. (Talk)
- **The example.** A first-level thinker says a company is good, so buy. A second-level thinker asks whether everyone agrees, and whether the price already reflects it. (Book, 2011)
- **Not simple.** He warns that second-level thinking is hard work and that easy formulas are a sign of first-level thinking. (Book, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-01-second-level-thinking.html

### 2. Risk means losing money

For Marks, risk is not the jiggling of prices. It is the chance of a permanent loss, and it is hardest to see when times are good.

- **The risk that matters.** The most important risk is the chance of losing money, not short-term volatility. (Book, 2011)
- **Three chapters.** He devotes three chapters to risk: understanding it, recognising it and controlling it. (Book, 2011)
- **Good times teach bad lessons.** Easy markets teach investors that risk doesn't matter, just before it does. (Book, 2011)
- **Risk and return.** Riskier assets must appear to offer higher returns, but that is a promise, not a guarantee. (Book, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-02-risk-means-losing-money.html

### 3. It's what you pay

There is no such thing as a good or bad asset in itself, only a good or bad price. A great company bought too dear can be a poor investment.

- **Price and value.** He gives separate chapters to value and to the relationship between price and value. (Book, 2011)
- **Quality can be risky.** A high-quality asset bought at too high a price can be risky; a low-quality one bought cheaply can be safe. (Book, 2011)
- **Below value.** His answer to when to buy is simple: when the price is below intrinsic value. (Book, 2018)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-03-its-what-you-pay.html

### 4. The pendulum

Investor psychology swings like a pendulum between greed and fear, optimism and despair. It spends little time at the sensible midpoint.

- **A chapter of its own.** His book has a chapter on awareness of the pendulum. (Book, 2011)
- **Rarely in the middle.** Moods overshoot in both directions; the extremes are where mistakes, and opportunities, are made. (Book, 2011 · Book, 2018)
- **Many cycles.** His second book traces the cycles in the economy, in profits, in attitudes to risk, in credit and in investor psychology. (Book, 2018)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-04-the-pendulum.html

### 5. You can't predict. You can prepare.

Nobody can forecast the economy or the market reliably. But you can know roughly where you are in the cycle and prepare for what usually follows.

- **Forecasts don't help.** He dismisses economic forecasts, which he thinks add no value, but believes deeply in cycles. (Memo, 2001)
- **Where you are.** The key is to know where you are, even if you cannot know exactly where you are going. (Memo, 2001)
- **Borrowed words.** He took the memo's title from an insurance company's advertising slogan. (Memo, 2001)
- **Scenarios.** Since we never know what will happen, consider the likely scenarios and prepare for them. (Memo, 2001)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-05-you-cant-predict-you-can-prepare.html

### 6. Take the temperature

Watch how people around you behave. When everyone is eager and careless, be careful; when everyone is frightened, look for bargains.

- **Read the mood.** He judges the market by its psychology: is it buoyant and greedy, or depressed and fearful? (Memo, 2023)
- **Excess and correction.** A strong move one way is more likely to be followed by a correction than to grow to the sky. (Memo, 2023)
- **Two extremes.** Beware optimism so strong that no price seems too high, and look for despair so deep that any sale seems good. (Memo, 2023)
- **Resist your own mood.** Stand apart from the crowd, and act when you see something illogical. (Memo, 2023)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-06-take-the-temperature.html

### 7. bubble.com

In January 2000, near the peak of the technology bubble, he warned that internet and telecom shares had become a bubble. It burst within weeks.

- **The memo.** His January 2000 memo, bubble.com, warned of a bubble in technology, internet and telecom shares as it peaked. (Memo, 2023)
- **Little exposure.** Oaktree owned little technology at the time. (Memo, 2023)
- **Then defence.** From 2004 to 2007 he grew steadily more cautious, selling assets and raising the bar for new investments before the crisis. (Memo, 2023)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-07-bubble-com.html

### 8. Buying in the panic

Being prepared paid off in late 2008. When panic was at its worst, Oaktree had money ready and spent it.

- **Ready money.** Oaktree had raised a large fund for distressed debt before the crisis, so it could buy when others were forced to sell. (Memo, 2023)
- **$400 million a week.** From mid-September to the end of 2008 his partner Bruce Karsh's team invested about $400 million a week, about $6 billion. (Memo, 2023)
- **Not the bottom.** In 2020 he again bought as markets fell, accepting it might not be the lowest point. (Memo, 2023)
- **Fear as a signal.** Extreme pessimism, in his view, is when risk is lowest and bargains are best. (Memo, 2023 · Book, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-08-buying-in-the-panic.html

### 9. Avoid the losers

Superior results over time come less from big wins than from avoiding big losses. His very first memo made the point.

- **The pension fund.** A pension fund he met was never in the top quarter in any of 14 years, yet ranked in the top 4% over the whole period. (Memo, 1990)
- **Why.** It never had a disastrous year, never falling below the middle of its peers. (Memo, 1990)
- **Bold steps.** He argued that bold moves aimed at great performance are as likely to be wrong as right. (Memo, 1990)
- **Oaktree's creed.** He later said the memo set out what became a core part of Oaktree's investment philosophy. (Memo, 2025)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-09-avoid-the-losers.html

### 10. Writing to think, and saying 'I don't know'

His memos are how he thinks. He is candid about uncertainty and avoids flat predictions.

- **Since 1990.** He has written memos to clients since 1990, and they are posted publicly on Oaktree's website. (Memo, 1990 · Memo, 2023)
- **Never 'get out'.** He says he never tells clients anything as flatly negative as to get out of the market. (CFA, 2019)
- **Admit ignorance.** When asked something he can't answer, he says he doesn't know. (CFA, 2019)
- **Buffett reads first.** Buffett has written that Marks's memos are the first thing he opens in his mail. (Book, 2011)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/marks-10-writing-to-think-and-saying-i-dont-know.html

## Read the originals

Start with his first memo, The Route to Performance (1990), three pages long, then the book The Most Important Thing (2011).

- [The Route to Performance](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1990-10-12-the-route-to-performance.pdf): His first memo: the pension fund that reached the top 4% over 14 years by never having a bad year.
- [You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2001-11-20-you-cant-predict-you-can-prepare.pdf): Why he ignores economic forecasts but takes cycles seriously, and why knowing where you are matters more than knowing where you are going.
- [Taking the Temperature](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/taking-the-temperature.pdf): Five market calls from 2000 to 2020, from bubble.com to the 2008 crisis and Covid, and how he reads the market's mood.
- [The Most Important Thing](https://news.columbia.edu/node/2519): His memos distilled: second-level thinking, price and value, understanding and controlling risk, cycles and the pendulum.
- [Mastering the Market Cycle](https://books.google.com/books?id=8hhDDwAAQBAJ): The cycles in the economy, profits, credit and investor psychology, and how to position yourself as they turn.
- [Talks at Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/talks-at-gs/howard-marks.html): Marks on second-level thinking and on cycles.
- [The Best of](https://cnt.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/the-best-of): Marks picks his favourite memos from 35 years and says why each matters.
- [Getting the odds on your side](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/investor/2019/02/19/howard-marks-cfa-getting-the-odds-on-your-side/): His cautious memos, why he never says 'get out', and being honest about what he does not know.

## Words to know

- **Second-level thinking**: Thinking about what others think, and what is already reflected in the price.
- **Distressed debt**: Bonds and loans of companies in, or close to, financial trouble.
- **High-yield bond**: A bond from a riskier company that pays more interest; also called a junk bond.
- **Cycle**: The repeated rise and fall of economies, profits, credit and moods.
- **Percentile**: A ranking out of 100: the 4th percentile means better than 96% of the group.
- **Intrinsic value**: What an asset is really worth, based on what it will earn.
- **Volatility**: How much and how fast a price moves up and down.
