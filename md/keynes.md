# John Maynard Keynes

> The century's most famous economist failed at forecasting markets, changed his method, and beat the UK market for a quarter of a century.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/keynes.html · Section: the United Kingdom · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

In 1938: choose a few cheap investments carefully, hold them steadfastly, and balance the risks.

## Summary

John Maynard Keynes was the most famous economist of the 20th century. He also ran King's College, Cambridge's investments for 25 years. He failed as a market forecaster in the 1920s, changed his method, and from 1922 to 1946 his fund averaged about 16% a year against 10.4% for the UK market.

## Record at a glance

- **16.0% a year**: his King's fund's average yearly return, 1922 to 1946
- **10.4% a year**: the UK stock market's average over the same years
- **+8 points**: a year ahead on his UK shares (Chambers & Dimson)
- **½ hour**: a day on it, usually in bed each morning

## Big ideas

### 1. The forecaster who failed

In the 1920s he tried to profit from the ups and downs of the economy. Even he could not time them.

- **The plan.** At first he believed profit could be made by holding shares in slumps and selling them in booms, which he called a credit cycle policy. (Memo, 1938)
- **The result.** Looking back in 1938, he admitted the fund had not managed to profit from moving in and out of shares as a whole. (Memo, 1938)
- **Why it fails.** He found that people who try usually move after the turn, both in and out, and trade too often, which piles up costs and makes them jumpy. (Memo, 1938)
- **After the 1929 crash.** Having suffered heavy losses, he sold a fifth of his UK shares and moved into government bonds, the conventional response. (Chambers & Dimson)
- **Currencies too.** In 354 currency trades he was usually right about direction but struggled with timing. (Currency study, 2016)

### 2. From forecaster to stock picker

In the early 1930s he stopped forecasting the market and started choosing individual companies. His results improved dramatically.

- **The switch.** From the early 1930s he moved from top-down market timing to bottom-up stock picking. (Chambers & Dimson)
- **Less trading.** His yearly turnover fell from about 55% in the 1920s to 30% in the 1930s and 14% in the 1940s. (Chambers & Dimson)
- **What did work.** Even in his 1938 review, buying particular shares when their prices were greatly depressed had done well. (Memo, 1938)
- **He changed his mind in writing.** His 1938 memo openly drew the opposite lesson from the one he had set out to prove. Admitting it is the first step. (Memo, 1938)

### 3. His three principles

In May 1938 he summed up successful investment in three principles: careful selection, steadfast holding, balance.

- **1. Careful selection.** A few investments, picked because they are cheap compared with what they are likely to be worth over the next few years, and compared with the alternatives. (Memo, 1938)
- **2. Steadfast holding.** Own them in size and refuse to be shaken out, for years if needed, until they deliver or you can see you were wrong. (Memo, 1938)
- **3. Balance.** Mix your risks even though each holding is big, ideally with some that tend to move the other way, as gold-mining shares often did against other shares. (Memo, 1938)
- **The £1 note.** Selling something worth £1 for 15 shillings (75p), hoping to buy it back for 12s 6d (about 62p), is a poor trade. So is refusing to buy it at 15 shillings because it looks too good to be true. (Memo, 1938)

### 4. A few big bets you understand

He thought spreading money across many things you know little about was a mistake. Better a few large holdings you understand.

- **Know the business.** In 1934 he told a colleague it is better to invest decent amounts in a few businesses you understand and whose managers you believe in. (Letter, 1934)
- **Two or three at a time.** He reckoned that at any one moment only two or three companies earned his complete confidence. (Letter, 1934)
- **Concentrated in practice.** His holdings grew from about 20 to over 60, yet at times more than half the fund sat in five shares. (CFA Institute, 2014)
- **A bold position.** In 1936, 66% of the fund was in mining shares. (CFA Institute, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/keynes-04-a-few-big-bets-you-understand.html

### 5. Shares over bonds, for the long run

He moved a centuries-old college endowment from land and bonds into shares, decades before other institutions.

- **Ahead of his time.** He moved King's into shares while most institutions still relied on bonds and property. (Chambers & Dimson)
- **Shares for the long run.** For investors with very long horizons, he favoured shares over bonds. (CFA Institute, 2014)
- **Look abroad.** By the late 1930s his fund held less in Britain than in the US or Africa. (CFA Institute, 2014)
- **Value and dividends.** He leaned towards cheap shares with high dividend yields. One famous buy, the mining group Union Corporation, cost 30% less than its estimated break-up value. (CFA Institute, 2014)

### 6. The beauty contest

He compared the stock market to a newspaper contest where you win by guessing what others will pick, not what you think is best.

- **The contest.** Readers had to pick the six prettiest faces from a hundred photographs. The prize went to whoever picked closest to the average choice. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Second and third guesses.** So each player picks not the face he finds prettiest, but the one he thinks others will pick, and some go further still. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Professionals do the same.** Many professional investors focus on guessing how market opinion will shift in the coming months, not on a business's long-run worth. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Your edge.** Prices can drift far from value for a long time. Thinking about the business, not the crowd, is what lets you profit when they meet again. (CFA Institute, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/keynes-06-the-beauty-contest.html

### 7. Convention and reputation

Most people assume tomorrow will look like today, and most professionals would rather be wrong with the crowd than right alone.

- **The convention.** Lacking real knowledge of the far future, we assume the present will continue unless we have a specific reason to expect change. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Safety in numbers.** He saw that for a professional's reputation, failing with everyone else is safer than succeeding alone. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Liquid but jumpy.** Because shares can be sold at any moment, their prices are revalued daily, letting short-term mood dominate. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Someone must back you.** He could be unconventional because the Fellows of King's trusted him. Long-term investing needs patient backers. (Chambers & Dimson)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/keynes-07-convention-and-reputation.html

### 8. Animal spirits

Much of what drives business and markets is not calculation but a spontaneous urge to act, which can switch off suddenly.

- **Not just arithmetic.** Many decisions to invest rest on spontaneous optimism, which he called animal spirits, rather than on careful calculation. (General Theory, 1936)
- **When spirits fade.** If optimism dims, investment can collapse even with no new facts. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Speculation and enterprise.** He warned that when a nation's investment is driven by casino-like speculation, the job is likely to be done badly. (General Theory, 1936)
- **Use the mood, don't share it.** His own best buys came when prices were greatly depressed: when animal spirits were at their lowest. (Memo, 1938)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/keynes-08-animal-spirits.html

### 9. Holding through the falls

Long-term investing means sitting through sharp falls. He did, and it cost him a chairmanship.

- **Two big falls.** In nine years his whole share portfolio had twice fallen 20 to 25% within a few months, and he could not escape either fall. (Memo, 1938)
- **1937–38.** When London shares fell sharply he stayed invested, and resigned as chairman of an insurance company whose board would not stick with his strategy. (Chambers & Dimson)
- **Half an hour a day.** He spent no more than about half an hour each morning on it, usually before getting out of bed. You don't need to watch every price. (CFA Institute, 2014)
- **The reward.** By his death, King's had drawn level with Trinity, the wealthiest college in Cambridge. (Chambers & Dimson)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/keynes-09-holding-through-the-falls.html

### 10. Words he never said

Keynes is the most misquoted investor of all. Checking who really said what is a good habit for every investor.

- **Not his line.** "The market can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent" has no solid source in Keynes's writings. The earliest record found is the American adviser Gary Shilling, in 1986. (Quote Investigator)
- **Nor this one.** "When the facts change, I change my mind" is also widely credited to him, but scholars of his papers have found no primary source. (Quote Investigator)
- **What he did write.** His real words, in chapter 12 of The General Theory, the 1938 memo and the 1934 letter, are sharper than the invented ones. Read them. (General Theory, 1936 · Memo, 1938 · Letter, 1934)
- **Check before you share.** If a quote is too perfect and has no date or source, it is probably not genuine. (Quote Investigator)

## In their own words (short quotes)

- “it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally” (The General Theory, 1936)
- “a steadfast holding of these in fairly large units through thick and thin” (Memo to King's College, 1938)
- “put fairly large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about” (Letter to F. C. Scott, 1934)

## Words to know

- **Bursar**: The college officer in charge of its money and property.
- **Endowment**: A fund given to an institution, invested so that it can pay for its work for ever.
- **Discretionary fund**: Money the manager may invest freely, without fixed rules on what to buy.
- **Credit cycle policy**: His 1920s attempt to buy shares in slumps and sell them in booms: market timing.
- **Intrinsic value**: What a business is really worth, judged from what it will earn, as opposed to its price.
- **Turnover**: The share of a portfolio bought and sold in a year.
- **Beauty contest**: His picture of a market where people guess what others will think, not what is good.
- **Animal spirits**: The spontaneous urge to act, rather than calculation, that drives much investment.
- **Shilling**: An old British coin worth one twentieth of a pound; 15 shillings was 75p.
- **Liquidity**: How easily something can be sold for cash at a fair price.
