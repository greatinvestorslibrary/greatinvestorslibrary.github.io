# Prem Watsa

> Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/watsa.html · Section: the wider world · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Value first. Buy below what a business is worth, as Graham and Buffett taught.

## Summary

Prem Watsa grew up in Hyderabad, studied chemical engineering at IIT Madras, and reached Canada in the early 1970s with almost no money. In 1985 he took control of a small, struggling trucking insurer and renamed it Fairfax Financial. He ran it on Ben Graham's value principles and wrote to its owners every year. Since 1985 Fairfax's book value per share has grown about 18.7% a year, and people call him Canada's Warren Buffett.

## Record at a glance

- **18.7%**: a year: growth in Fairfax's book value per share, with dividends, 1985 to 2025
- **11.5%**: a year: the S&P 500 with dividends over the same forty years
- **$2.1bn**: of gains on credit default swaps by September 2008, bought from 2003 as protection
- **15%**: the long-term yearly target in Fairfax's guiding principles

## Big ideas

### 1. Eight dollars and a degree

He grew up in Hyderabad, son of a teacher, studied engineering at IIT Madras, and arrived in Canada with almost nothing.

- **A teacher's son.** He was born in Hyderabad in 1950; his father taught English and maths and later ran a boys' school. (Encyclopedia · Horatio Alger)
- **IIT Madras.** He graduated in chemical engineering from IIT Madras in 1971. (Encyclopedia)
- **Almost nothing.** He reached Canada with only a few dollars and paid for his MBA by selling furnaces in winter and air conditioners in summer. (Encyclopedia)
- **Hard roots.** His father had lost both his own parents at the age of three. (Horatio Alger)

### 2. A rescue becomes Fairfax

In 1985 he and his partners took over a small trucking insurer close to failure, renamed it Fairfax, and built it into a global insurance group.

- **His own firm first.** In 1984 he started an investment firm, Hamblin Watsa, with his former boss. (Encyclopedia)
- **A near-bankrupt start.** In 1985 they took control of Markel Financial, a struggling Canadian trucking insurer, and renamed it Fairfax. (Encyclopedia)
- **The insurance engine.** Like Berkshire, Fairfax invests the money it holds for future claims, called float. (Letter, 2025)
- **Forty years on.** By 2025 Fairfax earned a record $4.8 billion in a year. (Letter, 2025)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/watsa-02-a-rescue-becomes-fairfax.html

### 3. Graham's disciple

From his first letter he said Fairfax would follow the value approach of Benjamin Graham and his famous student, Warren Buffett.

- **The value approach.** Buy securities below what they are worth, with a margin of safety, and wait. (Letter, 2025 · Encyclopedia)
- **Called Canada's Buffett.** His record and his insurance-plus-investing model earned him the nickname. (Encyclopedia)
- **Graham's warning.** He has quoted Graham that only 1 in 100 survived the 1929–32 crash unless bearish in 1925. (Nasdaq, 2017)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/watsa-03-grahams-disciple.html

### 4. Book value per share, not quarters

He judges Fairfax by one long-term number: the growth of book value per share. The target has been 15% a year since the start.

- **One number.** The goal is to compound book value per share by 15% a year over the long term. (Principles)
- **Not quarterly profit.** The focus is long-term growth in book value, not quarterly earnings. (Principles)
- **The record.** Since 1985 book value per share has grown 18.7% a year with dividends, and the share price 19.5%. (Letter, 2025)
- **The yardstick.** Over the same forty years the S&P 500 returned 11.5% a year with dividends. (Letter, 2025)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/watsa-04-book-value-per-share-not-quarters.html

### 5. Insure against the storm

He treats the investment portfolio like an insurer treats risk: buy protection against disasters others think impossible.

- **From 2003.** Fairfax began buying credit default swaps, a kind of insurance against companies failing to pay debts. (Press, 2008)
- **Protection, not a bet.** He described the swaps as steps to protect the balance sheet from investment risks. (Press, 2008)
- **The payoff.** By September 2008 the swaps had produced about $2.1 billion of gains as the credit crisis broke. (Press, 2008)
- **CEO of the year.** The Globe and Mail's business magazine named him CEO of the year, partly for holding on to the swaps. (Globe, 2009)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/watsa-05-insure-against-the-storm.html

### 6. Early looks like wrong

Being right too soon is painful. While he waited for the crisis, Fairfax's shares fell by about half, and critics mocked him.

- **Three lean years.** Between mid-2003 and mid-2006 Fairfax's share price fell about 50% as the swaps held back profits. (Reuters, 2013)
- **Early, again.** In 2012 he wrote that Fairfax continued to be early, and bearish, citing Graham. (Nasdaq, 2017)
- **Survive to be right.** Staying soundly financed is what lets an early investor survive until the market comes round. (Principles)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/watsa-06-early-looks-like-wrong.html

### 7. When protection costs too much

From 2010 he hedged all of Fairfax's shares against a deflationary slump that never came. The hedges cost billions before he removed them in 2016.

- **Fully hedged.** From 2010 Fairfax hedged about 100% of its shares; by September 2016 the hedges were 112.7% of its holdings. (Press, 2016 · Nasdaq, 2017)
- **The cost.** When removed in late 2016, the index shorts had cost about $1.2 billion that year alone. (Nasdaq, 2017)
- **A change of view.** After the 2016 US election he judged the hedges no longer needed and took them off. (Press, 2016)
- **The lesson.** Protection that works once can be too expensive the next time; the cost must be weighed. (Nasdaq, 2017)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/watsa-07-when-protection-costs-too-much.html

### 8. Own your mistakes

His letters take the blame for what goes wrong and share the credit for what goes right, including public apologies.

- **Blame and credit.** In his letters he accepts the blame for disappointments and shares the credit for successes. (Encyclopedia)
- **A public apology.** In 2009 he told shareholders Fairfax should have been far more careful with three failed value investments. (Globe, 2009)
- **BlackBerry.** Fairfax was BlackBerry's top shareholder through its decline; he later wrote of it as a mistake. (Reuters, 2013 · Letter, 2024)

### 9. Integrity under scrutiny

In 2006 a Fairfax subsidiary restated its accounts and the SEC investigated. In 2009 the SEC closed its review with no enforcement action.

- **Restatements.** A subsidiary, Odyssey Re, restated results for errors in accounting for finite reinsurance. (SEC, 2009)
- **Subpoenas.** Fairfax and Watsa received SEC subpoenas in 2006 over an answer he gave on an investor call. (SEC, 2009)
- **No action.** In June 2009 the SEC said it did not intend to recommend any enforcement action. (SEC, 2009)

### 10. Patient, quiet and decentralised

He avoids publicity, runs Fairfax through independent managers, and thinks in decades.

- **Decentralised.** Fairfax's companies are run by their own leaders, with the head office judging them over the long term. (Letter, 2025)
- **Soundly financed.** Always being soundly financed is one of the company's core principles. (Principles)
- **Honours.** He was appointed a Member of the Order of Canada in 2015. (Encyclopedia)

## Special section

**Fairfax's guiding principles, in short**

- 15% a year: compound book value per share over the long term.
- Long term: focus on book value, not quarterly earnings.
- Soundly financed: always.
- Friendly growth: grow from within, and through friendly acquisitions.

**Protection or a bet? Tap your answer**

- You buy cheap insurance that pays if credit markets freeze, because a freeze would badly hurt your business. Answer: Protection. Insuring against the one thing that could sink you is how he described the swaps from 2003.
- You short the market with borrowed money because you think it will fall next month. Answer: A bet. A short-term call on direction, with borrowed money, is a bet, not insurance.
- You keep paying for a hedge for six years, though the danger you feared keeps not coming. Answer: A bet. When protection costs more than it could save, it becomes a costly view on the market: the 2010–2016 lesson.
- You keep enough cash and little debt so a crash can never force you to sell. Answer: Protection. Being soundly financed is the cheapest protection of all.

**An honest note.** In February 2006 Fairfax's subsidiary Odyssey Re restated its results for errors in accounting for finite reinsurance contracts. Fairfax and Prem Watsa received SEC subpoenas in March 2006 relating to an answer he gave on an investor call about the company's review of those contracts. In June 2009 Fairfax said the SEC had completed its investigation and did not intend to recommend any enforcement action. Separately, the 2010–2016 equity hedges and some investments, such as BlackBerry, lost Fairfax a great deal of money, which he has acknowledged. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

## Words to know

- **Book value per share**: What a company's assets minus its debts are worth on paper, divided by the number of shares.
- **Float**: Money an insurer holds from premiums before it pays claims, which it can invest meanwhile.
- **Credit default swap (CDS)**: A contract that pays out if a borrower fails to repay its debts; a kind of insurance.
- **Hedge**: A position that gains when your main holdings fall, to cushion losses.
- **Deflation**: Prices falling across an economy, which makes debts heavier and profits harder.
- **Finite reinsurance**: A kind of reinsurance that transfers little real risk; its accounting drew scrutiny in the mid-2000s.
- **Decentralised**: Run by the managers of each business, not from head office.
