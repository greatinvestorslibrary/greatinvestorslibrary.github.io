# Atsuto Sawakami

> Founded Japan's first independent fund company in 1999 and preached long-term investing for decades, when few in Japan would listen.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/sawakami.html · Section: Japan · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Investing, not money-rolling. Buying and selling on price moves is not investing.

## Summary

Atsuto Sawakami ran the Japanese arm of the Swiss private bank Pictet, then in 1999 founded Japan's first fund company with no ties to a big financial group. He sold one fund, directly, with no up-front commission, and preached long-term investing through decades when few in Japan wanted to hear it. He refused pension money as too short-term. Bloomberg called him Japan's first evangelist of long-term investing.

## Record at a glance

- **1999**: founded Japan's first fund company independent of the big financial groups
- **1 fund**: sold directly, with no up-front commission
- **+9%**: his fund's gain in the year to March 2001, while the Nikkei fell 36%
- **10 years**: the shortest horizon he says an investor should think in

## Big ideas

### 1. The outsider from Pictet

He spent sixteen years at the head of a Swiss private bank's Japanese business, then left to build a fund company for ordinary savers, owned by no big group.

- **Geneva and Pictet.** Born in Nagoya in 1947, he studied international economics in Geneva and led Pictet's Japan business from 1980 to 1996. (Toyo Keizai, 2014)
- **On his own.** He set up an advisory firm in 1996 and the Sawakami Fund in 1999. (Toyo Keizai, 2014 · Bloomberg, 2016)
- **A first.** It was Japan's first fund company with no ties to a major financial institution. (Inst. Investor, 2001)
- **An evangelist.** Bloomberg later called him Japan's first evangelist of long-term investing, and an old rebel. (Bloomberg, 2018)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-01-the-outsider-from-pictet.html

### 2. Investing, not money-rolling

He divides the market in two: real long-term investing, and what he calls money-rolling, buying and selling on price moves.

- **Mostly money-rolling.** What most institutions, and many individuals, do is not investing but rolling money around, he told a 2015 audience. (Talk, 2015)
- **Opposite instincts.** Ordinary investors buy more as prices rise; long-term investors buy more as prices fall. (Talk, 2015)
- **The whole of it.** Buying low and selling high, he said, is the ideal and the whole of investing. (Talk, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-02-investing-not-money-rolling.html

### 3. Buy when they sell

His fund adds to holdings when others sell and trims when others buy. Most people, he says, do the reverse and pay for it.

- **Fear and chase.** In a crash most people fear further falls and don't buy; in a rally they keep buying, then panic-sell when prices collapse. (Talk, 2015)
- **The fund's habit.** The fund buys companies making what consumers will need in future, adds when others sell, and trims when others buy. (Bloomberg, 2016)
- **Unloved old Japan.** In 2001 he bought restructuring 'failed' old-industry companies, then beaten-down technology names. (Inst. Investor, 2001)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-03-buy-when-they-sell.html

### 4. Proof in a bad year, and the long record

In 2001, as Japanese shares crashed, his fund rose and gained customers. Over the long run its returns were steady rather than spectacular.

- **Up in a crash.** In the 12 months to early March 2001 his fund gained 9% while the Nikkei fell 36%, with much of it in cash. (Inst. Investor, 2001)
- **Money in, not out.** While other Japanese funds faced withdrawals, his kept attracting new money. (Inst. Investor, 2001)
- **The long run.** In 2015 he said the fund had returned about 5% a year over its 16 years. (Talk, 2015)
- **Since launch.** By 2016 Bloomberg put its total return since 1999 at 95%. (Bloomberg, 2016)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-04-proof-in-a-bad-year-and-the-long-record.html

### 5. Why even pensions turned short-term

He argues that pension funds, which should be the most patient money, became short-termist once they began judging managers every year. So he would not take their money.

- **Annual reviews.** From the mid-1970s pensions were managed for safety and checked every year, so short-term results came to matter most. (Talk, 2015)
- **Dancing to the music.** Managers who sell too early are blamed, so they keep buying in rallies and get caught by the crash. (Talk, 2015)
- **No, thank you.** He has refused to manage pension money, saying it doesn't give managers enough time. (Bloomberg, 2016)
- **No institutions.** He told Bloomberg he has basically no dealings with institutional investors, whom he calls short-termist. (Bloomberg, 2018)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-05-why-even-pensions-turned-short-term.html

### 6. Sell direct, charge little

He sold his fund straight to savers, with no up-front commission, a low fee, no advertising and no sales force.

- **Low cost.** No up-front commission and a 1% management fee, which meant he needed far more money to break even. (Inst. Investor, 2001)
- **No adverts.** He did not advertise; visitors to the office were offered wine and snacks. (Inst. Investor, 2001)
- **No salesmen.** He relied on no sales staff. (Japan Times, 2001)
- **Direct, or nothing.** He told a younger founder that selling through brokerages was a mistake; the only way was to sell directly. (Bloomberg, 2018)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-06-sell-direct-charge-little.html

### 7. Tell investors everything

He believed savers should be told more, not less, and reported on every move every month.

- **Monthly reports.** His fund issued monthly information on its movements and investment decisions. (Japan Times, 2001)
- **More, not less.** He criticised fund managers who put the sales side before the customers they serve. (Japan Times, 2001)
- **Customers.** The fund had about 27,000 customers in 2001 and more than 118,000 by 2016. (Japan Times, 2001 · Bloomberg, 2016)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-07-tell-investors-everything.html

### 8. Think in decades

Look at least ten years ahead, he says, and ask how a company helps people live now and will help them in future.

- **Ten years, at least.** Investors should look at least ten years ahead, and hold for about that long. (Toyo Keizai, 2014)
- **People's lives.** Ask how a company supports people's lives today and how it will tomorrow; avoid businesses built on chasing money. (Toyo Keizai, 2014)
- **185,000 a day.** With the world's population growing by about 185,000 people a day, demand for energy, food, materials and water must rise. (Toyo Keizai, 2014)
- **Don't fear pessimism.** He argued that 'Japan pessimism' was nothing to fear, and that it was the time for long-term investing. (Toyo Keizai, 2014)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-08-think-in-decades.html

### 9. Letting go

He hired a former part-time actor, handed him the fund, and promised to judge him only over years, even when investors left and the style changed.

- **An unusual hire.** In 2007 a 28-year-old part-time actor came to his seminar; he later gave him a job and watched him grow. (Bloomberg, 2016)
- **The handover.** In 2013 the young man became chief investment officer and Sawakami's son became president. (Bloomberg, 2016)
- **A hard start.** Investors withdrew ¥126.5 billion over three years after the handover. (Bloomberg, 2016)
- **Years, not months.** He said he would not judge his successor until five to ten years into the job. (Bloomberg, 2016)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-09-letting-go.html

### 10. NISA, Japan, and an honest note

He welcomed Japan's tax-free NISA accounts but warned about timing, and this library also sets out the one regulatory action on record against his firm.

- **Great system, bad timing.** He said the new NISA, launched in January 2024, was a great system started at the worst possible time. (President, 2024)
- **Don't fear the turn.** He expected the trend to turn over the following decade and found that exciting. (Toyo Keizai, 2014)
- **The 2006 order.** In 2006 Japan's regulator found his firm had placed share trades for overseas advisory clients, which the law then barred, and had kept incomplete records. (FSA, 2006)
- **The penalty.** It ordered a one-month halt to new advisory contracts and a plan to improve compliance. (FSA, 2006)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sawakami-10-nisa-japan-and-an-honest-note.html

## Special section

**Investing or money-rolling? Tap your answer**

- The Nikkei falls 25% in three months. You add to the funds you already own. Answer: Long-term investing. Buying more as prices fall is exactly his description of the long-term investor.
- A share has tripled this year and everyone on TV is talking about it, so you buy. Answer: Money-rolling. Buying because the price has risen is the habit he says most people fall into.
- You switch to whichever fund topped last year's table, every year. Answer: Money-rolling. Chasing last year's winner is judging on short-term results, the trap he says even pensions fall into.
- You buy a company because people will need what it makes in ten years. Answer: Long-term investing. Asking how a company serves people's lives a decade ahead is his test.
- One bad quarter, and you sell a sound company you planned to hold for years. Answer: Money-rolling. Selling on short-term news is the opposite of thinking in decades.
- Your monthly NISA contribution keeps going through a crash, untouched. Answer: Long-term investing. Steady buying through falls lets you buy more when prices are low.

**An honest note.** On 30 March 2006 Japan's Financial Services Agency took action against Sawakami Investment Trust. It found that the firm had placed securities orders many times for overseas clients with whom it had investment advisory contracts, which the investment advisory law of the time prohibited, and that it had not kept or delivered certain required documents for that business. The agency ordered the firm not to take on new investment advisory contracts for one month, from 3 April to 2 May 2006, and to set up a proper compliance system, strengthen internal controls and report on its measures. We include it because this library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

## Words to know

- **Investment trust (投資信託)**: Japan's name for a mutual fund, often shortened to tōshin.
- **Independent fund company**: A fund manager not owned by a bank, broker or insurance group.
- **Direct sales**: Selling a fund straight to savers, with no broker or bank in between.
- **Up-front commission**: A fee taken from your money the moment you invest.
- **Nikkei 225**: Japan's best-known share index; it peaked at the end of 1989 and took decades to recover.
- **NISA**: Japan's tax-free investment account, launched in 2014 and expanded in 2024.
- **Money-rolling (マネー転がし)**: His term for trading on price moves rather than owning businesses.
- **FSA**: Japan's Financial Services Agency, the financial regulator.
