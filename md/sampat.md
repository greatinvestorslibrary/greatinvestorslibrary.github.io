# Chandrakant Sampat

> India's original value investor: left the family business in the 1950s, bought the Indian arms of global companies cheaply, and taught a generation.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/sampat.html · Section: India · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Own businesses, not factories. Buying shares lets you share a business's growth without running it.

## Summary

Chandrakant Sampat is called India's original value investor. He left his family's business in the 1950s to buy shares, made his fortune owning the Indian arms of global consumer companies when few others saw their worth, and taught a generation, including Parag Parikh and Radhakishan Damani. He wrote no books and gave few interviews; he jogged, read, lived simply and kept to a handful of strict rules.

## Record at a glance

- **Mid-1950s**: began investing, when the Controller of Capital Issues still governed India's new share issues
- **₹16**: the price at which he bought Gillette's Indian arm, then called Indian Shaving Products
- **8–10 years**: his signal to buy a good company: its share price at an eight- or ten-year low
- **7 km**: his daily jog, from Haji Ali to Marine Drive, well into old age

## Big ideas

### 1. Own businesses, don't build factories

He left his family's business in the 1950s, drawn to shares. Buying a slice of a good listed business, he believed, beats the struggle of building one from scratch.

- **A first-generation investor.** He began in the mid-1950s, when the Controller of Capital Issues still oversaw new share issues, after leaving his family's business. (ET, 2015)
- **Advice to a young man.** When a young Parag Parikh's plan to make toothpaste tubes was rejected, Sampat told him to buy shares in good businesses instead: share the growth, skip the factory. (Obituary, 2015)
- **Easy to exit.** In those days, closing a business in India could drag on endlessly. A share could simply be sold. (Obituary, 2015)
- **A reader.** He read The Economist and Fortune closely, keeping an eye on business far beyond India. (Obituary, 2015)

### 2. The FERA windfall

In the 1970s, India's Foreign Exchange Regulation Act forced many foreign companies to sell shares to Indian investors, often cheaply. Sampat bought the best of them and held for decades.

- **Forced sellers.** FERA required foreign companies to dilute their holdings at prices below their real value. He made a killing. (Obituary, 2015)
- **Hindustan Lever in the teens.** He bought Hindustan Lever when its shares were priced in the teens. (Obituary, 2015)
- **Gillette at ₹16.** Later he bought Gillette's Indian arm at ₹16 a share, when it was still called Indian Shaving Products. (Obituary, 2015)
- **Decades of holding.** He held his consumer companies for decades; a profile names Hindustan Lever and Nestlé among his picks. (Profile)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-02-the-fera-windfall.html

### 3. Cash flow and durability

For Sampat a good investment had to throw off cash, year after year, and last. He judged businesses by their cash, not their story.

- **Three tests.** He looked for positive cash flow, cash flow that lasts, and barriers that keep rivals out. (Round table, 2000)
- **The 2003 definition.** A business qualifies as a good investment only if it generates cash and shows durability. (Interview, 2003)
- **His screens.** He favoured high return on capital employed, a long record of dividends and little need for capital spending. Few companies passed. (ET, 2015)
- **Not the spreadsheet.** He warned people to beware of those he called spreadsheet specialists. (Tribute, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-03-cash-flow-and-durability.html

### 4. Buy at the lows

Find good companies with good managements, then wait until their share price sinks to an eight- or ten-year low. Buy from pessimists; sell to optimists.

- **His rule.** Pick up good companies with good managements when the share price is at an eight- or ten-year low. (Profile)
- **A second rule.** If you must act sooner, buy good companies about 40% below their 52-week high. (Profile)
- **Pessimists and optimists.** Buy from pessimists, sell to optimists, and be patient in between. (Tribute, 2015)
- **Not rigid.** He preferred a P/E of 15 or less, but said to focus on the quality and longevity of the business, and not to be rigid about the number. (Tribute, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-04-buy-at-the-lows.html

### 5. Simple, with little debt

He wanted businesses simple enough for anyone to understand, carrying little debt and needing little new money to grow.

- **Even a fool.** He bought only businesses that even a fool could understand, with very little debt. (Profile)
- **Capital-light.** A great business needs little extra capital to grow; a weak one needs capital forever. (Tribute, 2015)
- **Drucker's reader.** He was strongly influenced by the management thinker Peter Drucker. (ET, 2015 · Tribute, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-05-simple-with-little-debt.html

### 6. Muscle, fat or cancer

Not all growth is good. He sorted it into muscle, fat and cancer, and warned that nothing grows exponentially for long.

- **Three kinds of growth.** Good growth is muscle, bad growth is fat, and some growth ultimately kills, like cancer. (Tribute, 2015)
- **Value, not volume.** Gillette was his example: the number of razors sold grows slowly, but the value of each sale grows a lot. (Tribute, 2015)
- **Nature's accountant.** He compared nature to a miserly accountant that punishes even the smallest extravagance. Businesses, he implied, are no different. (Tribute, 2015)
- **No exponential forever.** In 2000 he told the software enthusiasts that nothing grows exponentially for long. (Round table, 2000)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-06-muscle-fat-or-cancer.html

### 7. Technology and creative destruction

He believed new technology destroys old businesses ever faster. That made him wary of fashionable shares and focused on durability.

- **Software in 2000.** At the height of the technology boom he argued that Indian software firms lacked free cash flow. (Round table, 2000)
- **Shorter lives.** In 2003 he said faster technology would shorten the lives of companies and the business cycle. (Interview, 2003)
- **Creative destruction.** He drew on Joseph Schumpeter, the economist who described how innovation keeps destroying old businesses. (Tribute, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-07-technology-and-creative-destruction.html

### 8. Integrity: humility plus courage

Sampat defined integrity as humility plus courage, and lived it. His four touchstone values were high enlightenment, high integrity, low expectation and hard work.

- **His definition.** Integrity, he said, is humility plus courage. (Tribute, 2015)
- **Thought, speech, action.** A friend described his ethics as complete harmony between what he thought, said and did. (Tribute, 2015)
- **An open letter.** In 2006 he wrote an open letter to the directors of Wyeth India over the transfer of a drug's marketing rights. (ET, 2015)
- **Old wisdom.** He drew on the Bhagavad Gita and Jain scriptures, as well as on Drucker and Schumpeter. (Tribute, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-08-integrity-humility-plus-courage.html

### 9. How many things can you do without?

One of India's richest investors lived simply, jogged daily and asked how little he needed, not how much he could have.

- **His question.** Parag Parikh said Sampat's philosophy was how many things you can do without. (ET, 2015)
- **The morning run.** For years he jogged about seven kilometres a day, from Haji Ali to Marine Drive. (ET, 2015 · Profile)
- **Against excess.** He objected to excessive consumption, debt, money-printing and short-termism. (Tribute, 2015)
- **Think for yourself.** Take the best ideas from great thinkers in every field, connect them yourself, and take nothing and nobody for granted. (Tribute, 2015)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/sampat-09-how-many-things-can-you-do-without.html

### 10. A teacher, and his blind spot

He shaped some of India's best investors. But his caution in later years also cost him, which he would have wanted us to learn from too.

- **Teacher.** Parag Parikh said he always walked away wiser after sitting with Sampat. (Obituary, 2015)
- **Sitting it out.** In his last years he stayed out of the market, worried that cheap money had inflated prices, and missed a strong rally in shares like Hindustan Unilever and Nestlé. (ET, 2015)
- **Right idea, wrong timing.** A friend wrote that he foresaw a credit-driven bubble but may have misjudged when it would end. (Tribute, 2015)
- **A bold forecast.** In 2003 he feared fast-changing technology could spell doom for capital markets within a few years. That did not happen. (Interview, 2003)

## Words to know

- **Dalal Street**: The Mumbai street of the Bombay Stock Exchange; shorthand for India's stock market.
- **FERA**: The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973, which pushed many foreign companies to cut their stakes in Indian units.
- **Controller of Capital Issues**: The government office that approved and priced new share issues in India before 1992.
- **Return on capital employed (RoCE)**: Operating profit as a share of the money tied up in the business.
- **Free cash flow**: Cash a business generates after paying for the investment it needs.
- **Capex**: Capital expenditure: spending on plants, machines and other long-lived assets.
- **FMCG**: Fast-moving consumer goods: everyday items like soap, razors and food.
- **₹1 crore**: Ten million rupees; ₹1 lakh is one hundred thousand.
