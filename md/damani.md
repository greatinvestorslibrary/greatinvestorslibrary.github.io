# Radhakishan Damani

> Dalal Street's famous bear turned patient investor, then built DMart from one Mumbai store on low costs, owned shops and fast payments.

Source page: https://alienspacex.com/damani.html · Section: India · Updated 2026-11-28

## Core idea

Leave the small pond. Be a small fish in a big sea rather than the biggest in a pond.

## Summary

Radhakishan Damani was a famous bear, or short seller, on Dalal Street in the Harshad Mehta years, then a patient long-term investor, and then, from 2000, the founder of DMart, one of India's most successful retailers. He almost never speaks in public. His lessons are in what he did: own quality, keep costs lower than anyone, pay suppliers fast, own the shop, and grow slowly until the model is proven.

## Record at a glance

- **2000**: conceived DMart, after leaving active stock market trading
- **365 stores**: by March 2024, from two in its first year
- **104 times**: how many times DMart's ₹1,870 crore IPO was subscribed in March 2017
- **≈ 85%**: share of DMart's stores it owned rather than rented in 2017, by one analyst's count

## Big ideas

### 1. Leave the small pond

He did not start in the market. He ran a ball-bearings business until an uncle asked him: how rich is the top ball-bearings trader, next to Dalal Street's biggest names?

- **Dalal Street family.** He grew up in Mumbai, where his father worked on Dalal Street, and left college after a year of commerce. (BS, 2020)
- **Ball bearings.** He first ran a ball-bearings business and had no interest in the stock market. (BS, 2020)
- **The uncle's lesson.** After his father died, an uncle reportedly persuaded him that real wealth meant leaving a small pond, even as its biggest fish. (BS, 2020)
- **Bull and bear.** On Dalal Street he became skilled at both kinds of bet: on rises and on falls. (BS, 2017)

### 2. The bear in the Harshad Mehta years

In the early 1990s, while Harshad Mehta drove prices to dizzying heights with borrowed money, Damani bet on a fall. He was right, but it hurt before it paid.

- **On the short side.** He was a known bear, and twice took the other side of Harshad Mehta's rallies, winning both times. (BS, 2017)
- **The painful trade.** He sold ACC shares at about ₹3,000 and kept selling as they rose towards ₹10,000. Losses mounted until the prices finally cracked. (BS, 2020)
- **A bear's school.** He was a keen follower of Manu Manek, a famous short seller of his early years. (Profile)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-02-the-bear-in-the-harshad-mehta-years.html

### 3. From trader to owner

Influenced by Chandrakant Sampat, he moved from trading to owning quality businesses for years. Fund managers said they could never tell which hat he was wearing.

- **Sampat's influence.** He has often said he was inspired by Chandrakant Sampat, whom he met in the early 1990s. (Profile)
- **HDFC Bank.** By market folklore he was the largest individual shareholder of HDFC Bank at its 1995 listing, and kept buying. (BS, 2017)
- **Quality costs more.** He was willing to pay up for HDFC Bank; in his view, quality commands a premium. (BS, 2017)
- **Both at once.** A fund manager said it was impossible to follow him, never knowing whether he was investing or trading in a share. (BS, 2017)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-03-from-trader-to-owner.html

### 4. Listen more than you talk

He rarely speaks in public and has never appeared on television. Friends say he says little and listens a lot, asking people what they see.

- **Silence.** He rarely speaks publicly, has never appeared on television, and keeps his views to himself. (BS, 2017)
- **A listener.** People who know him say he listens far more than he speaks. (BS, 2017)
- **The shopper first.** When a friend complained that DMart staff hovered around him while he shopped, Damani told them never to do it again. (BS, 2017)
- **No interviews.** He did not respond to Business Standard's interview request for its 2020 profile. (BS, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-04-listen-more-than-you-talk.html

### 5. Why a shop?

In 2000, at the height of his market success, he turned to retailing, after studying how Indians shop.

- **Research first.** The company says he spent a couple of years in research and reflection, testing his ideas about the Indian consumer, before starting. (Annual report, 2023)
- **Apna Bazaar.** In the late 1990s he ran a franchise of Apna Bazaar, a cooperative store chain, in Navi Mumbai. DMart refined the idea. (BS, 2020)
- **Conceived in 2000.** DMart's own annual report says he conceived the chain in 2000. (Annual report, 2019)
- **Walmart.** He was reportedly inspired by reading about Walmart. (BS, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-05-why-a-shop.html

### 6. Everyday low cost, everyday low price

DMart's model is simple to say and hard to copy: keep costs lower than anyone, so prices can be lower than anyone's, every day, without flashy discounts.

- **EDLC/EDLP.** The company offers value retailing using its Everyday Low Cost, Everyday Low Price principle. (Annual report, 2019)
- **Buy direct, pay fast.** It buys directly from manufacturers and pays vendors early, which earns better purchase prices. (Research, 2017)
- **A week, not months.** It reportedly pays suppliers within about a week, against the usual three months. (BS, 2020)
- **Fast-moving goods.** It focuses on items that sell quickly, and on high sales from every square foot. (BS, 2020)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-06-everyday-low-cost-everyday-low-price.html

### 7. Own the shop

Most retailers rent. DMart prefers to buy land and build its own stores, which keeps costs stable and avoids rent shocks.

- **Buy, then build.** Its chief executive has said the company typically prefers to buy land and then construct the buildings. (Annual report, 2019)
- **Mostly owned.** In 2017 an analyst counted about 85% of its 131 stores as owned. (Research, 2017)
- **A model, not a policy.** Store ownership is part of the low-cost model that lets it keep prices low. (DSIJ, 2024)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-07-own-the-shop.html

### 8. Slow, then fast

He grew DMart slowly for years, proving the model in a few regions before expanding. Then it grew quickly, cluster by cluster.

- **Eight years, ten stores.** DMart took eight years to open its first ten stores. The company says he wanted to prove the model both profitable and scalable first. (Annual report, 2023)
- **Then faster.** It had about 25 stores by 2010, then sped up. (BS, 2017)
- **Clusters.** The company deepens its presence where it already operates before moving to new regions. (Annual report, 2019 · Annual report, 2023)
- **The count.** It had 176 stores in 11 states and a union territory in 2019, and 365 by March 2024. (Annual report, 2019 · DSIJ, 2024)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-08-slow-then-fast.html

### 9. Frugal at the top

The man who became one of India's richest kept DMart's head office in a corner of an early store and worked without hierarchy.

- **A corner of a store.** For years DMart's corporate office was a small space carved out of one of its early stores. (Annual report, 2019)
- **No hierarchy.** He and his early team worked as one unit, without hierarchy or barriers. (Annual report, 2019)
- **A shared room.** After the IPO he reportedly shared a budget hotel room with his buying team on a trip to China. (BS, 2020)
- **The founder's thinking.** The company credits its success to the values and way of thinking he instilled. (Annual report, 2023)
  Web page: https://alienspacex.com/ideas/damani-09-frugal-at-the-top.html

### 10. Listing day, and an honest note

In 2017 DMart's listing showed what patient building can be worth. And since this library puts integrity first, we also set out the one regulatory matter on record.

- **The IPO.** Avenue Supermarts' ₹1,870 crore IPO, priced at ₹299 a share, was subscribed more than 104 times. (Listing, 2017)
- **First day.** The shares listed on 21 March 2017 at more than double the issue price. (Listing, 2017)
- **The 2007 order.** SEBI examined trading by three broking firms of the R.S. Damani group in 2001. It found no evidence of manipulation, but censured the firms for delays in moving shares to clients. (SEBI, 2007)
- **Not a party.** Damani appeared at the hearing as a director of one firm; the order was against the three companies, not him personally. (SEBI, 2007)

## Special section

**Tap each step of the DMart flywheel** 

**An honest note.** SEBI's order of 2 January 2007 concerned three broking firms of the R.S. Damani group: Damani Shares & Stock Brokers, Maheshwari Equity Brokers and Avenue Stock Brokers. SEBI examined their sales of Infosys shares on 23 February and 2 March 2001, and other dealings. It found no evidence of price manipulation, held that alleged links to two other market groups were not established, and found the firms had broadly complied with short-sale rules. The one violation upheld was that the firms had moved securities from their pool accounts to clients' accounts later than the 15-day limit. SEBI treated this as a technical violation with no bad intent, and imposed censure, a minor penalty. Radhakishan Damani appeared at the hearing as a director of one firm; he was not a party to the order. We include it because this library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

## Words to know

- **Short selling**: Selling borrowed shares, hoping to buy them back cheaper after a fall.
- **Bear**: An investor who expects prices to fall.
- **EDLC/EDLP**: Everyday Low Cost, Everyday Low Price: keeping costs lowest so prices can stay low every day.
- **Cluster expansion**: Opening new stores near existing ones before entering new regions.
- **IPO**: Initial public offering: a company's first sale of shares to the public.
- **Subscribed 104 times**: Investors applied for 104 times as many shares as were on offer.
- **Censure**: A formal reprimand from a regulator; a minor penalty.
- **Pool account**: An account where a broker holds clients' shares before passing them on.
