Warren Buffett

His thinking in five minutes

What never changed

  • A share is part of a business.
  • Price and value are different; insist on a margin between them.
  • Stay inside what you understand.
  • No leverage that could force your hand. Large cash reserves always.
  • Never issue shares for less than they are worth.
  • Trust able, honest managers and leave them alone.
  • Write to owners plainly, bad news first.
  • Costs and activity are the investor's enemy.
  • Patience: few decisions, held a long time.

What changed over thirty years

  • From bargains to quality. He began by buying mediocre companies at very low prices. Munger moved him to excellent companies at fair prices. The shift happened before 1995, and he retells it in 2014 and 2023 as the decision that built Berkshire.
  • From capital-light to capital-heavy. See's was the ideal: high returns, little capital. By 2009 he had more cash than such businesses could absorb, so he bought a railroad and utilities and accepted moderate returns on very large sums.
  • From avoiding technology to owning Apple. In 1999 he would not buy what he could not forecast. From 2016 Apple became a core holding, seen as a consumer franchise.
  • From book value to operating earnings. Book value was his scorecard until 2018, when he retired it as no longer meaningful.
  • From no buybacks to large ones. Considered and not done in 1999. A price rule of 110% of book in 2011, raised to 120% in 2012, replaced by judgement in 2018, then used heavily in 2020 and 2021.
  • From America only to abroad. The first overseas acquisition came in 2006. The Japanese stakes followed from 2019.
  • Towards index funds for others. Hints in 1996, a clear statement in 2003, the instruction in his own will in 2013, and the ten-year bet as proof in 2016 and 2017.
  • Towards open confession. The later letters spend more space on his errors. In 2024 he counted how often he had used the words mistake and error.

The big ideas

  1. A share is a slice of a business. You are buying a claim on what a company will earn.
  2. Moats: what makes a business worth owning. A great business earns a high return on the money inside it, and can keep doing so because something stops competitors from taking it away.
  3. Price, value and the margin of safety. Value is the cash a business will hand its owners over time.
  4. Mr. Market and temperament. Prices swing far more than values do.
  5. Activity, costs and patience. The return from owning good businesses is earned by sitting.
  6. Risk, debt and survival. Risk is the chance of losing capital for good.
  7. Circle of competence. You do not need to understand many businesses.
  8. Capital allocation: what happens to retained profit. Over a decade, what management does with the profit it keeps counts for more than the profit itself.
  9. Acquisitions: a test for any company that buys others. Most deals help the seller and the adviser.
  10. Managers, pay and boards. He looks for people who run a company as if it were their family's only asset, and for pay and boards that keep them that way.
  11. Accounting: read like an owner. Reported numbers are where analysis starts.
  12. Insurance: float and the discipline of saying no. Every letter returns to insurance.
  13. Mistakes: his own list. Read what he took from each one.
  14. Productive assets, inflation and the long view. Wealth compounds in things that produce: businesses, farms, property.

His own mistakes

  • Sold his early GEICO stake; took Gillette preferred when the common was available. Selling a great business early is expensive. A clever structure earned less than plain ownership. (1995)
  • USAir preferred shares. Seniority in the capital structure does not protect you when the business economics are broken. (1996)
  • Sold McDonald's and trimmed other holdings. Activity cost money. Shareholders would have done better had he done nothing. (1998)
  • Worst year against the index; graded his own stock-picking a D. Take the blame in plain words, and keep the method. (1999)
  • Dexter Shoe: the moat vanished to imports, and he paid in Berkshire shares. Two errors at once: misjudged durability, and paid with a currency that became far more valuable. (2000 · 2007 · 2014)
  • Did not sell richly priced holdings in the bubble; too slow closing General Re's derivatives book. Talking about over-valuation is not acting on it. Exits get harder with delay. (2003 · 2004)
  • Bought ConocoPhillips near the top of the oil price; lost most of a stake in two Irish banks. A good company bought on a commodity peak is still a poor purchase. (2008)
  • A GEICO credit-card venture he pushed for. Staying inside the circle applies to the chairman's own ideas as well. (2009)
  • Energy Future Holdings bonds, bought without consulting Munger. A bet resting on one commodity price. Talk to your sceptic before, not after. (2011 · 2013)
  • Held Tesco after his doubts grew. Also retold 1964: took control of the textile mill out of pique over an eighth of a dollar. Sell when the thesis breaks. Pride is an expensive adviser. (2014)
  • Paid too much for Precision Castparts; $11 billion write-down. A wonderful business and able managers do not rescue an optimistic price. (2020)
  • Misjudged regulatory returns in utilities; the railroad needs more capital than depreciation shows. Capital-heavy businesses are only as good as the return the system allows. (2023)
  • Errors in judging people, and delay in correcting them. A decent batting average is all one can hope for. The sin is waiting. (2024)

Test yourself

  1. Explain the three savings accounts, with a real company you know for each.
  2. Why should a net buyer of shares want prices to fall?
  3. State Aesop's three questions and apply them to one holding.
  4. What is the difference between volatility and risk? Give the time horizon at which shares become the safer asset.
  5. What two conditions make a buyback sensible?
  6. Retell the Gotrocks story in one minute.
  7. Name his four types of costly mistake.
  8. Why does paying with shares magnify an acquisition error?
  9. What are the four disciplines of underwriting, and which one do most firms fail?
  10. How did Munger change his approach, and what are the limits of that change?
  11. What did he change his mind about after 2009, and why?
  12. Write one page, in his plain style, to the owners of your own portfolio about this year.

Words to know

Intrinsic value
The cash a business will hand its owners over its remaining life, valued today. Always an estimate, best held as a range.
Margin of safety
The gap between that value and the price you pay. It protects you when your estimate is wrong.
Moat
Whatever stops competitors from taking a business's profits: a brand, low costs, a network, a licence.
Mr. Market
Benjamin Graham's imaginary partner who offers to buy or sell every day at prices set by his mood. Use his offers; never take his advice.
Circle of competence
The businesses you genuinely understand well enough to value. Knowing its edge matters more than its size.
Retained earnings
Profit a company keeps and reinvests instead of paying out. What it earns on that money decides the next decade.
Buyback
A company buying its own shares. Sensible only with spare cash and a price below value.
Float
Money an insurer holds between collecting premiums and paying claims, invested in the meantime.
Owner earnings
Profit after the spending needed just to keep the business where it is.
Book value
Assets minus liabilities in the accounts. Buffett's yardstick for decades, retired in 2018.
