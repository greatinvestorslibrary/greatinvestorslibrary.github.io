His thinking in five minutes

Atsuto Sawakami ran the Japanese arm of the Swiss private bank Pictet, then in 1999 founded Japan's first fund company with no ties to a big financial group. He sold one fund, directly, with no up-front commission, and preached long-term investing through decades when few in Japan wanted to hear it. He refused pension money as too short-term. Bloomberg called him Japan's first evangelist of long-term investing.

  • 1999: founded Japan's first fund company independent of the big financial groups
  • 1 fund: sold directly, with no up-front commission
  • +9%: his fund's gain in the year to March 2001, while the Nikkei fell 36%
  • 10 years: the shortest horizon he says an investor should think in

His thinking, one line each

  • Investing, not money-rolling. Buying and selling on price moves is not investing.
  • Buy as prices fall. The long-term investor buys more when prices drop.
  • Think in decades. Ask how a company will serve people's lives ten years on.
  • Cut out the middlemen. Sell direct, charge little, and tell investors everything.
  • Beware short-term money. Even pensions have become short-termist.
  • Patience with people. Let successors learn, and judge them over years.

The big ideas

  1. The outsider from Pictet. He spent sixteen years at the head of a Swiss private bank's Japanese business, then left to build a fund company for ordinary savers, owned by no big group.
  2. Investing, not money-rolling. He divides the market in two: real long-term investing, and what he calls money-rolling, buying and selling on price moves.
  3. Buy when they sell. His fund adds to holdings when others sell and trims when others buy.
  4. Proof in a bad year, and the long record. In 2001, as Japanese shares crashed, his fund rose and gained customers.
  5. Why even pensions turned short-term. He argues that pension funds, which should be the most patient money, became short-termist once they began judging managers every year.
  6. Sell direct, charge little. He sold his fund straight to savers, with no up-front commission, a low fee, no advertising and no sales force.
  7. Tell investors everything. He believed savers should be told more, not less, and reported on every move every month.
  8. Think in decades. Look at least ten years ahead, he says, and ask how a company helps people live now and will help them in future.
  9. Letting go. He hired a former part-time actor, handed him the fund, and promised to judge him only over years, even when investors left and the style changed.
  10. NISA, Japan, and an honest note. He welcomed Japan's tax-free NISA accounts but warned about timing, and this library also sets out the one regulatory action on record against his firm.

Investing or money-rolling?

Investing or money-rolling? Tap your answer

  1. The Nikkei falls 25% in three months. You add to the funds you already own. Long-term investing. Buying more as prices fall is exactly his description of the long-term investor.
  2. A share has tripled this year and everyone on TV is talking about it, so you buy. Money-rolling. Buying because the price has risen is the habit he says most people fall into.
  3. You switch to whichever fund topped last year's table, every year. Money-rolling. Chasing last year's winner is judging on short-term results, the trap he says even pensions fall into.
  4. You buy a company because people will need what it makes in ten years. Long-term investing. Asking how a company serves people's lives a decade ahead is his test.
  5. One bad quarter, and you sell a sound company you planned to hold for years. Money-rolling. Selling on short-term news is the opposite of thinking in decades.
  6. Your monthly NISA contribution keeps going through a crash, untouched. Long-term investing. Steady buying through falls lets you buy more when prices are low.

Our own examples of the distinction he draws. A study aid, not advice.

An honest note

On 30 March 2006 Japan's Financial Services Agency took action against Sawakami Investment Trust. It found that the firm had placed securities orders many times for overseas clients with whom it had investment advisory contracts, which the investment advisory law of the time prohibited, and that it had not kept or delivered certain required documents for that business. The agency ordered the firm not to take on new investment advisory contracts for one month, from 3 April to 2 May 2006, and to set up a proper compliance system, strengthen internal controls and report on its measures. We include it because this library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Words to know

Investment trust (投資信託)
Japan's name for a mutual fund, often shortened to tōshin.
Independent fund company
A fund manager not owned by a bank, broker or insurance group.
Direct sales
Selling a fund straight to savers, with no broker or bank in between.
Up-front commission
A fee taken from your money the moment you invest.
Nikkei 225
Japan's best-known share index; it peaked at the end of 1989 and took decades to recover.
NISA
Japan's tax-free investment account, launched in 2014 and expanded in 2024.
Money-rolling (マネー転がし)
His term for trading on price moves rather than owning businesses.
FSA
Japan's Financial Services Agency, the financial regulator.
