His thinking in five minutes

Seth Klarman has run the Baupost Group in Boston since 1982, when four families hired him at 25 to manage about $27 million. In 1991 he wrote Margin of Safety, which sold few copies, went out of print and became a cult classic. His creed is Graham's, pushed further: avoid losing money first, value things cautiously, and wait for the market to offer a big discount.

  • 1982: founded Baupost with about $27 million from four families
  • ≈ 20% a year: the reported net return of Baupost's largest partnership, with one losing year from 1983 to the late 2000s
  • 5,000 copies: the first print run of Margin of Safety at $25; used copies later sold for over $1,000
  • $22 billion: Baupost's assets by 2026, as reported

His thinking, one line each

  • Avoid loss first. The first goal is not to lose money; returns come second.
  • Margin of safety. Buy at a big enough discount to survive mistakes and bad luck.
  • Value cautiously. Use several methods and lean on the most conservative.
  • Look where others aren't. Complex, unloved, forced-sale areas hold the bargains.
  • Cheap isn't enough. Look for a catalyst that can close the gap.
  • Patience and cash. Hold cash when there's nothing good to buy, though not too long.

The big ideas

  1. Avoid loss first. Klarman puts avoiding loss ahead of everything.
  2. Margin of safety. Valuation is never precise, the future is unknowable and people make mistakes.
  3. Investors and speculators. Investors buy businesses below their value; speculators bet on which way prices will move.
  4. The performance derby. Many professional investors are judged every quarter against an index.
  5. Value it three ways. No single method gives the true value.
  6. Look where others aren't. Bargains are most common in complicated, unpopular or forced-sale situations that most investors avoid.
  7. Cheap isn't enough. A cheap share can stay cheap for years.
  8. Patience and cash. When there is nothing good to buy, hold cash rather than take more risk.
  9. Fear is useful. Klarman sees fear as protective.
  10. A book that landed with a thud. His one book sold poorly, went out of print and became one of the most sought-after investing books in the world.

Three values, one margin

Value a business three ways

Estimate a share's value from its future cash, its break-up value and what similar firms fetch. Lean on the lowest, and compare it with the price: the gap is your margin of safety.

A study aid built on the methods his book discusses. Enter your own estimates; the tool leans on the most cautious. Not a recommendation.

Where he looks for bargains

  1. Distressed and bankrupt companies, which many funds are not allowed to hold.
  2. Complicated situations that take real work to understand.
  3. Forced sellers, who must sell for rules or cash, not value.
  4. Unpopular areas that the crowd has given up on.
  5. Situations with a catalyst that can close the gap between price and value.

Words to know

Margin of safety
The gap between a cautious estimate of value and the price you pay.
Absolute return
A return judged on its own, not against an index.
Liquidation value
What a business would fetch if its assets were sold off and debts paid.
Present value
What future cash is worth today, after allowing for time and risk.
Catalyst
An event that can make the market recognise value.
Distressed debt
Bonds and loans of companies in, or close to, financial trouble.
Thrift conversion
When a savings bank owned by its depositors sells shares to the public.
