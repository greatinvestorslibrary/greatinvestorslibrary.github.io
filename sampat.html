His thinking in five minutes

Chandrakant Sampat is called India's original value investor. He left his family's business in the 1950s to buy shares, made his fortune owning the Indian arms of global consumer companies when few others saw their worth, and taught a generation, including Parag Parikh and Radhakishan Damani. He wrote no books and gave few interviews; he jogged, read, lived simply and kept to a handful of strict rules.

  • Mid-1950s: began investing, when the Controller of Capital Issues still governed India's new share issues
  • ₹16: the price at which he bought Gillette's Indian arm, then called Indian Shaving Products
  • 8–10 years: his signal to buy a good company: its share price at an eight- or ten-year low
  • 7 km: his daily jog, from Haji Ali to Marine Drive, well into old age

His thinking, one line each

  • Own businesses, not factories. Buying shares lets you share a business's growth without running it.
  • Cash and durability. A good investment throws off cash and lasts for decades.
  • Buy at the lows. Wait for a good company's price to sink to an eight- or ten-year low.
  • Muscle, not fat. Growth that needs little new money is good; growth that eats capital is not.
  • Integrity first. Humility plus courage, in markets and in life.
  • How much can you do without?. Live simply, stay curious, and question everything.

The big ideas

  1. Own businesses, don't build factories. He left his family's business in the 1950s, drawn to shares.
  2. The FERA windfall. In the 1970s, India's Foreign Exchange Regulation Act forced many foreign companies to sell shares to Indian investors, often cheaply.
  3. Cash flow and durability. For Sampat a good investment had to throw off cash, year after year, and last.
  4. Buy at the lows. Find good companies with good managements, then wait until their share price sinks to an eight- or ten-year low.
  5. Simple, with little debt. He wanted businesses simple enough for anyone to understand, carrying little debt and needing little new money to grow.
  6. Muscle, fat or cancer. Not all growth is good.
  7. Technology and creative destruction. He believed new technology destroys old businesses ever faster.
  8. Integrity: humility plus courage. Sampat defined integrity as humility plus courage, and lived it.
  9. How many things can you do without?. One of India's richest investors lived simply, jogged daily and asked how little he needed, not how much he could have.
  10. A teacher, and his blind spot. He shaped some of India's best investors.

The Sampat screen

Try his screen on a company

  1. A business even a fool could understand. You could explain what it sells and why people pay, in two sentences.
  2. Good management. Honest, capable, and fair to minority shareholders.
  3. Little debt. It could repay its borrowings easily from a few years of cash.
  4. Cash flow, year after year. It turns profit into cash and pays dividends regularly.
  5. Needs little capital to grow. Not much spending on new plants each year.
  6. Price at or near an 8–10-year low. His preferred moment to buy a good company.
  7. How far below its 52-week high? (%). His second rule: about 40% or more below.
  8. Price to earnings (P/E). He preferred 15 or less, but warned not to be rigid.

His touchstone

  1. High enlightenment: keep learning from every discipline.
  2. High integrity: humility plus courage.
  3. Low expectation: expect little, and you're rarely disappointed.
  4. Hard work: there is no substitute.

The four values he called essential for surviving a turbulent world, as recorded by Rohan Shah.

Words to know

Dalal Street
The Mumbai street of the Bombay Stock Exchange; shorthand for India's stock market.
FERA
The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973, which pushed many foreign companies to cut their stakes in Indian units.
Controller of Capital Issues
The government office that approved and priced new share issues in India before 1992.
Return on capital employed (RoCE)
Operating profit as a share of the money tied up in the business.
Free cash flow
Cash a business generates after paying for the investment it needs.
Capex
Capital expenditure: spending on plants, machines and other long-lived assets.
FMCG
Fast-moving consumer goods: everyday items like soap, razors and food.
₹1 crore
Ten million rupees; ₹1 lakh is one hundred thousand.

Related investors

Also from India

  • Parag Parikh. Started as a sub-broker in 1979, became India's voice of behavioural finance, and built a fund with one scheme and his own money in it.
  • Radhakishan Damani. Dalal Street's famous bear turned patient investor, then built DMart from one Mumbai store on low costs, owned shops and fast payments.

From other countries

  • Francisco García Paramés. Taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch and Buffett's letters, then beat Spain's market for 21 years, survived his worst years, and kept going.
  • Mark Minervini. Lost all his capital in the 1980s, then studied history's biggest winning stocks and won the U.S. Investing Championship twice, in 1997 and 2021.
  • John Templeton. Bought 104 cheap shares as war broke out in 1939, then searched the whole world for bargains at the point of maximum pessimism.
