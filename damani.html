His thinking in five minutes

Radhakishan Damani was a famous bear, or short seller, on Dalal Street in the Harshad Mehta years, then a patient long-term investor, and then, from 2000, the founder of DMart, one of India's most successful retailers. He almost never speaks in public. His lessons are in what he did: own quality, keep costs lower than anyone, pay suppliers fast, own the shop, and grow slowly until the model is proven.

  • 2000: conceived DMart, after leaving active stock market trading
  • 365 stores: by March 2024, from two in its first year
  • 104 times: how many times DMart's ₹1,870 crore IPO was subscribed in March 2017
  • ≈ 85%: share of DMart's stores it owned rather than rented in 2017, by one analyst's count

His thinking, one line each

  • Leave the small pond. Be a small fish in a big sea rather than the biggest in a pond.
  • Trader to owner. From short-selling to owning quality businesses for decades.
  • Listen more than you talk. He gathers views and keeps his own counsel.
  • Everyday low cost. Keep costs lowest so prices can be lowest, every day.
  • Own the shop. Buy the land and build, rather than rent.
  • Slow, then fast. Prove the model in a cluster of stores, then expand.

The big ideas

  1. Leave the small pond. He did not start in the market.
  2. The bear in the Harshad Mehta years. In the early 1990s, while Harshad Mehta drove prices to dizzying heights with borrowed money, Damani bet on a fall.
  3. From trader to owner. Influenced by Chandrakant Sampat, he moved from trading to owning quality businesses for years.
  4. Listen more than you talk. He rarely speaks in public and has never appeared on television.
  5. Why a shop?. In 2000, at the height of his market success, he turned to retailing, after studying how Indians shop.
  6. Everyday low cost, everyday low price. DMart's model is simple to say and hard to copy: keep costs lower than anyone, so prices can be lower than anyone's, every day, without flashy discounts.
  7. Own the shop. Most retailers rent.
  8. Slow, then fast. He grew DMart slowly for years, proving the model in a few regions before expanding.
  9. Frugal at the top. The man who became one of India's richest kept DMart's head office in a corner of an early store and worked without hierarchy.
  10. Listing day, and an honest note. In 2017 DMart's listing showed what patient building can be worth.

The DMart flywheel

Tap each step of the DMart flywheel

  1. Buy direct, pay fast. DMart buys from manufacturers and pays vendors early, often within about a week. Suppliers give better prices to a buyer who pays quickly and in bulk.
  2. Own the stores. It prefers to buy land and build, rather than rent. No rent increases; occupancy costs stay low and predictable.
  3. Low costs everywhere. Simple stores, fast-moving products, a frugal head office. Every rupee saved can be passed on in prices.
  4. Everyday low prices. Low prices every day, not flashy discounts. Shoppers trust the price, so they keep coming back.
  5. More shoppers, more volume. Busy stores sell more per square foot and turn their stock fast. Stock sells before it has to be paid for again.
  6. Bigger buying power. Higher volumes, store clusters and quick payment mean better terms from suppliers. Which brings the wheel back to step one, a little faster each time.

An honest note

SEBI's order of 2 January 2007 concerned three broking firms of the R.S. Damani group: Damani Shares & Stock Brokers, Maheshwari Equity Brokers and Avenue Stock Brokers. SEBI examined their sales of Infosys shares on 23 February and 2 March 2001, and other dealings. It found no evidence of price manipulation, held that alleged links to two other market groups were not established, and found the firms had broadly complied with short-sale rules. The one violation upheld was that the firms had moved securities from their pool accounts to clients' accounts later than the 15-day limit. SEBI treated this as a technical violation with no bad intent, and imposed censure, a minor penalty. Radhakishan Damani appeared at the hearing as a director of one firm; he was not a party to the order. We include it because this library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Words to know

Short selling
Selling borrowed shares, hoping to buy them back cheaper after a fall.
Bear
An investor who expects prices to fall.
EDLC/EDLP
Everyday Low Cost, Everyday Low Price: keeping costs lowest so prices can stay low every day.
Cluster expansion
Opening new stores near existing ones before entering new regions.
IPO
Initial public offering: a company's first sale of shares to the public.
Subscribed 104 times
Investors applied for 104 times as many shares as were on offer.
Censure
A formal reprimand from a regulator; a minor penalty.
Pool account
An account where a broker holds clients' shares before passing them on.
