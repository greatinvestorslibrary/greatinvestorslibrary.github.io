His thinking in five minutes

John Maynard Keynes was the most famous economist of the 20th century. He also ran King's College, Cambridge's investments for 25 years. He failed as a market forecaster in the 1920s, changed his method, and from 1922 to 1946 his fund averaged about 16% a year against 10.4% for the UK market.

  • 16.0% a year: his King's fund's average yearly return, 1922 to 1946
  • 10.4% a year: the UK stock market's average over the same years
  • +8 points: a year ahead on his UK shares (Chambers & Dimson)
  • ½ hour: a day on it, usually in bed each morning

His thinking, one line each

  • He failed first. In the 1920s he tried to forecast the business cycle and time the market. It did not work.
  • Then he changed. From the early 1930s he picked a few undervalued shares and held them. Results improved dramatically.
  • Three principles. In 1938: choose a few cheap investments carefully, hold them steadfastly, and balance the risks.
  • Markets are a beauty contest. Many investors guess what others will think rather than what a business is worth. That creates both danger and opportunity.
  • Bet big where you know. He preferred large holdings in businesses he understood to many small bets.
  • Hold through the falls. His fund fell 20 to 25% twice in nine years. He did not escape, and he held on.

The big ideas

  1. The forecaster who failed. In the 1920s he tried to profit from the ups and downs of the economy.
  2. From forecaster to stock picker. In the early 1930s he stopped forecasting the market and started choosing individual companies.
  3. His three principles. In May 1938 he summed up successful investment in three principles: careful selection, steadfast holding, balance.
  4. A few big bets you understand. He thought spreading money across many things you know little about was a mistake.
  5. Shares over bonds, for the long run. He moved a centuries-old college endowment from land and bonds into shares, decades before other institutions.
  6. The beauty contest. He compared the stock market to a newspaper contest where you win by guessing what others will pick, not what you think is best.
  7. Convention and reputation. Most people assume tomorrow will look like today, and most professionals would rather be wrong with the crowd than right alone.
  8. Animal spirits. Much of what drives business and markets is not calculation but a spontaneous urge to act, which can switch off suddenly.
  9. Holding through the falls. Long-term investing means sitting through sharp falls.
  10. Words he never said. Keynes is the most misquoted investor of all.

How he changed

  • Approach: 1920s, forecast the economy; move in and out of the market; 1930s on, pick a few cheap companies; hold them.
  • Turnover: 1920s, about 55% a year in the 1920s; 1930s on, 30% in the 1930s, 14% in the 1940s.
  • After big falls: 1920s, sold a fifth of UK shares after the 1929 crash; 1930s on, stayed invested through 1937–38.
  • Results: 1920s, disappointing; he struggled; 1930s on, dramatically better.

An honest note

Keynes was a supporter of eugenics throughout his life and director of the Eugenics Society from 1937 to 1944. These views, held by many of his era, are now widely condemned. We include him for his investing, where the record is strong and well documented, and state this plainly so you can judge for yourself.

Words to know

Bursar
The college officer in charge of its money and property.
Endowment
A fund given to an institution, invested so that it can pay for its work for ever.
Discretionary fund
Money the manager may invest freely, without fixed rules on what to buy.
Credit cycle policy
His 1920s attempt to buy shares in slumps and sell them in booms: market timing.
Intrinsic value
What a business is really worth, judged from what it will earn, as opposed to its price.
Turnover
The share of a portfolio bought and sold in a year.
Beauty contest
His picture of a market where people guess what others will think, not what is good.
Animal spirits
The spontaneous urge to act, rather than calculation, that drives much investment.
Shilling
An old British coin worth one twentieth of a pound; 15 shillings was 75p.
Liquidity
How easily something can be sold for cash at a fair price.
