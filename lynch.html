His thinking in five minutes

Peter Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, averaging 29.2% a year, about double the S&P 500. He retired at 46 and spent the following decades teaching ordinary people to pick stocks the way he did: look around, sort, check, explain, then own.

  1. Get ready: Your edge, and whether you have the stomach for it.
  2. Find it: Spot companies in daily life, then sort them into his six kinds.
  3. Check it: Traits, earnings, the famous numbers and the balance sheet.
  4. Tell it: The two-minute drill: if you can't explain it, don't own it.
  5. Own it: Let winners run, ignore forecasts, and keep checking the story.

The big ideas

  1. The amateur's edge. An ordinary person who keeps their eyes open often spots a good company years before the professionals do.
  2. Is it gambling? Stocks and your stomach. Stocks reward patient owners of good companies; the real test is your stomach, not your brain.
  3. Look in your own backyard. The best leads come from everyday life: the store you like, the product people keep buying, the trend at your job.
  4. The six kinds of stock. Before buying, decide which of six kinds of stock you are looking at; that tells you what to expect and when to sell.
  5. The perfect stock, and the ones to avoid. He looked for dull, overlooked companies in boring niches, and stayed away from the hottest story in town.
  6. Earnings are the engine. Over months the price can do anything; over years it follows the company's earnings.
  7. The famous numbers. A few simple numbers tell you whether a good company is also a good price.
  8. Balance sheet first. The biggest losses come from weak balance sheets; know a company's finances before you risk money on it.
  9. The two-minute drill. If you can't explain why you own a stock in two minutes, simply enough for a child, you don't know enough to own it.
  10. Ten-baggers: let the winners run. You only need a few big winners in a lifetime, so let them run and don't cut the flowers to water the weeds.
  11. Don't time the market. Nobody can predict the economy or the next fall; study companies, not forecasts.
  12. Keep checking the story. Hold as long as the story holds; recheck it every few months, and sell when the story, not the price, changes.

His six kinds of stock

  • Slow grower (About as fast as the economy). Expect: Dividends, low risk, low gain. Sell: When the dividend or the business weakens.
  • Stalwart (Earnings up about 10–12% a year). Expect: Moderate gains; protection in recessions. Sell: After a solid rise, often 30–50%.
  • Fast grower (Earnings up about 20–25% a year). Expect: The big multi-baggers, and the biggest risks. Sell: When its room to expand runs out.
  • Cyclical (Profits swing with the economy). Expect: Big moves, if the timing is right. Sell: When the cycle tops out.
  • Turnaround (Battered, might recover). Expect: Big gains when it works; many never do. Sell: Once it has turned around.
  • Asset play (Sits on something overlooked). Expect: Gains when the market notices the asset. Sell: When the asset's value is recognised.

Words to know

Ten-bagger
A stock that rises tenfold. His baseball term: two home runs and a double.
Slow grower
A large, mature company growing about as fast as the economy, owned for its dividend.
Stalwart
A big, established company growing earnings about 10 to 12% a year.
Fast grower
A small, aggressive company growing 20 to 25% a year. His favourite kind.
Cyclical
A company whose profits rise and fall with the economy.
Turnaround
A battered company that might recover.
Asset play
A company sitting on something valuable that the market has missed.
PEG ratio
The P/E divided by the earnings growth rate. Around 1 is fair; well below 1 may be a bargain.
Diworseification
His word for a profitable company buying unrelated businesses and making itself worse.
Two-minute drill
A short spoken case for a stock: why you like it, what must happen, what could go wrong.

Related investors

Also from the United States

  • Warren Buffett. Ran Berkshire Hathaway for sixty years and explained every decision in a yearly letter to its owners.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
  • Benjamin Graham. Taught Buffett, wrote the two founding books of value investing, and turned a near-ruinous 1929 into rules for never being ruined again.
  • Philip Fisher. Ran his own firm for 68 years, found great companies by talking to everyone around them, and held Motorola for nearly half a century.
  • John Templeton. Bought 104 cheap shares as war broke out in 1939, then searched the whole world for bargains at the point of maximum pessimism.

From other countries

  • Zhang Lei. Learned investing at Yale under David Swensen, founded Hillhouse with $20 million, and preaches long-term bets on great entrepreneurs.
  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • Paul Tudor Jones. Lost most of his clients' money on one cotton trade in 1979, built his rules around defence, and made a reported 125.9% after fees in 1987, the year of the crash.
