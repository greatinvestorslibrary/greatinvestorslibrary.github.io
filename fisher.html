His thinking in five minutes

Philip Fisher ran his own investment firm in San Francisco from 1931 until he retired at 91. Where Graham looked for cheap shares, Fisher looked for outstanding companies, found out everything about them by talking to the people around them, and then held on, in Motorola's case for nearly fifty years. Buffett once said he was 15% Fisher.

  • 1955 → 2004: bought Motorola when it made radios; he still owned it when he died
  • 15 points: his checklist for an outstanding company, from his 1958 book
  • 68 years: running his own firm, from 1931 until he retired at 91 in 1999
  • 1958: Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits, one of the first investing books on the New York Times bestseller list

His thinking, one line each

  • Scuttlebutt. Learn about a company from its customers, suppliers, rivals and former staff.
  • The fifteen points. Growth, research, selling, margins, people, and above all integrity.
  • Outstanding people. Business ability and honesty: he would not invest without both.
  • Hold for a very long time. If you bought right, the time to sell is almost never.
  • Own the best, not the most. A few outstanding companies beat a long list of average ones.
  • Think years ahead. What matters is the price against earnings a few years from now.

The big ideas

  1. Scuttlebutt. Find out about a company from the people around it: customers, suppliers, competitors, former employees and scientists.
  2. The fifteen points. He listed fifteen questions an outstanding company should answer well.
  3. Ability and integrity. Two traits in the people who run a company are essential: business ability, and honesty.
  4. Growth that lasts. Look for companies that can keep growing for years, because they keep developing new products.
  5. Conservative investors sleep well. A conservative investment keeps your purchasing power with the least risk.
  6. When to sell: almost never. If you bought the right company, the time to sell is almost never.
  7. Own the best, not the most. Over-diversifying into companies you don't know well is riskier than owning a few you know thoroughly.
  8. Don't follow the crowd. Prices swing with fashion.
  9. Dividends matter less. He thought investors made too much fuss about dividends.
  10. Lessons from his own mistakes. He saw the 1929 crash coming and still lost money.

The fifteen points

Tick the points a company passes

  1. Its market can lift sales sizably for years
  2. Management is set on finding the next products
  3. Its research is effective for its size
  4. Its sales organisation is better than average
  5. It earns a worthwhile profit margin
  6. It has a plan to keep or improve that margin
  7. Relations with its staff are outstanding
  8. Its executives work well together
  9. It has depth of management, not one star
  10. It controls and understands its costs
  11. It is strong in the ways that matter in its industry
  12. It thinks long term about profits
  13. Growth won't need so many new shares that owners gain little
  14. Managers speak as openly about bad news as good
  15. Management has unquestionable integrity

Our short paraphrase of his fifteen points, as a study aid; not a recommendation.

His three reasons to sell

  1. A mistake: the company was never what you thought.
  2. A change: it no longer passes the points as it did.
  3. A much better use: a clearly superior company appears. This should be rare.

His ten don'ts, in brief

  1. Don't buy promotional companies with no record yet.
  2. Don't ignore a good share just because it trades off the main exchange.
  3. Don't buy for the tone of the annual report.
  4. Don't assume a high P/E means the growth is already priced in.
  5. Don't haggle over tiny sums on an outstanding company.
  6. Don't overstress diversification.
  7. Don't fear buying on a war scare.
  8. Don't be swayed by irrelevant past prices and earnings.
  9. Don't forget time as well as price for a true growth share.
  10. Don't follow the crowd.

  • Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits. His main book: the scuttlebutt method, the fifteen points, when to buy and sell, dividends, and ten don'ts for investors.
  • Paths to Wealth through Common Stocks. How big changes in technology and society affect whole groups of companies, and how inflation shapes investing.
  • Conservative Investors Sleep Well. What makes an investment conservative: low costs and strong skills, good people, a business that earns above average, and a sensible price.
  • Developing an Investment Philosophy. His own story: Stanford, a dishonest first job, the 1929 crash, starting his firm, and the lessons behind each part of his method.
  • Philip A. Fisher, 96, is dead; wrote key investment book. His life and family, Motorola and Texas Instruments, and what Buffett said he learned from him.
  • How Omaha beats Wall Street. The young Buffett explains his approach, and how much of it comes from Graham and how much from Fisher.

Words to know

Scuttlebutt
Information gathered informally from people who deal with a company.
Growth stock
A share in a company expected to grow sales and profits much faster than average.
Profit margin
Profit as a share of sales.
Research and development
Spending on new products and processes.
Dilution
When a company issues new shares, each existing share owns less of it.
Diversification
Spreading money across many holdings to reduce the harm of any one mistake.
Low-cost producer
A company that can make its product more cheaply than its rivals.
Dividend
Part of a company's profit paid out to shareholders.

Related investors

Also from the United States

  • Warren Buffett. Ran Berkshire Hathaway for sixty years and explained every decision in a yearly letter to its owners.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
  • Peter Lynch. Ran the Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, beat the market by a mile, and showed ordinary people how to find good stocks.
  • Benjamin Graham. Taught Buffett, wrote the two founding books of value investing, and turned a near-ruinous 1929 into rules for never being ruined again.
  • John Templeton. Bought 104 cheap shares as war broke out in 1939, then searched the whole world for bargains at the point of maximum pessimism.

From other countries

  • Atsuto Sawakami. Founded Japan's first independent fund company in 1999 and preached long-term investing for decades, when few in Japan would listen.
  • Cheah Cheng Hye. Sold pineapples as a boy in Penang, spent 17 years as a journalist, then built Value Partners from under US$5 million: Asia's Warren Buffett.
  • Duan Yongping. Built the companies behind OPPO and vivo, then turned investor: buy businesses you understand, never borrow, and keep a list of things you won't do.
