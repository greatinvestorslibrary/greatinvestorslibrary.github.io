His thinking in five minutes

Benjamin Graham taught Warren Buffett, wrote Security Analysis (1934) and The Intelligent Investor (1949), and is called the father of value investing. The 1929 crash nearly ruined him, and he spent the rest of his life working out how an ordinary person could invest without being ruined. His answers: know the difference between investing and speculating, treat the market as a moody partner, and always insist on a margin of safety.

  • −70%: his account, 1929 to 1932, against −74% for the Dow: the crash nearly wiped him out
  • 17.4% a year: Graham-Newman, 1945 to 1956, leaving out GEICO; the S&P 500 did 18.3%, but with far bigger swings
  • ≈ $0.7m: paid in 1948 for half of GEICO ($712,500 to $720,000, by source); it outgrew everything else he owned
  • 1934: Security Analysis, with David Dodd; The Intelligent Investor followed in 1949

His thinking, one line each

  • Investing, not speculating. An investment protects your money and earns a fair return. Anything else is speculation.
  • Mr Market. A moody partner offers you prices every day. Use them; never obey them.
  • Margin of safety. Pay well below what something is worth, so mistakes and bad luck don't ruin you.
  • Know which investor you are. Defensive investors keep it simple; enterprising ones do more work.
  • Simple rules, many stocks. Buy groups of cheap stocks by plain rules, and sell by rules too.
  • Value wins in the end. In the short run prices follow the crowd; in time they follow value.

The big ideas

  1. Investing or speculating?. An investment, after careful study, protects your money and promises a fair return.
  2. Mr Market. Picture a partner who offers every day to buy your share or sell you his, at a price that depends on his mood.
  3. Margin of safety. Pay well below what something is worth.
  4. Know which investor you are. He split investors in two.
  5. Seven tests for the defensive investor. For investors who want safety, he set seven plain tests: size, strong finances, steady earnings, dividends, growth, and moderate prices against earnings and assets.
  6. Buying below working capital. His favourite bargain: shares selling for less than the company's current assets minus all its debts, giving nothing at all for factories or brands.
  7. Simple rules, many stocks. Late in life he decided that detailed analysis rarely paid any more.
  8. Stocks and bonds: the 25–75 rule. Keep at least a quarter of your money in shares and at least a quarter in bonds, always.
  9. The market catches up with value. Prices can stray far from value for a long time.
  10. Loss, luck and generosity. The crash taught him caution, GEICO taught him how much luck matters, and his friends remembered his generosity.

Seven tests and a price check

Try his seven tests on a company

  1. Adequate size. A large, established company. His 1973 line: at least $100 million of yearly sales for an industrial firm.
  2. Strong finances: current ratio. Current assets ÷ current liabilities. Passes at 2 or more.
  3. Strong finances: debt below working capital. Debt due after a year is no larger than current assets minus current liabilities.
  4. A profit every year for 10 years. No loss in any of the past ten years.
  5. Dividends for 20 years. Paid without a break for at least twenty years.
  6. Earnings up a third in 10 years. Compare three-year averages at the start and the end.
  7. P/E on 3-year average earnings. Passes at 15 or below.
  8. Price to book value. Passes at 1.5 or below, or if P/E × P/B is 22.5 or less.

His 1973 tests for defensive investors in US industrial companies, as a study aid. Numbers are for learning, not a recommendation; he expected few companies to pass.

A quick price check

Tests 6 and 7 together cap the price at the square root of 22.5 × earnings per share × book value per share. People now call this the Graham number. Enter a company's figures to see it.

Use average earnings over three years, as he did. A loss-making company has no Graham number.

Graham called himself a defensive investor's teacher first. These tests protect; they do not promise big gains.

Words to know

Speculation
Buying in the hope that the price will rise, without a margin of safety in value.
Margin of safety
The gap between a cautious estimate of value and the price you pay.
Mr Market
Graham's picture of the market as a moody partner offering prices you may take or ignore.
Current ratio
Current assets divided by current liabilities.
Working capital
Current assets minus current liabilities.
Net current asset value
Current assets minus all liabilities, with nothing for factories or brands.
Book value
A company's assets minus its liabilities, as shown in its accounts.
Price to earnings (P/E)
The share price divided by the profit per share.
Rebalancing
Selling some of what has risen and buying what has fallen, to return to a set mix.

Related investors

Also from the United States

  • Warren Buffett. Ran Berkshire Hathaway for sixty years and explained every decision in a yearly letter to its owners.
  • Charlie Munger. Buffett's partner for over forty years, and the clearest thinker on why smart people make bad decisions.
  • Peter Lynch. Ran the Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990, beat the market by a mile, and showed ordinary people how to find good stocks.
  • Philip Fisher. Ran his own firm for 68 years, found great companies by talking to everyone around them, and held Motorola for nearly half a century.
  • John Templeton. Bought 104 cheap shares as war broke out in 1939, then searched the whole world for bargains at the point of maximum pessimism.
  • The Timeline: Buffett, Munger and Lynch side by side

From other countries

  • Parag Parikh. Started as a sub-broker in 1979, became India's voice of behavioural finance, and built a fund with one scheme and his own money in it.
  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Anthony Bolton. Ran Fidelity Special Situations for 28 years, about 19.5% a year, by buying good companies the crowd had given up on.
