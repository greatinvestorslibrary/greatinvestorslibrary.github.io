Wahei Takeda

His thinking in five minutes

Wahei Takeda built Takeda Seika into Japan's biggest maker of tamago boro, the little egg biscuits Japanese babies grow up on. From 1956 he invested the company's spare cash, and after a painful lesson moved to small and mid-sized companies, becoming a large shareholder in more than a hundred of them. Japan called him its Warren Buffett. He called being a shareholder being the company's danna, its patron.

  • 1956: the year he began investing the company's spare cash, at 23
  • 100+: listed companies in which he became a large individual shareholder
  • P/E ≈ 10: the price he liked to pay: about ten times earnings
  • Gold: medals he sent companies to thank them for their dividends

His thinking, one line each

  • Investing, not speculating. Owning a company to support it is not the same as betting on its price.
  • Be the patron. A shareholder is the company's danna: patient, encouraging, loyal.
  • Fields and harvests. Watch the yearly harvest of dividends, not the price of the land.
  • Cheap, sound, generous. Low price, a strong balance sheet, and dividends paid out.
  • Calm in storms. Use spare money only, and let prices go up or down.
  • Sincerity. A chain of sincerity brings success and happiness.

The big ideas

  1. The biscuit maker. He was a confectioner first.
  2. From speculator to investor. He began by trading famous shares for quick profit.
  3. The lesson of a collapse. In the 1990s a large company in which he was the top individual shareholder went bankrupt, its managers insisting all was well to the end.
  4. Be the danna. In old Japan a danna was a patron who stood behind a business or an artist.
  5. Fields and harvests. He saw investing as farming.
  6. Cheap, sound and generous. He wanted shares that were cheap against earnings, companies with strong balance sheets, and managers who shared profits with shareholders.
  7. Dividends first. Dividends, not price gains, were the core of his returns.
  8. Calm in storms. He used only spare money, never borrowed to the limit, and refused to panic.
  9. When to sell. He held for the long run, but sold when a share looked overvalued, and lost patience with companies that hoarded cash instead of rewarding owners.
  10. Sincerity, and giving back. He believed sincerity passed from person to person brings success and happiness, ran a circle to develop young people, and gave back much of what he gained.

Would he be its danna?

Would he be this company's danna?

  1. The share is cheap: around ten times earnings, or less
  2. It pays a healthy share of its profit as dividends
  3. Its equity ratio is strong: little reliance on debt
  4. You can see the manager's face, and you know who runs it
  5. Its accounts are simple enough to read yourself
  6. Its success would make you genuinely glad
  7. It does not pile up cash it cannot use
  8. You are buying with spare money, not borrowed money

The farmer's year

  1. Sow in companies likely to bloom, when the season is right.
  2. Tend as a patron: encourage, don't harass.
  3. Harvest the dividends, year after year.
  4. Replant the harvest, and let it grow.

Words to know

Danna (旦那)
A patron who supports a business or an artist; also a master of a household.
Tamago boro
Small egg biscuits that melt in the mouth, a classic Japanese baby snack.
Shikiho (会社四季報)
Japan's quarterly handbook of listed companies, a staple for individual investors.
Payout ratio
The share of after-tax profit a company pays out as dividends.
Equity ratio
Shareholders' equity as a share of total assets; higher means sturdier.
P/E
Price to earnings: the share price divided by yearly earnings per share.
Retained earnings
Profits kept in the company rather than paid out (naibu ryūho).
Margin trading
Buying shares with borrowed money.
