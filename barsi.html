His thinking in five minutes

Luiz Barsi Filho lost his father at one and grew up in a São Paulo tenement, shining shoes and selling sweets. At 14 he found work at a stockbroker. Worried that Brazil's state pension would fail him, he began in the early 1970s to buy shares in one durable company a month and reinvest every dividend. Half a century later he is Brazil's biggest individual investor, said to receive more than a million reais a day in dividends, and he still rides the metro.

  • 14: his age when he started work at a stockbroker, after years shining shoes
  • ~10 years: until his dividends passed what a state pension would pay him
  • R$0.50: his cost for Santander Brasil preferred shares later worth over R$25
  • R$1m+: a day in dividends, as reported by Fast Company Brasil, from a portfolio near US$2 billion

His thinking, one line each

  • A private pension. Build your own retirement out of dividend-paying shares.
  • Durable sectors. Banks, energy, sanitation, insurance, telecoms: things people always need.
  • Buy monthly. Add a little every month, from salary first, then from dividends.
  • Reinvest. Plough dividends back into more shares while building.
  • Never sell in need. Only invest money you will not need.
  • Avoid what can blow up. No leverage, options or futures.

The big ideas

  1. A tenement childhood. He lost his father at one, grew up with his mother in a São Paulo tenement, and worked from about seven.
  2. A stockbroker at fourteen. At 14 he got a job at a stockbroker and fell for company accounts.
  3. Shares guarantee the future. Before 30 he feared Brazil's state pension would fail him.
  4. One thousand shares a month. He chose São Paulo's power company and bought about a thousand shares every month until he reached his target.
  5. The pension portfolio. His method: own durable companies that pay good dividends, bought cheaply, held for decades.
  6. Banks, energy, sanitation, insurance, telecoms. His followers sum up where he starts looking with one word: BESST, the sectors whose services people always need.
  7. What he never does. He never invests money he may need, never sells because he has to, and avoids leverage, options and futures.
  8. Patience pays. His best results came from holding for decades: shares bought for cents grew into fortunes and paid him every year.
  9. Mistakes and a clean verdict. He lost money in banks that failed in the 1990s.
  10. Rich, but simple. He still lives simply, rides the metro on a free senior pass, and asks what wealth means.

His pension portfolio

Would it fit his pension portfolio?

  1. It is in a durable, essential sector: banks, energy, sanitation, insurance or telecoms
  2. It has paid dividends regularly for years
  3. Its products will still be needed in decades
  4. It is well governed and well managed
  5. Its finances are sound and it makes a profit
  6. The price is reasonable, ideally near or below book value
  7. You are buying with money you will not need

An honest note

Brazil's securities regulator, the CVM, brought a case alleging that Luiz Barsi Filho traded on inside information when he bought Unipar shares worth about R$1.1 million on 28 May 2021, while he sat on the company's board, days before it announced a deal. On 26 March 2024 the CVM's board acquitted him unanimously. His defence was that the purchase was made to collect dividends, in line with his long-standing strategy. He has also lost money on banks that failed in the 1990s. We include this because the library puts integrity first and tells readers what is on the public record.

Words to know

Dividend
A share of a company's profit paid to its shareholders.
Dividend yield
Yearly dividends as a percentage of the share price.
Book value
Assets minus liabilities, as shown in the accounts.
Preferred shares (PN)
In Brazil, shares with priority on dividends but usually no vote.
INSS
Brazil's state social security and pension system.
Renda fixa
Fixed income: bonds and deposits. He calls it perda fixa, fixed loss.
CVM
Comissão de Valores Mobiliários, Brazil's securities regulator.

Also from the wider world

  • Prem Watsa. Reached Canada from Hyderabad with almost nothing, turned a failing insurer into Fairfax, and grew its book value 18.7% a year for forty years.
  • Francisco García Paramés. Taught himself value investing from Peter Lynch and Buffett's letters, then beat Spain's market for 21 years, survived his worst years, and kept going.
  • André Kostolany. A speculator, not a value investor: rich twice, broke in between, and the man who taught Germany how crowds move markets.
  • Peter Cundill. Read Graham on a plane in 1973, then hunted the world for shares below their liquidation value: the Indiana Jones of Canadian money managers.
  • Allan Gray. Rose 10% at Fidelity while the 1962 market crashed, founded South Africa's biggest private fund firm and Orbis, then gave his control to charity.
