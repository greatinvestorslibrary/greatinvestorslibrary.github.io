His thinking in five minutes

Anthony Bolton ran Fidelity Special Situations from December 1979 to December 2007, about 19.5% a year, well ahead of the UK market. His method: buy good companies the crowd has given up on, write down why you own each one, meet the managers, and sell when the reason breaks.

  • 19.5% a year: Fidelity Special Situations, Dec 1979 to Dec 2007
  • £1,000 → £147,000: over his 28 years, dividends reinvested
  • about 13.5% a year: the UK stock market over the same 28 years
  • £6bn: the fund's size when it was split in two, 2006

His thinking, one line each

  • Go against the crowd, with a reason. He bought what others disliked, but only once he understood why the gloom was overdone.
  • Write down why you own it. Every holding had a short thesis, retested often, and a counter-thesis: what would make it a bad investment.
  • Know what is already in the price. He looked for a gap between price and value using several measures, never one favourite ratio.
  • Meet the people. He met two or three companies a day and kept handwritten notes of every meeting.
  • Know yourself. Temperament matters more than IQ. Find a style that suits you and stick with it.
  • Sell when the reason breaks. Even at a loss. Never hold on just to get back to even.

The big ideas

  1. The contrarian's edge. The best bargains are usually in companies the crowd has given up on.
  2. Write down why you own it. Every share you own needs a reason you can write in a few lines, and a written case against it.
  3. What is already in the price. Look for a gap between price and value, measured in several ways, then give it time to close.
  4. Meet the managers. He learnt most by meeting the people who run companies, again and again, and writing everything down.
  5. What makes a good company. Start with the quality of the business, then ask whether it will be worth more in ten years.
  6. Where the analysts are not. Medium and smaller companies, and those analysts have given up on, are where mispricing lives.
  7. Charts as a second opinion. Research comes first.
  8. When to sell. Sell when the reason for owning breaks, when the price reaches your target, or when you find something better.
  9. Know yourself. Temperament matters more than intelligence.
  10. Even legends stumble. His China fund went badly at first.

Before you buy: his questions

  1. The business franchise is strong
  2. It will still be here in ten years, and be worth more
  3. It is in control of its own destiny
  4. The managers know the detail, and are candid with owners
  5. I can write my reason for owning it in three lines
  6. I have written the case against it, and why I disagree
  7. It looks cheap on more than one measure, against its own history and its rivals
  8. The crowd is gloomy, and I can say why the gloom is overdone
  9. The chart does not contradict my research

His three reasons to sell

  1. Something breaks the reason you bought it.
  2. The share reaches the value you worked out.
  3. You find a clearly better idea for the money.

Words to know

Contrarian
An investor who deliberately goes against the crowd: buying what is unloved and selling what is loved.
Investment thesis
The short written reason for owning a share.
Counter-thesis
The written case against your own idea: what would make it a bad investment.
Recovery share
A company in trouble whose shares are priced as if it will never recover.
Company value to cash profits (EV/EBITDA)
The value of a whole company, shares plus debt minus cash, divided by profits before interest, tax and depreciation.
Free cash flow yield
Cash a company has left after investing, as a percentage of its value.
Technical analysis
Studying price charts and trading patterns, rather than the business itself.
Special situations
Companies going through change: recovery, takeover, restructuring or neglect.
Gearing
Borrowing money to invest more than you have. It magnifies both gains and losses.
