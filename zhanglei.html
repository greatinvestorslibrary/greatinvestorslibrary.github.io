His thinking in five minutes

Zhang Lei learned investing as an intern under David Swensen at Yale's endowment, then founded Hillhouse in 2005 with $20 million from Yale. He backed Tencent early, put $300 million into JD.com in 2010, and built one of Asia's largest investment firms. His creed: think long term, research deeply, back entrepreneurs with great vision, and be a friend of time.

  • $20m: Yale's seed money when he founded Hillhouse in 2005
  • $2.4bn: gains Hillhouse had made for Yale by 2020, according to David Swensen
  • $300m: invested in JD.com in 2010, in one go
  • ¥41.66bn: paid in 2019 for 15% of Gree Electric; the shares were still below that price in early 2026

His thinking, one line each

  • Long-termism. Put time and conviction into things that create value over many years.
  • Research is the base. Understand real businesses like a field researcher, not only from accounts.
  • Back people with great vision. Long-term, deep insight, focused execution, strong empathy.
  • Moats must keep moving. A lasting edge comes from a company that keeps innovating.
  • Create value, don't just find it. Help good companies get better, with technology and patience.
  • Be a friend of time. Choose things that grow more valuable as time passes.

The big ideas

  1. Long-termism. Put your time and conviction into things that create value over many years.
  2. Swensen's lessons. David Swensen was his first teacher.
  3. Research is the base. Deep research comes first, and few bets.
  4. People first. Hillhouse's growth came mainly from people: the managers it hires and the entrepreneurs it backs.
  5. Backing great vision. He looks for entrepreneurs with great vision, which he defines in four parts.
  6. Moats must keep moving. Static moats erode.
  7. Create value, don't just find it. Hillhouse tries to help good companies get better, rather than planning the exit first.
  8. Fire cannot burn it. As a student he decided that what fire can burn, money and houses, matters least.
  9. Patience with big bets. Long-term bets can stay underwater for years.
  10. The LONGi investigation. In 2023 China's securities regulator investigated a Hillhouse unit over undisclosed share sales.

Long-term, and an honest note

Four signs of an entrepreneur with great vision

  1. Long-termism: seeking the long run amid uncertainty.
  2. Deep insight: into the industry, spotting key trends through constant innovation.
  3. Focused execution: doing things to the highest standard.
  4. Strong empathy: bringing people and resources together to make ideas real.

From his book Value (2020), in our words.

What he looks for in people

  1. Curiosity.
  2. Independence of mind.
  3. Intellectual honesty.
  4. Empathy.
  5. Long-term and aligned with customers, for the owners and managers he backs.

From notes of his 2015 Columbia lecture and CFA Institute's report of his 2018 talk.

An honest note

In November 2023 China's securities regulator opened an investigation into HHLR, the public-markets unit of Hillhouse. HHLR owned 5.85% of the solar company LONGi. In March 2023 it lent out shares, taking its stake below 5%, the level above which sales must be announced; when the loan ended its stake was only 4.98%, implying it had sold about 0.9% of the company without an announcement. In April 2024 HHLR began buying the shares back and promised to hand any gain to LONGi. The case concerned the rules on announcing share sales; we have seen no allegation that clients' money was misused, and we have found no published final penalty decision. We include Zhang Lei for his long record and ideas, and state this plainly so you can judge for yourself.

Words to know

Endowment
A fund given to a university or charity and invested so it can pay for its work for ever.
Fiduciary
Someone trusted to act in another person's interest, before their own.
Private equity
Buying stakes in companies, often unlisted, usually with a view to improving them.
Venture capital
Investing in young companies in return for a stake.
Take-private
Buying all the shares of a listed company so it leaves the stock market.
Dynamic moat
A competitive edge that a company keeps renewing through innovation, rather than a fixed advantage.
First principles
Reasoning from what a thing fundamentally is, rather than by comparison with something else.
Disclosure threshold
The shareholding, 5% in China, above which an investor must announce changes in its stake.
Securities lending
Lending shares to another investor for a fee, to be returned later.
